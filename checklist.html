Age-by-Age Care Checklist

Every recommended visit, test, referral, and home precaution for achondroplasia — from the newborn nursery to older adulthood — on one page you can bring to appointments.

Achondroplasia comes with a well-mapped monitoring schedule. That is genuinely good news: almost every serious complication of this condition can be found and treated before it causes harm, if the right check happens at the right age. This page merges the three most important schedules — the American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report (AAP, 2020), the International Consensus Statement written by 55 experts from 16 countries (2021), and the practical allied-health cadence from the Children's Hospital at Westmead (2010) — plus the European Achondroplasia Forum's focused guidance on the skull base and on sleep, and the GeneReviews surveillance tables. Where the guidelines differ, we say so and name the source, because your doctors may follow any of them.

How to use this page

Print it or keep it on your phone, and bring it to appointments — a 2025 practice-gap study found that real-world care for infants and young children with achondroplasia often falls short of the published guidelines, so families frequently need to ask for these checks by name. Two caveats: local practice varies between countries and centers, and your own skeletal dysplasia clinic's schedule always wins over anything written here. This is a map, not a prescription. Each item links to the life-stage page that explains why it matters.

Before the checklist: know the red flags

No schedule replaces day-to-day watchfulness. In an infant: breathing pauses, blue or gray lips, sudden floppiness, choking or gagging with feeds, or loss of previously gained skills — call emergency services. In an adult: new bladder or bowel changes with back or leg symptoms — that is an emergency too. The full list, by age, is on the Warning Signs page.

Birth & the newborn month

The busiest month of the schedule. Everything here has one goal: confirm the diagnosis, check the opening at the base of the skull (the foramen magnum), and set up the team. The First Years page explains each item in depth.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Refer immediately to a skeletal dysplasia center or a clinician experienced with achondroplasia (Consensus Rec. 24) — this team coordinates everything else. A directory of centers is on the Medical Library page.
  • Establish care with a pediatric orthopedist (AAP).
  • Genetic counseling for the family: about 80% of babies are born to average-height parents via a brand-new gene change; recurrence risk for those parents is only ~1% in a future pregnancy. See Understanding It.
  • Connect with family support (e.g., Little People of America) — introducing parents to advocacy groups is proven beneficial (Consensus Rec. 3). See Wellbeing & Community.
  • Urgent referral to a pediatric neurosurgeon if any trigger below appears on exam, sleep study, or imaging.

Tests & imaging

  • Confirm the diagnosis: physical exam plus skeletal X-rays, with FGFR3 gene testing to confirm (98–99% of cases carry the same variant). A full skeletal survey is not routinely needed (Consensus Rec. 4).
  • Overnight sleep study (polysomnography) — AAP: in the newborn period for every infant, including end-tidal CO2, read by a pediatric pulmonologist, looking specifically for central apnea. (Consensus timing: within the first year at the latest — see the 0–6 month band.)
  • Neuroimaging of the craniocervical junction for every baby — MRI is the preferred modality (Consensus Rec. 35), often possible without sedation using a "feed-and-wrap" technique; the Consensus recommends including whole-brain imaging (Rec. 37) to give a baseline for the ventricles. CT with foramen-magnum measurements is an alternative where MRI isn't available.
  • Careful neurologic exam: feeding and any choking/gagging, breathing pauses in sleep, blueness of the lips, muscle tone, symmetry of movements and reflexes.
  • Plot length, weight, and head circumference on achondroplasia-specific growth charts — never standard charts, which will always look "abnormal" and cause false alarms.
  • Confirm the newborn hearing screen; formal follow-up of any failed screen.

Refer to neurosurgery promptly if…

  • The neuro exam is abnormal: marked floppiness, weakness, sustained ankle clonus (rhythmic foot beating), or asymmetric reflexes; choking or gagging with feeds.
  • The sleep study shows oxygen saturations below 85% or central apnea beyond what is expected in a newborn.
  • Imaging shows a markedly small foramen magnum, deformation or signal change of the cord, or absent fluid (CSF) space around the cord.

Lifestyle & prevention (teach every caregiver)

  • Always support the head and neck when lifting and carrying — no "head bobbling". Never lift the baby by the arms.
  • Rear-facing car seat with a firm back; installation checked before discharge. Babies must never sleep unattended in a car seat or any sitting device.
  • No carrying slings or pouches, no mechanical swings — they allow uncontrolled head movement and curl the spine.
  • Back to sleep on a firm flat surface; feed with the back straight and head/neck aligned. Full detail on the First Years and Everyday Life pages.

Discuss with your team

  • The warning signs to watch for at home (printable list here).
  • Ask for a copy of the achondroplasia growth charts to keep yourself (Consensus Rec. 26 — parents should hold their own parameters register; see Build your binder below).
  • Whether your center starts growth-modifying treatment discussions this early — vosoritide is approved from birth (open growth plates) in the US, and the 2025 implementation consensus supports starting as early as possible after genetic confirmation, but this is a family decision made with your specialist team, not a checklist item.

0–6 months

Visits every 2–4 months (Consensus) or every 3–4 months (EAF); Westmead reviewed every 4 months in the first year. The single theme of this band: protect the neck and spine while the baby grows into them.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Skeletal dysplasia / multidisciplinary team review every 2–4 months, with the cervicomedullary compression check at every single medical visit (Consensus Rec. 29) — this check is a history and hands-on exam, not a scan.
  • Physiotherapy/occupational therapy involvement for positioning and handling coaching (Westmead model).
  • Prompt ENT referral if ear infections begin early.

Tests & imaging

  • Craniocervical MRI by 3–6 months if not already done — the European Achondroplasia Forum recommends one routine screening MRI at 3–6 months of age for all infants, repeated only as findings dictate. (The AAP includes neuroimaging in the universal newborn assessment; the Consensus says "during the first months of life, even in asymptomatic infants." All three agree: every baby gets imaged once, early.)
  • Head circumference monthly on achondroplasia-specific charts (Consensus Rec. 45); length and weight at every visit. A big head is expected — the alarm sign is crossing upward through achondroplasia percentiles.
  • Sleep study now if it did not happen in the newborn period.
  • Vaccinations on the normal national schedule (Consensus Rec. 33); ask about RSV prophylaxis with monoclonal antibodies (Rec. 105) — small chests make respiratory infections riskier.

What parents can watch at home

  • Breathing during sleep: long pauses, gasping, struggling or rib-sucking effort, blueness — report the same day.
  • Feeding: choking, gagging, or repeated coughing with feeds is never "just messy eating" in achondroplasia — it is a red flag for pressure on the brainstem.
  • Poor weight gain despite feeding well; a bulging, tense, or hard soft spot (fontanelle); marked new floppiness.
  • Reflux (spitting up) is common; mention it — persistent reflux can aggravate airway problems.

Development & therapy

  • Expect the baby to lift the head in prone (tummy time) by about 5 months (Westmead). Motor milestones run later than average-height timelines and that is normal for achondroplasia — insist on achondroplasia-specific milestone charts, never standard ones.
  • Daily tummy time builds the back and neck strength that everything else depends on.
  • Supported reclined sitting (firm-backed infant seat, reclined about 30 degrees) can start around 2–3 months, limited to 10–20 minutes while awake and alert (Westmead).
  • Social smiling (1–3 months) and cognitive engagement should be on time — social or cognitive delay is not expected and should always be investigated.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Keep all newborn handling rules: head/neck support, no lifting by the arms, no pull-to-sit with an unsupported head, avoid neck hyperextension in prone.
  • Not recommended at this age (Westmead/AAP): baby bouncers, rockers, jumpers, walkers, swings, slings, and soft "C-position" seats. Car seats, capsules, and strollers should have a firm back, reclined as much as possible at first.
  • No propped or unsupported sitting — wait until the baby's own trunk can hold an upright spine. This is how the infant "hump" (thoracolumbar kyphosis) is prevented; see First Years.

Discuss with your team

  • MRI logistics (feed-and-wrap vs. sedation) and how results will be shared.
  • Vosoritide timing if your family is considering it, and any open clinical trials.
  • Aquatic physiotherapy from around 6 months (depending on head control) — a Westmead favorite for building strength safely.

6–12 months

The deadline band: two universal tests must be completed by the first birthday if they haven't been already.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Team review every 2–4 months continues; cervicomedullary check at every visit.
  • ENT referral at the first sign of recurrent ear infections — short, horizontal eustachian tubes make middle-ear fluid nearly the rule; over half of children eventually need ear tubes (grommets).
  • Discuss early-intervention services and, in the US, Supplemental Security Income (SSI) eligibility (AAP).

Tests & imaging

  • Formal behavioral audiometry by 9–12 months (AAP), then at least annually. Untreated ear fluid muffles hearing during the exact window speech is learned.
  • Sleep study completed by the first birthday at the very latest for every infant (Consensus Rec. 30) — about one in five children with abnormal sleep studies has no symptoms, which is why history alone is not enough.
  • Head circumference still monthly; length/weight on achondroplasia charts each visit.
  • If ear tubes are planned: the surgeon should check for a high jugular bulb (a large vein that can lack its bony roof in achondroplasia) before operating (Consensus Rec. 111).

What parents can watch at home

  • Snoring with pauses or gasps, sweating with labored breathing at night (sweating alone is common and normal in achondroplasia).
  • Early hand preference (before 12 months) — can signal one-sided weakness; asymmetric movements or reflexes.
  • Head size jumping percentile lines on the achondroplasia chart; repeated vomiting with unusual sleepiness or irritability.
  • Loss of any previously gained skill — always urgent.

Development & therapy

  • Typical windows (Westmead norms): rolls by 7 months; reaches for objects by 6 months; transfers hand-to-hand by 7 months; bangs two objects together by 9 months; babbles at 6–12 months; first specific words at 8–15 months.
  • Unusual movement styles are normal variants, not problems: commando crawling, "snowplowing" (head-down pushing along the floor), bear-walking, bottom-scooting. Classic hands-and-knees crawling is uncommon.
  • PT/OT with skeletal-dysplasia experience helps — the rule is support, don't force: never place the baby in positions (propped sitting, standing) they can't get into themselves.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Positioning rules continue all year: no unsupported sitting, no umbrella strollers or soft canvas seats, firm-backed everything, rear-facing car seat.
  • Keep floor play — especially prone — the main event; use small, light toys that suit small hands.

1–2 years

The year of standing and (usually) first steps — on achondroplasia's own timetable. Visit frequency relaxes slightly to every 3–6 months.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Team review every 3–6 months (Consensus/EAF); Westmead reviewed every 6 months from age 1 to 3.
  • Pediatric orthopedist follows the spine: the infant kyphosis ("hump") should straighten once walking starts. If it has not resolved within a year of walking, or the curve measures 20–40 degrees and is progressing, refer to a pediatric orthopedic spine surgeon (Consensus Rec. 60).
  • Repeat hearing test at 1 year for all children (Westmead), then annual audiology.
  • Book a formal speech and language evaluation no later than age 2 (AAP).

Tests & imaging

  • Growth and head circumference on achondroplasia charts every visit; neurologic exam every visit.
  • Repeat sleep study or MRI only as symptoms or prior findings dictate — obstructive breathing sometimes worsens in the second year.
  • Sitting/standing spine X-rays if the kyphosis looks significant or is stiffening.
  • If the diagnosis was made late (after age 1): still do a sleep study for all, plus imaging guided by signs (AAP).

What parents can watch at home

  • Everything from 6–12 months still applies (breathing, reflexes, head growth, regression).
  • New snoring or restless, gasping sleep as tonsils and adenoids grow.
  • The outward-turned legs and feet of infancy: normal, no bracing needed, self-correcting within about 6 months of standing.

Development & therapy

  • Typical windows (Westmead): pull to stand by 16 months; get into sitting independently by 17 months; walk with hands held by 19 months; stand alone by 20 months; walk independently by about 23 months. Cohort data run to ~16–30 months for first independent steps, with nearly all children walking by 36 months — not walking by 2.5–3 years deserves specialist review.
  • Language: understands peek-a-boo by 8–14 months; identifies body parts by 13–24 months; two-word phrases at 20–31 months (Westmead) — language is typically on time or mildly delayed. About 13% of toddlers with achondroplasia are "late talkers"; ear fluid is the usual culprit, which is why the hearing schedule matters.
  • Fine motor: scribbles with a crayon and builds a 2-block tower by ~18 months.
  • Standardized developmental tests built for average-height children should not be used (Westmead) — ask for achondroplasia-specific charts at every assessment.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Trips and falls are common with new walking — keep the child active with sensible precautions rather than restriction (Consensus Rec. 59).
  • Stay rear-facing in the car as long as the seat allows; firm-backed seating continues.
  • Healthy eating habits start now — from age 2 onward, excess weight becomes the main modifiable risk in achondroplasia.

Discuss with your team

  • Whether any repeat MRI or sleep study is planned, and what would trigger one.
  • Treatment options: vosoritide (approved from birth with open growth plates in the US) and navepegritide (approved from age 2 in the US, Feb 2026). These are decisions to weigh carefully with your specialist team — this page is educational, not medical advice.
  • Childcare/daycare preparation: a visit plus written recommendations from your therapy team smooths the start (Westmead). See Everyday Life.

2–5 years

Monitoring settles into a rhythm: yearly hearing, yearly reviews (Westmead moved to annual reviews from age 3 until school), growth and weight at every visit, and a sharp eye on sleep and legs. The Childhood page covers the why.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Reviews every 3–6 months to age 3 (EAF), then at least annually; growth, BMI, neurologic exam, and development on achondroplasia charts at each visit (AAP).
  • Hearing test every year; ENT follow-up for recurrent infections; tubes recommended when middle-ear fluid persists 3 months or more with documented hearing loss (Consensus Rec. 109).
  • Speech therapy referral for any speech/language delay (the evaluation itself should have happened by age 2).
  • Refer to a pediatric orthopedist if leg bowing (genu varum) produces a sideways "thrust" at the knee during walking, difficulty walking, or persistent pain. Bowing occurs in roughly 40–70% of children; bracing is not indicated for it (Consensus Rec. 93), and surgery is rarely needed in the first 5 years (Westmead).
  • Orthodontic assessment at 5–6 years, ideally alongside ENT/breathing specialists — dental crowding and palate issues are common and connect to the airway.

Tests & imaging

  • Sleep study for suspected obstructive sleep apnea — snoring is common; get pulmonology input and polysomnography if there are retractions, glottal stops, choking, observed apneas, deep sighs, new bedwetting after being dry (secondary enuresis), night waking, or vomiting (AAP). OSA affects roughly half or more of children with achondroplasia at some point.
  • After an adenotonsillectomy: control sleep study 2–4 months post-surgery (EAF 2025) — residual OSA occurs in ~18%. Overall, 25–40% of children undergo adenotonsillectomy; counting all pharyngeal surgeries over a lifetime, around 45% have at least one.
  • Standing full-leg X-rays only if angular leg deformity develops (Consensus Rec. 91).
  • No routine repeat MRI — imaging is symptom-driven from here on. Careful clinical monitoring of the skull-base issue continues to at least age 3 (EAF).

What parents can watch at home

  • Sleep: snoring with pauses, gasps, morning headaches, unusual daytime tiredness or behavior change.
  • Walking: a knee that visibly shifts sideways with each step, new limping, or complaints of leg pain.
  • Reflux, and heavy sweating — sweating alone is usually normal in achondroplasia.
  • Note: the soft spot can normally stay open to age 5–6 in achondroplasia — your doctor will not be alarmed by this alone.

Development & therapy

  • Fine motor: draws a circle by ~33 months (Westmead). Short fingers and hypermobile joints can make cutting, drawing, and dressing slower — an occupational therapist can adapt tools rather than lower expectations.
  • Feeding self-sufficiency: self-feeds with a spoon by 15–27 months; drinks from a cup by 8–22 months.
  • Children with achondroplasia are expected to follow normal education pathways — intelligence is normal, full stop.
  • Do not "baby" the child: they look younger than they are, so give age-appropriate play, language, and responsibility (Westmead).

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Weight: plot BMI on achondroplasia-specific charts and build active habits now — obesity worsens the spine, the airway, and the joints later.
  • Adapt the home for independence: step stools, lever door handles, lowered light switches, toileting aids (AAP). Practical ideas on the Everyday Life page.
  • Car seat: use a well-fitted full-harness seat as long as possible under local safety standards (Consensus Rec. 66).
  • Avoid passive smoking and, where possible, close contact with people with respiratory infections — small chests have less pulmonary reserve (Consensus Rec. 105).

Discuss with your team

  • Preschool/school preparation: an access visit and written recommendations (reachable hooks, steps at sinks and toilets, classroom near amenities — the full Westmead list is on Everyday Life).
  • Growth-modifying treatment status and goals, if using or considering — reviews every 3 months at ages 0–2, every 4–6 months at 3–5 on vosoritide per the 2025 implementation consensus.

5–13 years

School age: fewer tests, more prevention. The two headline items are weight and the yearly reflex check. Details on the Childhood page.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Review at least annually (GeneReviews cadence), with growth and weight at every visit — obesity prevention is the major theme of this band (AAP).
  • Annual hearing checks with ENT follow-up as needed; orthodontic and speech review — dental crowding and palate issues are common.
  • Any new neurological finding → skeletal dysplasia team / neurosurgery review promptly.

Tests & imaging

  • Deep-tendon reflexes checked yearly for asymmetry or increased briskness — the early clue to spinal stenosis (AAP).
  • Keep asking about obstructive sleep apnea at every visit; sleep study if symptoms.
  • Spine and leg alignment reviewed clinically; X-rays only if deformity or symptoms.

What parents and kids can watch at home

  • New tripping, clumsiness, numbness or tingling, weakness, or changes in bladder/bowel habits — report promptly (possible cord or nerve compression; see Warning Signs).
  • Return of snoring/pauses, morning headaches, slipping school performance or daytime sleepiness (sleep apnea can masquerade as attention problems).
  • Joint pain and fatigue with writing or long walks — common with hypermobile joints, and worth raising rather than pushing through (Westmead).

Development & school

  • Children belong in mainstream education; prepare the school, not just the child: desk and foot support (supported sitting protects the spine), step stools, reachable door handles and restrooms, extra time or rest on long walks, modified PE rather than exclusion (AAP + Westmead).
  • Fine-motor speed and accuracy (writing, cutting) may need monitoring, modified tools, or extra time in class.
  • Prepare the child for peers' questions; involve them in explaining their condition their own way. The Wellbeing page has scripts and support ideas.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Avoid trampolines, competitive gymnastics, and collision/contact sports — lifetime rule, because of the risk of neurological injury at the neck (AAP). Swimming and biking are encouraged.
  • Weight: healthy eating involving the whole family, plotted on achondroplasia BMI charts.
  • Car: full-harness child seat to its highest allowed weight, then a belt-positioning booster (AAP).

Discuss with your team

  • Limb lengthening, if the family wants to explore it: guidelines advise deferring so the young person can participate in the decision, with psychological consultation before undertaking it (Consensus Rec. 62). See Treatments.
  • Growth-modifying medication reviews (every 6 months over age 5 on vosoritide, per the 2025 consensus) — treatment decisions always belong with your own specialist team, not a website.
  • Pain: ask the child directly at each review — kids often under-report.

13–18 years: adolescence & transition

The teen band adds three new conversations — genetics, driving, and the handover to adult care — on top of the ongoing checks. The Teen Years page covers this stage in full.

Doctor visits & referrals

  • Continue at least annual reviews with growth records and weight counseling; monitor pain and its effect on mood, self-care, school, and leisure (Consensus Rec. 68).
  • Any spinal stenosis symptoms → referral to an experienced spinal service (Consensus Rec. 69).
  • Offer genetic counseling from adolescence: inheritance (50% chance per pregnancy with one affected parent; 25% risk of lethal homozygous achondroplasia when both parents have it) and reproductive options (Consensus Recs. 7, 81).
  • Mental-health support offered proactively — dedicated professional support for adjustment, coping, school and employment planning (Consensus Rec. 73).

Tests & imaging

  • Annual formal hearing tests (AAP).
  • Neuro-oriented exam at each review: nerve-compression signs, reflexes, tone, sensation.
  • Sleep study if symptomatic (snoring, pauses, daytime sleepiness).
  • Orthodontic status; jaw (orthognathic) surgery becomes an option only after bone growth completes (Consensus Rec. 114).

What teens can watch themselves

  • Numbness, tingling, weakness, or heaviness in the legs, especially with standing or walking; changes in bladder or bowel control (urgent).
  • Snoring, unrefreshing sleep, morning headaches.
  • Mood, body image, bullying — real medical agenda items, not extras. See Wellbeing & Community.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Keep the lifetime activity rules (no collision sports, trampolines, or competitive gymnastics; swimming and cycling encouraged); stay active — weight control gets harder and matters more from here.
  • Driving: pedal extenders and adapted vehicles work well; get guidance on airbag on/off switches (in the US, via NHTSA) (AAP). Vehicle safety deserves emphasis — a large mortality study found motor-vehicle accidents were a leading cause of death at ages 5–24.
  • Some teens avoid modified equipment at school out of embarrassment — ask about it and problem-solve together (Consensus).

Discuss with your team

  • Transition to adult care: ask for specific transition appointments (Consensus Rec. 131) and a written summary of the person's history — see the transition section and Build your binder.
  • Contraception and pre-conception planning — people with achondroplasia are usually fertile; for women, any future delivery from about 32 weeks onward should be by planned caesarean section (see the adult band).
  • If on growth-modifying treatment: stopping criteria (for vosoritide, when growth velocity falls below ~1.5 cm/year with growth-plate closure on X-ray, per the 2025 consensus) and structured post-treatment follow-up.
  • Standard adolescent counseling: smoking, alcohol, sexual health, mental health (AAP).

Adults: the annual review

Most adults with achondroplasia lead ordinary, independent lives — and the adult checklist exists to keep it that way. Historical cohorts showed roughly a 10-year reduction in average life expectancy, driven largely by causes that are now preventable (infant brainstem compression, untreated sleep apnea, cardiovascular disease); more recent cohorts show clear improvement with modern proactive care. The levers are on this list. GeneReviews suggests a formal review every 3–5 years at minimum; the European Achondroplasia Forum publishes a patient-held adult health checklist (2024) precisely because adult follow-up tends to lapse. The Adults & Aging page explains each item.

Doctor visits — what an annual check should cover

  • Spinal stenosis surveillance: symptom history (leg pain, numbness or weakness that eases when sitting, squatting, or leaning forward — "neurogenic claudication"; reduced walking distance; any bladder/bowel change) plus a neurologic exam. Back pain with neurologic symptoms → MRI of the entire spine (Consensus Rec. 74), and imaging-confirmed impingement → prompt referral to an experienced spine center. Symptoms most commonly begin from the 20s–40s onward; conservative measures (weight loss, physical therapy) come first, surgery when they fail.
  • Blood pressure at every visit, with a properly sized cuff — forearm measurement is an accepted option when the upper arm is short (Consensus Rec. 77). Cardiovascular health is the big adult modifiable risk: one 42-year cohort found heart-disease deaths at ages 25–35 more than 10 times the general population rate.
  • Weight on condition-specific BMI references, with diet and activity review.
  • Sleep apnea: symptom check every visit; overnight sleep study for any symptoms (Consensus Rec. 76) — OSA affects around 60% of adults. CPAP users need regular sleep-specialist review.
  • Hearing: low threshold for testing; consider routine screening earlier than the general population (Consensus Rec. 78) — functionally relevant hearing loss affects ~40% across life.
  • Pain assessed at every check-up (tools like the Brief Pain Inventory): pain affects up to ~75% of adults and is under-treated; chronic pain deserves multidisciplinary management, not resignation.
  • Mental health and quality of life screening as part of routine care — physical and mental QoL scores tend to run lower than the general population, and support helps.
  • Routine general health care as for everyone else: there is no increased cancer risk — participate in all standard screening programs (Consensus).

Before any surgery or anesthesia

  • Anesthesia should be given by staff experienced with achondroplasia (Consensus Rec. 83): airway management is harder (small mouth opening, limited neck movement), the neck must be handled with extreme care, drugs are dosed by weight not age, IV access can be difficult, and spinal/epidural anesthesia needs individualized assessment of the narrow spinal canal.
  • Pre-operative assessment should cover airway, neck range, and snoring/OSA history; pre-op sleep study and cervical-spine imaging are recommended in the skeletal-dysplasia perioperative consensus (2017).

Pregnancy (women with achondroplasia)

  • Pregnancy is usually possible and babies are usually healthy — but plan ahead: pre-conception evaluation, obstetrician-led (not midwife-only) care, anesthesia consultation during pregnancy, respiratory monitoring in the third trimester, and delivery in a hospital with critical-care facilities.
  • Any delivery from about 32 weeks onward should be by planned caesarean section at a hospital with critical-care facilities (before 32 weeks, individualized expert assessment) — the pelvis cannot safely pass even an average-size head.
  • Genetic counseling covers the inheritance odds; serial ultrasound, including at least one third-trimester scan, checks the baby.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Exercise throughout life to maintain strength, healthy body composition, and flexibility (Consensus Rec. 51) — it is also the first-line treatment for early stenosis symptoms.
  • Aids and adaptations at home, at work, and in the car are a normal part of adult life and worth reviewing periodically (Consensus Rec. 84) — see Everyday Life.
  • Keep your own copy of your records and the EAF adult checklist (below) — adult care is where the follow-up gap opens.

Older adults

The European LIAISE study found complications follow a U-shape across life — clustering in infancy and again after 40. The later arm of the U is mostly about the spine, the heart, sleep, hearing, and pain — all monitorable.

Keep on the annual list

  • Spine first: cumulative risk of spinal stenosis needing surgery approaches 90%+ by age 80 in natural-history data — so treat every new leg symptom, walking-distance change, or bladder/bowel change as reportable. Signs of cord involvement (weakness, clumsy hands or gait, brisk reflexes, clonus, bladder/bowel changes) → MRI (Consensus Rec. 89). Decompression, when needed, works best done early rather than after fixed damage.
  • Blood pressure (properly sized cuff), weight, and cardiovascular risk review every visit.
  • Sleep apnea and hearing checks continue; hearing aids are a routine option for conductive loss.
  • Pain at every check-up, with active multidisciplinary management — 37.9% of adults in LIAISE reported moderate-to-extreme pain or discomfort, and its impact on work and daily life is measurable and addressable.
  • Mental health and social participation — isolation is a health risk like any other; see Wellbeing & Community.
  • All standard older-age screening (cancer screening, vaccinations) exactly as for the general population.

What to watch at home

  • Leg pain, tingling, numbness, or weakness that improves when you sit, squat, or lean forward (e.g., over a shopping cart) — the classic stenosis pattern. Track your comfortable walking distance; shrinkage is data your doctor needs.
  • New bladder or bowel changes with back or leg symptoms are an emergency — same-day care. Full list on Warning Signs.
  • Snoring, unrefreshing sleep, daytime dozing; new hearing difficulty.

Lifestyle & prevention

  • Stay active within comfort — strength, flexibility, and weight control remain the best protection for the spine and heart.
  • Review home and mobility adaptations as needs change; falls prevention becomes standard-issue advice here as for all older adults.

Build your binder

People with achondroplasia see many clinicians across many decades, and no single hospital system holds the whole story. The International Consensus explicitly recommends that families keep their own register of growth parameters (Rec. 26). A simple binder — paper or digital — solves the problem.

What goes in the binder

Always-current pages: diagnosis confirmation (the FGFR3 result and/or X-ray report), medication list, the achondroplasia growth charts with your own plotted points, and this checklist with your clinic's amendments.

Results, newest first: every sleep-study report, MRI/CT report (ask for the images on disc or portal access too), audiogram, spine X-ray report, and operation note. Bring the actual reports — "it was normal" is not enough when a new specialist needs the baseline.

Printables to add: achondroplasia-specific growth charts from the free chart hub at achondroplasia-growthcharts.com; the Johns Hopkins Greenberg Center developmental recording form (achondroplasia-specific milestone centiles); for adults, the European Achondroplasia Forum patient-held adult health checklist (2024) — ask your clinic or patient organization for a copy; and the AAP report itself (free full text) to hand to any pediatrician who hasn't cared for a child with achondroplasia before.

At transition to adult care, the binder becomes the handover document — a teen who owns their binder owns their health story. See Teen Years.
