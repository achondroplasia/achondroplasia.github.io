Treatments & Research

An honest, complete tour of every treatment for achondroplasia — the approved growth medicines, the pipeline, limb lengthening, the surgeries that treat complications, and the "treatments" that are scams — plus how families actually decide.

Start here: treatment is a choice, not a fix

Before any drug names or trial numbers, one thing needs saying plainly: achondroplasia is not something that has to be "fixed." People with achondroplasia have typical intelligence, and generations of them have built full, independent, happy lives long before any growth medicine existed. Little People of America and the wider dwarfism community emphasize that short stature itself is not a disease — and within that community you will find every view on growth-modifying treatment, from families who embrace it to adults who decline it on principle. Both are legitimate decisions. The Wellbeing & Community page explores the identity side of this conversation in depth.

What does need active medical attention is a set of specific complications — pressure at the opening at the base of the skull (the foramen magnum) in infancy, obstructive sleep apnea, ear infections and hearing loss, bowed legs, and narrowing of the spinal canal (spinal stenosis) in adulthood. For most of history, achondroplasia care meant exactly that: monitoring for these complications and treating them when they arise. That is still the foundation of good care, and nothing on this page replaces it.

The treatments below fall into two groups worth keeping mentally separate:

  • Growth-modifying treatments (vosoritide, navepegritide, the pipeline drugs, limb lengthening) — these change how much or how fast bones grow. Whether to use them is a values question as much as a medical one.
  • Complication treatments (decompression surgery, ear tubes, adenotonsillectomy, guided growth, spinal surgery) — these treat specific medical problems when monitoring detects them, and they are covered in detail on the relevant life-stage pages, with a quick map below.
The state of play in mid-2026

For decades there was no medicine for achondroplasia. Since 2021 there has been one approved growth drug (vosoritide/Voxzogo, a daily injection), since February 2026 a second (navepegritide/Yuviwel, a weekly injection), and a third — infigratinib, a daily pill — reported positive phase 3 results in February 2026 with regulatory filings planned for the second half of 2026. Every one of them makes children grow faster while taking it. What is not yet proven for any of them is the effect on final adult height, on the serious complications, or on quality of life over a lifetime. Those answers are still being collected — which is exactly why regulators used "accelerated approval" pathways that require ongoing confirmatory data.

How the growth medicines work: the FGFR3 and CNP story

Achondroplasia is caused by a single change (mutation) in the FGFR3 gene — the same change in 98–99% of people, which is why one drug can work for nearly everyone. (The full genetics story is on the Understanding It page, and every term here is in the glossary.)

FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3) sits on the cartilage cells inside the growth plates of bones, where it normally acts as a brake on bone growth. The achondroplasia mutation leaves that brake stuck partly on, mostly through a signaling chain inside the cell called the MAPK pathway. The result: the long bones of the arms and legs, the base of the skull, and the spine grow less than usual.

The body has a natural counter-signal: C-type natriuretic peptide (CNP). When CNP binds its receptor (called NPR-B) on those same cartilage cells, it switches off the MAPK branch of FGFR3 signaling — in the brake analogy, CNP is the hand that eases the brake off. Natural CNP is destroyed within minutes in the bloodstream, so it cannot simply be injected as-is. The approved medicines solve that problem in different ways, and the pipeline pill attacks the brake directly:

One rule applies to all growth drugs

They only work while the growth plates (the cartilage zones at the ends of bones, also called epiphyses) are still open — that is, during childhood and adolescence. Once the growth plates close after puberty, no drug can add height. This is why treatment is stopped when an X-ray confirms growth-plate closure, and why these drugs are not an option for adults.

Vosoritide (Voxzogo): the first approved medicine

Vosoritide is a once-daily injection under the skin (subcutaneous) of a stabilized CNP analog. It has the longest track record of any achondroplasia drug and is the only one approved from birth.

What the trials showed

In the pivotal phase 3 trial (121 children aged 5 to under 18, published in The Lancet in 2020), a year of vosoritide at 15 µg/kg daily increased growth velocity by about 1.57 cm/year over placebo — roughly 50% faster growth than untreated peers of the same age. Extension studies show the effect is sustained over multiple years of treatment, with additional reported findings over time including improved spinal alignment, less leg bowing, and some improvement in physical quality-of-life measures after about three years.

What about final adult height? Lead with what regulators say: the effect on final adult height has not yet been established. Long-term extension data are encouraging, and "near-final height" results are emerging — but the FDA label still carries that caveat. You may also read about computer models projecting roughly 26 cm of extra adult height in males and 22 cm in females if treatment ran from birth to maturity: those are unvalidated model projections, not observed results, and no child has yet been followed from birth on treatment all the way to adult height.

Approval timeline

  • November 2021 (FDA): accelerated approval for ages 5 and older, based on growth velocity.
  • October 2023 (FDA): expanded to children under 5 — effectively from birth — with open growth plates, after a randomized placebo-controlled trial in ages 0–5.
  • Europe: approved August 2021 from age 2; extended in late 2023 to age 4 months and older.
  • Continued approval remains contingent on confirmatory long-term data — the accelerated-approval framework.

Daily injections in practice

Parents and caregivers are trained to give the injection at home. The label uses weight-band dosing — younger and lighter children receive a higher dose per kilogram (the pivotal trial dose was 15 µg/kg daily). Your care team sets and adjusts the dose; nothing here is dosing advice. Practical points families learn in training:

  • Injection-site reactions are very common — redness, swelling, or hives at the spot, almost always mild and short-lived. In the pivotal trial these occurred at about 120 events per patient-year on drug versus 29 on placebo. Ask your team to show you how to rotate injection sites to give skin a break.
  • The hypotension precaution: about 13% of treated children in the trial (versus 5% on placebo) had temporary drops in blood pressure, typically starting around 31 minutes after injection and resolving within about 31 minutes; a small number felt dizzy or nauseated. The label's advice: the child should be well hydrated and have eaten, including drinking about 8–10 ounces (240–300 mL) of fluid in the hour before the injection.
  • Other common reactions (in more than 10% of children): vomiting, joint pain (arthralgia), rash, gastroenteritis.
  • Ask your team exactly what to do about a missed dose before it happens — get it in writing with the rest of your injection training.
  • Real-world data so far (for example, the French early-access program) have mirrored the trial safety profile.

Stopping rules

Treatment continues until an X-ray confirms the growth plates have closed — at that point the drug is stopped, because it can no longer do anything. There is no taper or withdrawal issue; it simply has no target left.

The foramen magnum question — hope, with honest limits

The biggest hope for starting treatment in infancy is not height — it is that easing the FGFR3 brake might help the skull-base opening (foramen magnum) grow, reducing the risk of brainstem compression and the need for decompression surgery (explained on the First Years page). The evidence so far, honestly labeled:

  • Encouraging but early (phase 2 evidence): in a phase 2 study of infants, foramen magnum area grew about 44% in treated infants under 6 months versus about 25% in untreated comparators. That is a trend from a small early-phase study — not proof that surgery is prevented.
  • A dedicated trial in infants at risk of needing cervicomedullary decompression is running (NCT04554940).
  • The counter-signal: a 2025 case report described an infant whose foramen magnum stenosis progressed to needing surgery despite vosoritide.
A growth drug is not a safety net

Whether or not a child is on treatment, the monitoring schedule stays the same: MRI surveillance, sleep studies, hearing checks, and spine monitoring continue exactly as on the care checklist. Signs of brainstem compression in an infant — long breathing pauses, severe snoring, unusual floppiness or weakness, asymmetric movement, abnormal reflexes, unusual head arching — are a medical emergency: call your neurosurgical team or go to the emergency department now. See Warning Signs for the full list.

Navepegritide (Yuviwel): the once-weekly option

Navepegritide — you will also see it called TransCon CNP — takes the same CNP molecule and attaches it to an inert carrier that releases active CNP slowly over a week, so one injection replaces seven. Same target (NPR-B, easing the same FGFR3 brake), different delivery.

The ApproaCH trial and approval

  • In the pivotal ApproaCH trial (84 children aged 2–11, randomized 2:1 to weekly navepegritide or placebo; published in JAMA Pediatrics, 2025), growth velocity at one year was 5.89 cm/yr on drug versus 4.41 cm/yr on placebo — a difference of about 1.49 cm/yr. Ages 5–11 gained +1.78 cm/yr over placebo; ages 2–4 gained +1.02 cm/yr (younger children on placebo still grow relatively fast, so the gap is smaller).
  • The trial also reported improved lower-limb alignment (less bowing), improved body proportions, and quality-of-life gains; two-year extension data presented in 2026 show the growth benefit maintained through week 104.
  • FDA accelerated approval came on February 27, 2026 for ages 2 and older with open growth plates, with US launch in April 2026. In Europe, the application was submitted to the EMA in October 2025 and a decision is pending as of this writing. As with vosoritide, continued approval depends on confirmatory long-term evidence — and the effect on final adult height is not established.

Side effects and practicalities

In the trial the overall safety profile was similar to placebo. The most common effects (5% or more): vomiting, injection-site reactions, arm/leg pain, and nausea. Injection-site reactions were far less frequent than with daily vosoritide — on the order of one every couple of years of treatment. Increased body hair (hypertrichosis) occurred in about 3%. Notably, there was no symptomatic low-blood-pressure signal: the label has no pre-injection food or fluid requirement, and dosing can float ±2 days around the scheduled day — flexibility many families find easier to live with.

Vosoritide or navepegritide?

They work on the same pathway, so they are alternatives, not add-ons. The trade-offs clinicians describe: vosoritide brings a daily injection but the longest track record and approval from birth; navepegritide brings a weekly injection, fewer injection-site reactions, and no hypotension precautions, but approval only from age 2 and a shorter safety history. No head-to-head trial exists — the growth numbers look broadly similar across trials, but the trial populations differed, so direct comparison is unreliable. (Ascendis is also testing navepegritide combined with a long-acting growth hormone in the COACH trial — combination therapy is strictly experimental.)

The pipeline: infigratinib and what comes next

Infigratinib — the daily pill (watch 2026–2027)

Infigratinib (BridgeBio) is a low-dose, once-daily oral tyrosine kinase inhibitor of FGFR1–3. Instead of boosting the CNP counter-signal, it reaches into the brake mechanism itself and directly quiets the overactive FGFR3 receptor. It was originally developed at much higher doses as a cancer drug; the achondroplasia dose (0.25 mg/kg/day, chosen in the phase 2 PROPEL 2 study) is a small fraction of that.

In February 2026, the pivotal phase 3 trial — PROPEL 3 (children 3 to under 18 with open growth plates, randomized 2:1, 52 weeks; published in the New England Journal of Medicine, 2026) — reported positive topline results:

  • Growth velocity of 5.96 cm/yr on infigratinib versus 4.22 cm/yr on placebo — a company-reported raw difference of about +2.1 cm/yr (least-squares mean difference +1.74 cm/yr) — the largest seen in any randomized achondroplasia trial, though cross-trial comparisons remain unreliable.
  • The first statistically significant improvement in body proportions (upper-to-lower body segment ratio) in a phase 3 achondroplasia study — significant in children under 8.
  • Safety in the trial: no drug-related serious adverse events or discontinuations; mild, transient, symptomless high phosphate levels (hyperphosphatemia) in about 4% — a known FGFR-inhibitor effect; no eye toxicity; and, because it is not a CNP drug, no injection reactions or blood-pressure effects. It is a pill — which matters enormously to needle-averse children.

Regulatory status, stated precisely: infigratinib is not yet approved anywhere. Regulatory submissions (a New Drug Application in the US and a Marketing Authorisation Application in Europe) are planned for the second half of 2026; the company anticipates a possible US launch in 2027. It holds FDA Breakthrough Therapy, Fast Track, Orphan Drug, and Rare Pediatric Disease designations. Expect regulators to look hard at long-term safety — FGFR receptors do many jobs in the body, and this would be a years-long childhood treatment.

Further back in the pipeline

  • TYRA-300 (dabogratinib): a next-generation oral inhibitor designed to be selective for FGFR3 — aiming for infigratinib-like benefit with even less off-target activity. The phase 2 BEACH301 trial in children aged 3–10 dosed its first child in 2025; preclinical data hint at possible benefit for foramen magnum and spine dimensions. Years from any approval.
  • Meclizine: an old, cheap motion-sickness antihistamine that inhibits FGFR3 downstream signaling in the lab; only small exploratory studies exist (mainly in Japan). Not proven, not an approved use — do not give it to a child as a growth treatment.
  • Beyond single drugs: combination approaches (such as CNP plus growth hormone in the COACH trial) are being explored strictly in trials. No gene therapy or legitimate stem-cell therapy for achondroplasia exists as of 2026.

The failures — and why they are worth knowing about

  • Recifercept (Pfizer) — discontinued 2022–2023. A "decoy receptor" designed to soak up growth factors before they reached the mutant FGFR3. Pfizer stopped development after a planned interim analysis showed the phase 2 study would not meet its efficacy endpoint — not for safety reasons. The lesson for families evaluating any future therapy: a plausible mechanism and a good story can still fail. Randomized data matter.
  • SAR442501 (Sanofi) — discontinued. An anti-FGFR3 antibody dropped early in development during a company-wide pipeline reprioritization (a business decision, not a safety signal).

How to evaluate — or join — a clinical trial

Every real advance above happened because families joined trials, and trials genuinely need participants. To vet one:

  1. Find it on clinicaltrials.gov (or the EU/WHO registries). Every legitimate trial is registered with an NCT number, a named sponsor, a phase, and sites. Real trials never charge you for the experimental drug.
  2. Understand the phase: phase 1 tests safety and dosing; phase 2 tests early efficacy; phase 3 is the decisive randomized test; an open-label extension gives everyone the drug after the controlled period. Observational and natural-history studies (like the PROPEL run-in study) collect data without giving a drug — joining one is a low-burden way to help and often a doorway to later treatment trials.
  3. Questions for the study team: What is the chance of getting placebo, and for how long? Will my child get the drug afterward in an extension? What exactly is measured — growth velocity, function, complications — and does it matter to us? What are the known and theoretical risks? How many visits and how much travel, and who pays travel costs? Can we withdraw at any time (the answer is always yes)? Who reviews safety (ethics committee, data safety monitoring board)? Will participating now affect eligibility for approved drugs later?
  4. Sanity-check anything you are offered with your skeletal dysplasia clinic and with LPA — both track the legitimate achondroplasia studies.

Growth hormone: approved only in Japan — and a cautionary tale

Recombinant human growth hormone has been approved for achondroplasia only in Japan, since 1997 — and from 1997 to 2021 it was the world's only approved drug for the condition. Its story is a useful caution about early promise:

  • Growth speeds up at first, but the effect fades with time. Long-term Japanese data put the final adult height gain at only about +3.5 cm in males and +2.8 cm in females.
  • Children with achondroplasia are not growth-hormone deficient (their GH and IGF-1 levels are normal), and there are concerns GH could aggravate disproportion and comorbidities such as foramen magnum stenosis and obstructive sleep apnea.
  • The AAP health-supervision guidance and international consensus practice do not recommend growth hormone for achondroplasia outside Japan — and even in Japan it is being superseded by the CNP-pathway drugs.
If a clinic offers your child growth hormone "for dwarfism"

Outside Japan, that is off-guideline. Get a second opinion from a skeletal dysplasia center before agreeing — the Medical Library lists how to find one.

Should we start a growth drug — and when?

There is no single right answer, and a good clinic will never pressure you either way. A useful framing that clinicians and the community both use — weigh four things:

  1. What is proven: faster growth while on treatment, and (in trial data) improvements in proportion and limb alignment.
  2. What is hoped but unproven: more final adult height, fewer complications (foramen magnum stenosis, spinal stenosis, sleep apnea), better lifelong function and quality of life.
  3. The real burdens: daily or weekly injections for years, clinic monitoring, possible side effects, and cost or insurance battles.
  4. The legitimacy of not treating: fully supported by generations of people with achondroplasia thriving without it — this is a valid endpoint of the decision, not a failure to decide.

Timing adds its own layer. The strongest argument for starting early (vosoritide is approved from birth) is the hoped-for effect on the foramen magnum and complications in the years when they matter most — but that is exactly the part of the evidence still labeled "encouraging trend," not proof. Starting later means fewer total years of injections but forgoes whatever early benefit may exist. And as children grow up, their own voice belongs in the decision — many families revisit the question yearly rather than deciding once.

Questions worth bringing to your clinic

  • What outcome are we actually hoping for — height, proportion, fewer surgeries, function? Which of those does the evidence currently support?
  • Given our child's age and monitoring findings so far (MRI, sleep study), what would you expect treatment to change — and what would it not change?
  • Which drug fits our family's life — daily with the longest record, or weekly with fewer injection reactions? Would a trial (for example, of the oral pill) be a reasonable alternative for us?
  • How will we know if it is "working," and what would make you recommend stopping?
  • What does the lived experience of adults with achondroplasia — treated and untreated — tell us? (The community perspectives page is a good companion here.)
A reminder before any treatment decision

This page is education, not medical advice. Decisions about starting, choosing, or stopping any treatment belong with your own care team — ideally a multidisciplinary skeletal dysplasia clinic that knows your child. Nothing here, including any numbers about doses, is a recommendation.

Cost, insurance, and getting access

The prices are genuinely startling, so let's put them on the table and then talk about why most treated families do not pay anything like them:

  • Vosoritide (Voxzogo): US list price about $320,000 per year (roughly $240,000 net after discounts); about CAD $347,000 in Canada and around €260,000 in Europe before confidential discounts.
  • Navepegritide (Yuviwel): US wholesale cost at launch of $38,325 per four weekly doses — roughly $498,000 per year, about a 20% premium over Voxzogo's net price.

In the US, insurance and Medicaid coverage is typical when the label criteria are met — but almost never automatic. What the process actually looks like:

  • Prior authorization is universal. Packets that succeed typically contain: the FGFR3 genetic test result confirming achondroplasia; evidence of open growth plates (a hand or knee X-ray); a letter of medical necessity from a pediatric endocrinologist or geneticist at a skeletal dysplasia center; and growth records plotted on achondroplasia-specific charts.
  • First denials are common — and frequently overturned. Never accept a first "no." Ask the skeletal dysplasia clinic's authorization team to appeal, and if internal appeals fail, use your state's external-review program: these overturn a large share of specialty-drug denials (around 50% in Pennsylvania's program, for example).
  • Use the manufacturer hubs — that is what they are for. BioMarin RareConnections (1-833-869-9646) and Ascendis Signature Access do much of the paperwork and run copay-assistance programs that can bring commercially insured families to $0 out of pocket.
  • TEFRA / Katie Beckett Medicaid: most states offer a pathway letting a child under 19 qualify for Medicaid based on the child's disability and income alone — ignoring parental income — when the child meets an institutional level-of-care standard but lives at home. It can act as secondary insurance covering what primary insurance rejects. Compare states at kidswaivers.org.
  • EPSDT: for any child on Medicaid, the Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment benefit legally obligates coverage of medically necessary services for children — a powerful lever when a state plan "doesn't cover" something a physician has documented as medically necessary.
  • Keep a binder (or shared drive) of every genetic report, sleep study, MRI report, growth chart, and denial letter. The same documents get requested for every authorization, for decades. (More paperwork strategy on the Everyday Life page.)

Limb lengthening surgery

Extended limb lengthening (ELL) is the surgical route to more height and reach — and the single most debated topic in the dwarfism community. Here is the whole picture, stated as fairly as we can.

What it is and how it works

Surgeons cut the bone (an osteotomy) and then pull the two ends apart very slowly — about 1 mm per day — so new bone continuously forms in the widening gap. The technique is called distraction osteogenesis, historically the Ilizarov method. Two device families do the pulling:

  • External fixators — rings or rails outside the limb, attached by pins through the skin. The classic approach; pin sites need daily care and can get infected.
  • Internal motorized telescopic nails (for example, PRECICE) — implanted inside the bone and lengthened from outside with a magnet. No pin sites, but implant-specific risks and higher cost.

What it can achieve

Published series report average gains per bone segment of roughly 8.9 cm in the femur (thigh), 7.4 cm in the tibia (shin), and 8.4 cm in the humerus (upper arm). Full "extended" programs — lengthening thighs, shins, and often upper arms across multiple staged operations over several years — can add 20–30+ cm of height (GeneReviews cites up to roughly 30–35 cm as possible).

Arm (humeral) lengthening deserves its own mention. Adding around 8 cm of arm reach can meaningfully change self-care — dressing, toileting, driving — and some families pursue arms even while declining leg lengthening. It is the part of ELL with the clearest functional (rather than appearance) case.

The honest costs

  • Time and pain: each segment means months in a fixator or nail, daily lengthening adjustments, intensive physiotherapy, missed school, and significant pain. A full program commonly spans 2–4+ years of childhood or adolescence.
  • Complications are common: across studies, roughly 20% of lengthened segments — and in some series over 50% of patients — experience complications: pin-site infections, joint stiffness and contractures (hip, knee, ankle), nerve injury, fractures of the new bone, delayed healing, and possible long-term arthritis. Most are treatable; some are not trivial.
  • There is no medical indication. LPA's Medical Advisory Board states it plainly: "There are no established medical indications for symmetric extended limb lengthening." It is an elective procedure about appearance and function. (Do not confuse it with limb realignment surgery for severe symptomatic bowing — that is medically indicated and is a different, much smaller operation, covered on the Childhood page.)

The controversy — stated fairly

Within the dwarfism community, many view ELL as unnecessary "normalization" surgery — changing the person to fit the world rather than the world to fit the person (a theme explored on Wellbeing & Community, with the practical alternative — adapting the environment — on Everyday Life). Others who chose it report real satisfaction with the functional gains. In an international survey of 467 people, only 23% of those who knew the procedure would recommend it, 28% would not, and 49% were unsure — about as evenly split as opinion gets.

LPA's official position is deliberately neutral — "not intended to either advocate for or condemn" ELL — but it insists on conditions, and the International Consensus Statement (2021) adds its own (recommendation 62: the possibility should be discussed with the patient and family during childhood, with psychological consultation before undertaking it):

  • Confirmed diagnosis and counseling about the natural history of achondroplasia;
  • Full disclosure of risks and complication rates;
  • Psychological assessment first — self-image, body image, peer and family relationships;
  • Surgery only at experienced, multidisciplinary skeletal dysplasia centers with long-term follow-up, with the team involved before and after;
  • And — crucially — that the patient "should be of an age to participate fully in these discussions." The AAP and many ethicists likewise recommend deferring purely elective lengthening until the young person can genuinely take part in, and ideally lead, the decision — not deciding it for a toddler. (See Teen Years for how this conversation often lands in adolescence; the survey's mean age at first surgery was 10.5 years.)

Where it is done — and a medical-tourism warning

Practice varies enormously by country and culture. In the European LIAISE study, about 60% of Italian and 56% of Spanish adult respondents had undergone limb lengthening versus about 17% in Germany (19.5% overall across 24 countries). In North America only a small minority choose it. Russia and Turkey also have well-known lengthening centers. A medical-tourism industry sells lengthening abroad at attractive prices — but managing a complication from thousands of miles away is dangerous. If considering ELL, use a center that does high volumes in skeletal dysplasia patients specifically and will follow your child for years.

How the new drugs change the calculus

Whether vosoritide, navepegritide, or infigratinib will reduce demand for lengthening — or end up combined with it — is an open question actively discussed in the field. Drug-treated children are still expected to have below-typical stature, so families weighing both paths should discuss sequencing with a skeletal dysplasia team rather than treating the two as either/or.

What doesn't work — and how to spot a scam

The genetics here are worth restating because they are the best scam repellent a family can own: the FGFR3 brake is genetic. No nutrient, stretch, or gadget releases it.

  • Supplements, vitamins, minerals, "bone broths," protein powders, homeopathy, Ayurvedic "height" treatments: none increase height in achondroplasia — or in any child, beyond correcting a true deficiency. The US Federal Trade Commission fined the makers of HeightMax $375,000 in 2006 for fabricated claims (including an invented expert) that pills could add 2–3 inches, and in 2026 fined TruHeight $750,000 for unsubstantiated "height growth" claims aimed at kids and teens, boosted by fake reviews. These products target exactly the anxiety families of short-statured children feel.
  • Stretching, hanging, traction devices, insoles, "grow taller" exercise programs: no effect on bone growth. (Normal physical activity and physiotherapy are excellent for strength and function — just not for height.)
  • Growth hormone outside Japan: not recommended — see above.
  • "Stem cell" or gene-therapy clinics: no legitimate stem-cell or gene therapy for achondroplasia exists as of 2026. Any clinic selling one today is selling a scam.
Red flags of a scam — the quick checklist

Cash-only payment or crowdfunding pressure · guarantees ("3 inches in 6 months") · testimonials instead of randomized trials · the same treatment offered to everyone regardless of diagnosis · no publication in peer-reviewed journals · not registered on clinicaltrials.gov · hostility toward your regular doctors. Any two of these together: walk away. When unsure, ask your skeletal dysplasia clinic or LPA — vetting offers is a normal thing to ask them.

Surgery for complications: the quick map

These operations are not about height — they treat specific medical problems when monitoring finds them. Each is covered in depth on its life-stage page; this is the map.

Infancy

Cervicomedullary decompression

Relieves pressure at the foramen magnum when compression is symptomatic or confirmed on MRI. Roughly 7–13% of infants in the first 2 years at experienced centers; around 20% lifetime in one large US cohort, with wide variation between centers (5–43%).

 Infancy & childhood

Ear tubes (tympanostomy)

For middle-ear fluid persisting 3 months or more with documented hearing loss — protecting hearing during the speech-learning years.

 Childhood

Adenotonsillectomy (T&A)

First-line treatment for obstructive sleep apnea. About 25–40% of children with achondroplasia have one; counting all pharyngeal surgeries over a lifetime, around 45% have at least one. A follow-up sleep study comes 2–4 months after.

 Childhood

Guided growth / osteotomy for bowed legs

For genu varum (which occurs in 40–70%) causing pain, instability, or gait problems. Bracing is not indicated; surgery timing is tailored at centers of excellence.

 Teens & adults

Spinal decompression ± fusion

For symptomatic spinal stenosis after conservative measures (weight management, physical therapy) fail — prompt referral to an experienced spine center matters.

One rule for all of these

Surgery in achondroplasia should be done by teams experienced with the condition, decided with the multidisciplinary team — including anesthesia staff who know the airway and neck precautions (International Consensus Statement, 2021). Questions to ask at a skeletal dysplasia clinic — and finding one

Both the International Consensus Statement (2021) and the AAP's 2020 health-supervision report anchor achondroplasia care in lifelong, multidisciplinary management — genetics, pediatrics, neurosurgery, orthopedics, ENT, sleep medicine, endocrinology, audiology, physical and occupational therapy, and psychosocial support working together. For treatment decisions specifically, that team matters because deciding whether and when to start a growth drug (and when to stop) requires achondroplasia-specific growth charts, imaging judgment, and injection training; because drugs never replace complication surveillance; and because experienced centers run or know every credible trial and can fight the insurance battles.

US examples of experienced centers include the Johns Hopkins Greenberg Center, Nemours/duPont, and CHOP — and many countries have equivalents. LPA's medical resources list clinics, and the Medical Library page collects clinic directories and the underlying guidelines.

Bring these questions to a first treatment-focused visit

  • How many patients with achondroplasia does this clinic follow, and who is on the multidisciplinary team?
  • Which growth drugs do you prescribe, and what has your real-world experience matched — or not matched — from the trials?
  • What monitoring would continue regardless of treatment, and on what schedule? (Compare their answer against the care checklist.)
  • If we choose not to treat, how does our follow-up here change? (Correct answer: it doesn't.)
  • Who on your team handles prior authorizations and appeals?
  • Are any trials or registries open here that we should know about?
  • For limb lengthening questions: do you have — or refer to — a high-volume dysplasia lengthening team, and what is your protocol for psychological assessment and long-term follow-up?

And a final word for the road: whatever you decide about treatment — everything, something, or nothing — a child with achondroplasia who is monitored well, supported well, and connected to their community is set up to thrive. The medicine is optional. The monitoring and the belonging are not. Continue to Everyday Life for the practical adaptations that help every day, treated or not.
