Dental & Oral Health

Achondroplasia affects the bones of the face and jaw just as it affects the rest of the skeleton — meaning specific, predictable dental and orthodontic patterns that families and dentists need to know about from the first tooth onward.

The bottom line up front

Most people with achondroplasia will need some form of orthodontic or dental intervention — typically because the jaw bones are small relative to the number and size of the teeth. Early, regular dental care (starting before age 1, per standard AAP/AAPD guidance) combined with a dentist who knows the condition makes a large practical difference. Good news: most dental issues in achondroplasia are treatable, and adults with achondroplasia who had early orthodontic care report good functional outcomes.

Why achondroplasia affects the teeth

The key anatomy: the skull in achondroplasia has two differently affected parts. The vault (top and sides of the skull) grows almost normally, because it forms by intramembranous ossification — a process not greatly impaired by overactive FGFR3. But the skull base (the bottom layer the face sits on) grows by cartilage-based (endochondral) ossification, the same process that is slowed everywhere else. The result: a relatively small skull base produces a relatively small midface — the cheekbones, nose, and upper jaw (maxilla) — while the lower jaw (mandible) grows more normally.

This disproportion — a small maxilla underneath a normally-sized mandible — is the root cause of most of the dental issues on this page. When the upper jaw is smaller than the lower, everything that grows in it is crowded, misaligned, and poorly positioned relative to the lower arch.

Malocclusion: the bite problems

Malocclusion means a bite that does not line up properly. The characteristic patterns in achondroplasia flow directly from the skull-base anatomy:

Class III malocclusion (underbite)

The most frequently reported pattern: the lower teeth sit in front of the upper teeth when biting, because the upper jaw is underdeveloped relative to the lower. Published case series and reviews find Class III malocclusion in 30–70% of people with achondroplasia, a much higher rate than in the general population (where Class III affects about 3–5%). The bite discrepancy ranges from mild (a slightly edge-to-edge relationship) to severe (the lower teeth clearly protrude in front). Severity tends to increase through adolescence as the mandible completes its growth while the maxilla remains small.

Crossbite

When upper back teeth fall inside the lower back teeth rather than outside — a consequence of the narrow upper jaw. Both posterior (back) and anterior (front) crossbites occur. Early palatal expansion (a fixed orthodontic device that gradually widens the upper jaw) can address this before the palatal suture fuses, typically in the mid-teens.

Open bite

When front teeth do not meet at all when the back teeth are closed — common when mouth breathing is also present (below), which can alter the resting tongue position and change how the teeth erupt.

Overjet vs. overbite reversal

In typical dentitions, the upper front teeth sit slightly in front of and overlap the lower front teeth. In achondroplasia the relationship is often reversed: lower teeth protrude (negative overjet) and the lower front teeth close over the upper (reverse overbite). This can cause enamel wear on the upper front teeth and difficulty biting into food.

Crowding and late eruption

Teeth are essentially the same size in people with achondroplasia as in the general population — but the jaw they sit in is smaller. The predictable result is dental crowding: teeth that overlap, rotate, and push each other out of position. Crowded teeth are harder to clean, increasing the risk of decay and gum disease, and they often require extraction of teeth (including permanent teeth in some cases) to make room for orthodontic alignment.

Delayed tooth eruption

Both the primary (baby) teeth and the permanent teeth may erupt later than average — a reflection of the same slowed bone growth that affects the rest of the skeleton. Published reports note delayed eruption of primary teeth, and the permanent teeth may take longer to emerge and may erupt in an unusual order because of crowding. A dental panoramic X-ray (OPG) in the early mixed-dentition phase (around ages 6–8) gives the treating dentist and orthodontist a map of all developing permanent teeth and helps time any extractions or orthodontic interventions.

Supernumerary and retained teeth

Extra (supernumerary) teeth and retained (over-retained) baby teeth that do not shed on time have both been described. An OPG rules these out and guides the treatment sequence.

Mouth breathing and its dental consequences

Narrow nasal passages (from midface hypoplasia) and the very high prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea in achondroplasia mean that many children and adults breathe predominantly through the mouth rather than the nose. Mouth breathing has its own dental and facial consequences:

  • Altered tongue posture: a mouth-breather's tongue rests on the floor of the mouth rather than pressing against the roof. That upper tongue pressure is what naturally widens the upper arch; without it, the arch narrows further and crowding worsens.
  • Dental caries (tooth decay): a dry mouth from mouth breathing reduces saliva flow, which is the mouth's main protection against decay-causing bacteria. Children who mouth-breathe have consistently higher cavity rates.
  • Gum disease: for the same reason — dry, irritated gums are more susceptible to gingivitis.
  • Anterior open bite development: mouth breathing with a low tongue position can cause the front teeth to erupt less completely, creating or worsening an open bite.

The implication: treating sleep apnea (adenotonsillectomy, CPAP) is not only a respiratory and neurological intervention — it has real downstream dental benefits. Tell your orthodontist about any history of sleep apnea and whether it has been treated, because it affects how the orthodontic plan is timed and designed.

Orthodontic treatment: what to expect and when

Orthodontic treatment in achondroplasia broadly follows the same phases as in the general population, with important timing modifications driven by the jaw discrepancy and by the later jaw growth that accompanies later puberty.

Phase 1 (early interceptive) — approximately ages 6–10

The goal of early treatment is to address problems that worsen if left untreated until all permanent teeth are in — primarily crossbites and severe crowding. Common Phase 1 appliances:

  • Palatal expander (RPE or Hyrax): a fixed device bonded to the upper back teeth, activated daily by a key to widen the upper jaw while the midpalatal suture is still open. Must be placed before the suture fuses (typically by the mid-teens, sometimes earlier). Can correct a posterior crossbite and create room for crowded teeth.
  • Space maintainers: if baby teeth are lost early, a fixed spacer prevents neighboring teeth from drifting into the gap before the permanent tooth is ready.
  • Selective extractions: removing specific baby teeth to guide erupting permanent teeth into better positions.

Not everyone needs Phase 1; the orthodontist's assessment with X-rays determines who benefits from early intervention.

Phase 2 (comprehensive) — typically ages 12–18

Full fixed braces or aligners after most or all permanent teeth have erupted. In achondroplasia the Class III bite complicates this phase: standard orthodontics can align the teeth within each arch, but it cannot change the skeletal position of the jaws. When the jaw discrepancy is mild to moderate, orthodontics alone (sometimes with extraction of upper teeth to allow the upper arch to compensate somewhat) gives a functional, acceptable result. When the discrepancy is severe, orthodontics alone cannot achieve a normal bite — which leads to the next section.

The timing constraint: jaw growth must be complete

The lower jaw (mandible) continues growing until the mid-to-late teens in average-height individuals — and puberty, which typically comes a little later in achondroplasia, means jaw growth also finishes a little later. Definitive orthodontic treatment or jaw surgery should generally wait until jaw growth is complete, confirmed by comparing two jaw X-rays (lateral cephalograms) taken 6–12 months apart. Starting too early means the bite can relapse as the jaw continues to grow.

Orthognathic (jaw) surgery

When the jaw discrepancy is skeletal and severe enough that orthodontics alone cannot produce a functional bite, orthognathic surgery (literally "straight jaw" surgery) moves the bones of the upper jaw, the lower jaw, or both into better positions. This is elective surgery on the jaw skeleton, done in a hospital by a maxillofacial surgeon working in coordination with an orthodontist.

For achondroplasia specifically:

  • The most common operation is Le Fort I maxillary advancement — moving the upper jaw forward and/or downward to better match the lower jaw.
  • Distraction osteogenesis of the midface (gradual forward pulling using an internal or external device, analogous to limb-lengthening) has been described as an alternative that can achieve larger corrections with less relapse than single-stage surgery.
  • Timing is after jaw growth is complete and after orthodontic preparation is in place (braces are worn before surgery to level and align the teeth within each arch).
  • Postoperative orthodontic refinement typically runs for 6–12 months after surgery.

Honest caveats: The published evidence base for orthognathic surgery specifically in achondroplasia is limited to case reports and small series — the condition's rarity means no randomized trials exist. Outcomes are generally described as good for patients who have realistic expectations and are treated at experienced centers. The decision belongs in a joint conversation between a maxillofacial surgeon, an orthodontist, and the patient and family — with psychological support, since this is major elective facial surgery.

Anesthesia planning is mandatory. The achondroplasia airway (midface hypoplasia, limited neck extension, small nasal passages) makes any oral or maxillofacial procedure involving general anesthesia higher-risk. Every procedure requiring sedation or general anesthesia — including routine dental procedures under GA for young children — must be done at a center where the anesthesiology team is familiar with the skeletal dysplasia airway. See the anesthesia checklist on the Warning Signs page.

What to tell your dentist — at every appointment

Most general dentists will have treated no more than one or two patients with achondroplasia in their careers. A short, matter-of-fact briefing at the first appointment sets up every subsequent visit. The key points to convey:

  • "My child has achondroplasia — a skeletal dysplasia that affects jaw and facial bone growth. The midface is smaller than typical, which means crowding and a misaligned bite are expected, not unusual for this population."
  • Anesthesia alert: if any procedure might require sedation or general anesthesia — including dental work under GA, which is common in young children who cannot cooperate — the anesthesiologist must be briefed about the difficult airway (midface hypoplasia, small nasal passages, limited neck extension). See the emergency card on the Warning Signs page. Written documentation helps.
  • Sleep apnea history: whether it has been diagnosed and treated, because it affects mouth breathing, saliva flow, and orthodontic planning.
  • Hearing history and ear tubes: inform the dentist if the child has had recurrent ear infections or grommets, as children with chronic ear disease sometimes have mouth breathing and may be more anxious or have more sensory sensitivities.
  • Current medications: if the child or adult is on vosoritide or navepegritide, there are no known interactions with local anesthetics or common dental drugs — but mention it and let the dentist document it.
Practical tip: request referral to a pediatric dentist experienced with special needs

Pediatric dentists (paediatric dental specialists) — particularly those who practice in hospital or academic settings — see more patients with craniofacial and skeletal conditions and will be more familiar with achondroplasia than a typical community dentist. Ask your skeletal dysplasia clinic for a dental referral when the first tooth appears, before dental problems develop. The community organizations (LPA, Beyond Achondroplasia) often maintain informal referral lists built from families' own experience.

Preventive care: the calendar

AgeWhat should happen
By age 1 (or within 6 months of first tooth) First dental visit — standard AAP/AAPD guidance, no different in achondroplasia. Purpose: establish a dental home, fluoride varnish application, parental guidance on bottle use and cleaning. Tell the dentist about the diagnosis and anesthesia considerations.
Every 6 months (ages 1–18) Routine dental examination, professional cleaning, fluoride varnish, caries risk assessment. More frequent if high caries risk (mouth breathing, poor salivary flow, past cavities).
Ages 6–8 Orthodontic screening with dental panoramic X-ray (OPG) and bite-wing X-rays. Identifies supernumerary teeth, retained baby teeth, position of unerupted permanent teeth, and severity of crowding. Determines whether Phase 1 orthodontic treatment is needed.
Ages 10–12 (as permanent teeth emerge) Formal orthodontic consultation with lateral cephalometric X-ray to assess jaw relationship and plan comprehensive treatment. Monitor whether the Class III discrepancy is mild (orthodontics-only candidate) or severe (future jaw surgery candidate).
Ages 15–18 Re-assess jaw growth completion. Begin comprehensive Phase 2 orthodontic treatment when growth is stable. Discuss jaw surgery if the bite discrepancy cannot be managed orthodontically.
Adulthood Routine 6-monthly dental care continues. Adults who have completed orthodontic treatment should wear retainers long-term to prevent relapse. Adults with untreated or residual malocclusion should be offered referral to a maxillofacial orthodontic unit. Dry-mouth management (saliva substitutes, fluoride toothpaste, good hydration) if sleep apnea is untreated.

Daily oral hygiene — adapted for small hands and hypermobile fingers

Children with achondroplasia have shorter fingers and hypermobile finger joints, which can make the fine motor task of toothbrushing harder. A few practical adaptations:

  • Electric (powered) toothbrushes reduce the manual dexterity required and are as effective as, or better than, manual brushing — particularly for children who struggle with the coordination of circular brushing. Start with a soft, child-sized electric brush around age 3.
  • Build-up the brush handle with foam tubing (available from occupational therapy suppliers) if grip is a problem for older children and adults.
  • Water flossers (Waterpik-type devices) are an effective alternative to string floss for people with limited dexterity; they are particularly useful in crowded dentitions and around fixed orthodontic appliances.
  • High-fluoride toothpaste (1450 ppm fluoride, the standard UK "family" paste) is appropriate for children from age 3 onward per current evidence; the dentist will advise whether a prescription-strength fluoride is warranted for high-risk patients.
  • Parental involvement in tooth cleaning should continue until the child can reliably clean their own teeth — in achondroplasia, fine motor development follows its own timetable; some children need parental assistance for tooth cleaning longer than same-age peers.

Dental considerations in adulthood

Adults who did not receive orthodontic treatment in childhood — or who had treatment but experienced relapse — may present with significant malocclusion that affects chewing efficiency, jaw joint (TMJ) comfort, and facial appearance. Options include:

  • Removable clear aligners (e.g., Invisalign) for mild to moderate crowding and bite issues in adults — particularly useful for adults who prefer not to wear visible fixed braces.
  • Fixed adult orthodontics for moderate-to-severe cases, combined with maxillofacial surgical consultation if jaw surgery is also indicated.
  • Prosthodontic restoration (crowns, bridges, implants) for worn or damaged teeth from long-term malocclusion.
  • TMJ assessment if there is jaw clicking, pain, limited opening, or headaches — chronic malocclusion can load the jaw joint asymmetrically.
  • Dry mouth management: adults with untreated obstructive sleep apnea may have significant xerostomia (dry mouth), increasing cavity risk. In addition to addressing the apnea itself, saliva substitutes, prescription-fluoride toothpaste, regular fluoride varnish applications, and dietary counseling (reducing sugary snacks) all help.

Dental procedures and anesthesia

Many young children — and some adults — have dental treatment done under general anesthesia or intravenous sedation. In achondroplasia this is higher-risk than in the general population and must be planned carefully:

  • Midface hypoplasia means bag-mask ventilation is often difficult (the mask does not seal well over a flat nasal bridge).
  • The nasal passages are narrow — nasal intubation, which is preferred in many oral surgical procedures because it keeps the oral cavity clear, may require a smaller tube than expected.
  • Neck extension for intubation must be minimized because of the narrow foramen magnum.
  • Where possible, maxillofacial procedures requiring GA should be done at a hospital center (not a standalone community dental clinic) with a pediatric or adult anesthesiologist who has experience with craniofacial or skeletal dysplasia cases.
  • The pre-surgery anesthesia checklist on the Warning Signs page applies fully to dental procedures under GA.
Caution: nitrous oxide (laughing gas) and conscious sedation

Nitrous oxide (used for anxious dental patients) is generally considered safe in achondroplasia, but it reduces protective airway reflexes. The treating team should know the diagnosis and have resuscitation equipment accessible. Oral sedation (midazolam), IV sedation, and general anesthesia all invoke the full airway precautions described on the Warning Signs page. Always inform the treating dentist and any sedationist of the diagnosis before the appointment — not at the appointment.
