The Medical Library

The annotated guide to every major achondroplasia guideline, paper, chart, and clinic — so you and your doctors can go straight to the primary sources.

Here is a truth worth saying plainly: because achondroplasia occurs in about 1 in 21,000 to 1 in 28,000 births (~4.7 per 100,000; older sources quote 1 in 15,000–40,000), your local pediatrician or family doctor may never have cared for a patient with it before. That is not a reason to panic — it is a reason to bring the guidelines with you. Every document on this page is written by clinicians who see achondroplasia every week, most are free to read in full, and all of them are meant to be handed to the doctor in front of you. Families who do this routinely report smoother care, and a 2025 practice-gap study confirmed why it matters: real-world care for infants and young children still often falls short of what the guidelines recommend, so informed families frequently have to be the ones who ask for the recommended screening.

This page tells you, for each document: what it is, who wrote it, what it covers, and its most important recommendations in plain English — plus a free link wherever one exists. For the practical "what to do at each age" version of all this, see the age-by-age care checklist; for the symptoms that should never wait, see warning signs & emergencies.

How to use this library

If you only read two documents

Make them the AAP Health Supervision report (2020) — the age-by-age checklist American pediatricians follow — and the International Consensus Statement (2022) — the worldwide "rulebook" for lifelong care, with 160 numbered recommendations. Everything else here adds depth on one topic: growth charts, the spine, sleep, anesthesia, pregnancy, or the new medicines.

A few habits that make primary sources useful rather than overwhelming:

  • Print or save the PDF of the AAP report and bring it to well-child visits. Its Table 1 is literally a schedule your pediatrician can follow.
  • Note the year. Medicine moves. A 2010 document is still excellent on positioning and handling but says nothing about vosoritide (approved 2021) or navepegritide (approved 2026). We flag the age of every source below.
  • Recommendations have numbers. The International Consensus Statement numbers its 160 recommendations; quoting "Recommendation 36" to a specialist is a perfectly normal thing to do and tends to get taken seriously.
  • Look up unfamiliar words in the glossary — the papers use terms like "cervicomedullary" and "polysomnography" that this site translates.
Emergencies live on another page

This is a reference page. The one-page list of symptoms that need urgent care — in infants, children, and adults — is at Warning Signs & Emergencies. If you are worried about a symptom right now, go there first.

The core guidelines

These are the documents that define the standard of care. Between them they cover every age and every organ system that achondroplasia touches.

1. AAP Clinical Report: "Health Supervision for People With Achondroplasia" (2020)

Citation: Hoover-Fong J, Scott CI, Jones MC; AAP Committee on Genetics. Health Supervision for People With Achondroplasia. Pediatrics. 2020;145(6):e20201010.
Free full text: publications.aap.org · PDF mirror

Who wrote it: The American Academy of Pediatrics Committee on Genetics; the lead author, Dr. Julie Hoover-Fong, directs the Greenberg Center for Skeletal Dysplasias at Johns Hopkins. This is the second revision of the AAP's original 1995 guidance (the first revision was 2005 — see entry 6).

What it covers: The age-by-age monitoring "schedule" from the prenatal visit through adulthood — what the pediatrician should check, image, and discuss at every stage. If your child's doctor reads one document, this is it.

Its age-banded schedule, in brief (the full working version is on our care checklist):

Age bandWhat the AAP schedule asks for
PrenatalConfirm the diagnosis (ultrasound features are rarely visible before roughly the mid-20s of gestation; FGFR3 testing via CVS or amniocentesis); genetic counseling; if the mother has achondroplasia, plan a caesarean delivery and a pre-delivery anesthesia consult; deliver where pediatric services exist.
Birth–1 monthConfirm diagnosis; plot length, weight, and head circumference on achondroplasia-specific charts at every visit; assess the craniocervical junction in every infant — careful neurologic exam, an overnight sleep study (polysomnography), and neuroimaging (MRI preferred); car seat with a firm back, never unattended car-seat sleep, no swings or slings with uncontrolled head movement.
1 month–1 yearGrowth and neurologic exam every visit; kyphosis prevention (back support, no unsupported or curled "C" sitting in the first year); formal hearing test by 9–12 months, then annually; expect motor delay with normal social and cognitive development; early-intervention services.
1–5 yearsGrowth and BMI on achondroplasia charts; watch the kyphosis resolve with walking; monitor leg bowing (refer if a thrust at the knee, trouble walking, or pain); hearing test yearly; speech evaluation no later than age 2; screen for obstructive sleep apnea; adapt the home.
5–13 yearsWeight control becomes a major theme; annual hearing checks; orthodontic and speech review; check deep tendon reflexes yearly for early spinal stenosis signs; avoid trampolines, gymnastics, and collision sports; swimming and biking encouraged; school adaptations.
13–21 yearsWeight, neuro-oriented exam, sleep apnea assessment if symptomatic, annual hearing tests; contraception and recurrence-risk counseling (people with achondroplasia are usually fertile); driving with pedal extenders; transition to adult health care.
AdultsWeight management, spinal stenosis surveillance, sleep apnea, hearing, and pain assessment — covered in more depth by the International Consensus Statement (next entry) and our Adults & Aging page.

Other key content: average adult height of roughly 120–135 cm (about 4 to 4½ feet); the most common adult complication is lumbar spinal stenosis, usually treatable surgically and best caught early; growth hormone is not effective in achondroplasia; unexpected infant death occurs in 2–5% of infants if aggressive early craniocervical assessment is not pursued — which is exactly why the newborn workup matters and why this document exists. It states clearly that intelligence is normal and most people lead independent, productive lives.

2. International Consensus Statement on lifelong care (2022)

Citation: Savarirayan R, Ireland P, Irving M, et al. International Consensus Statement on the diagnosis, multidisciplinary management and lifelong care of individuals with achondroplasia. Nature Reviews Endocrinology, 2022 (published online 26 Nov 2021). doi:10.1038/s41574-021-00595-x.
Full text: nature.com · Free plain-language overview written with patient advocates: Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases, 2026 — excellent family reading.

Who wrote it: 55 experts from 16 countries on 5 continents, spanning 15 specialties, together with patient advocates, using a formal (modified Delphi) consensus process. The result is 160 numbered consensus recommendations, each of which required at least 80% agreement. (The paper states that the initial meeting's logistics were sponsored by BioMarin, which had no influence or input on content.)

What it covers: the entire life course, prenatal diagnosis through old age. It is the closest thing achondroplasia has to a global rulebook. Highlights families most often need:

  • Immediate referral of every newly diagnosed infant to a skeletal dysplasia reference centre, with lifelong multidisciplinary care and visits at least every 2–4 months in the first two years (Recs 24–25).
  • MRI screening of the foramen magnum — the opening at the base of the skull — in the first months of life for all infants, even those with no symptoms, plus regular neurological checks (Recs 34–36); decompression surgery only for symptomatic compression, by an experienced neurosurgical team (Recs 40–41).
  • A sleep study (polysomnography) during the first year for all infants, and parent education on sleep-apnea warning signs (Recs 30, 98).
  • Hearing checked early in infancy and annually through early childhood; ear tubes (grommets) when middle-ear fluid persists 3 months or more with documented hearing loss (Recs 31, 53, 109).
  • Kyphosis and legs: a specialist consult if the infant spinal hump has not resolved within a year of walking; bracing is not indicated for bowed legs, and surgery is reserved for pain, instability, or gait problems (Recs 60, 90–94).
  • Adults: back pain plus neurological symptoms means a whole-spine MRI; blood pressure checked with a properly sized cuff; routine cancer screening as for everyone else (no increased malignancy risk); pain and mental health assessed at every check-up (Recs 74–82).
  • Pregnancy in women with achondroplasia: pre-conception evaluation, obstetrician-led care, an anesthesia consultation during pregnancy, and — the key rule — any delivery from about 32 weeks onward should be by planned caesarean section at a hospital with critical-care facilities (Recs 11–23).
  • Psychosocial care is medicine too: more than 80% of people with achondroplasia are born to average-height families with no prior experience of the condition; the panel's consensus is that children with achondroplasia can thrive and become adults with independent and fulfilling lives, and that meetings with other families benefit most children (Recs 128–136 — see our Wellbeing & Community page).

3. European Achondroplasia Forum (EAF) publications (2021–2025)

Who they are: an independent network of senior European and Middle-East clinicians plus patient organizations. Their papers are shorter, highly practical, and all free to read. Four to know:

  • Principles of management (2021). Cormier-Daire V, et al. Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases, 2021. Free full text. Six guiding principles, each with at least 91% agreement: achondroplasia is a lifelong condition needing lifelong multidisciplinary management with close monitoring in the first 2 years; immediate referral to an experienced physician once suspected; shared decision-making with the person and family; care aims to anticipate and treat problems and support healthy lifestyle, self-esteem, and autonomy; access to adaptive measures and approved treatments; continued adolescent and adult monitoring. Sobering numbers it cites: life-threatening events in 3.8% of infants in the first year, and pain affecting up to 74.5% of adults — and under-treated.
  • Diagnosis & referral best practice (2022). Cormier-Daire V, et al. Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases, 2022. Free full text. How to get an accurate, early diagnosis (prenatal and postnatal) and a fast referral to an expert centre.
  • Foramen magnum stenosis guiding principles (2023). Irving M, et al. Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases, 2023. Free full text. The most concrete infant-screening protocol in print: clinical checks every 3–4 months from birth to age 1, then every 3–6 months to age 3; one routine screening MRI at 3–6 months of age; urgent neurosurgical referral for red flags (overactive reflexes, sustained ankle clonus, asymmetric reflexes, rapid head growth, developmental delay, or central apnea on a sleep study); decompression decided by an achondroplasia-experienced neurosurgeon on combined clinical, neurological, and imaging grounds — jointly with the family.
  • Sleep-disordered breathing guiding principles (2025). Fauroux B, et al. Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases, 2025. Free full text. The first dedicated lifelong sleep guideline: obstructive sleep apnea affects roughly 56–60% of infants and children with achondroplasia (versus 2–6% of children generally); the in-lab attended sleep study is the gold standard; first-line treatment in children is removal of the adenoids and tonsils (adenotonsillectomy), with a follow-up sleep study 2–4 months after surgery (about 18% have residual apnea); CPAP is usable at any age; central apnea from brainstem compression is treated with decompression surgery, not airway surgery.

The EAF also published a patient-held adult health checklist (2024) to help adults and their primary-care doctors track spinal stenosis symptoms, sleep apnea, hearing, weight, and pain — a good companion to our Adults & Aging page.

4. GeneReviews: "Achondroplasia" (continuously updated; last revised April 2026)

Citation: Legare JM. Achondroplasia. In: GeneReviews. University of Washington, Seattle; first posted 1998, last revised April 2026.
Free full text: ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK1152

What it is: the standard genetics reference chapter — diagnosis, inheritance, natural history, and surveillance tables — and, unusually for medical literature, it is kept up to date, which makes it the best single place to check current facts. Numbers worth knowing from it:

  • The FGFR3 gene variant p.Gly380Arg accounts for ~99% of cases; about 80% of children are born to average-height parents via a new (de novo) mutation. More on the genetics on our Understanding It page.
  • Average adult height: about 130 cm for men and 122 cm for women.
  • Lifetime complication frequencies: hydrocephalus needing treatment in under 5%; infant kyphosis in 90–95% (resolving in most); ear tubes in over half; hearing loss that affects daily life in ~40%; 25–40% of children undergo adenotonsillectomy, and counting all pharyngeal surgeries over a lifetime, around 45% have at least one; symptomatic spinal stenosis approaches a >90% cumulative risk of needing surgery by age 80, most often becoming symptomatic from the 20s–40s onward.
  • The number that explains all the infant screening: estimated infant mortality risk of ~7.5% without modern craniocervical evaluation, reduced to ~0.3% with it.
  • Its treatment section now covers both approved growth-modifying drugs — see our Treatments page for the full story.

5. Westmead Therapy Guidelines, ages 0–5 (2010)

Citation: Achondroplasia (0–5 years of age): Therapy Guidelines. The Children's Hospital at Westmead (Sydney, Australia), Physiotherapy Department, practice guideline no. 0/C/10:8047-01-00, December 2010.
Free PDF: schn.health.nsw.gov.au

Who wrote it: the physiotherapy and occupational-therapy team of Westmead's Connective Tissue Dysplasia Clinic — allied-health professionals who handle babies with achondroplasia daily. What makes it unique: no other document is this detailed on the practical, hands-on questions new parents actually have — how to hold, carry, seat, feed, and play with a baby with achondroplasia, and how to set up a preschool classroom. It is the backbone of our First Years and Everyday Life pages.

Its most useful content:

  • Kyphosis prevention rules: don't encourage independent sitting until the back is strong enough; build trunk strength with tummy time; firm back support in every seat (car capsule, stroller, high chair, bath seat), reclined at first and gradually brought upright; supported sitting starting around 2–3 months, initially limited to 10–20 minutes at a time; no carrying the baby curled up for more than a few minutes; baby slings, pouches, bouncers, rockers, jumpers, walkers, and swings are not recommended.
  • Neck protection: everyone who handles the baby supports the head and neck; never lift by the arms or pull to sit with an unsupported head; avoid neck hyperextension in tummy time.
  • Achondroplasia-specific motor milestones — the "within normal limits for achondroplasia" numbers that keep families off the standard charts (which will always look "delayed" and cause needless alarm): head up in tummy time by 5 months; rolling by 7 months; pulling to stand by 16 months; getting into sitting alone by 17 months; standing alone by 20 months; walking independently by 23 months. Commando crawling, "snow-ploughing," and bear-walking are common, normal, and safe — classic hands-and-knees crawling is actually uncommon.
  • Speech and feeding norms: first words by 8–15 months; two-word phrases by 20–31 months (language is typically on time or only mildly delayed; about 13% of toddlers are "late talkers"); self-feeding with a spoon by 15–27 months. It flags that trouble swallowing or coordinating the mouth can be a sign of high spinal-cord compression — a reminder echoed on our warning-signs page.
  • Joints: the limited elbow extension is bony — do not splint, cast, or stretch it, and don't lift the child by the arms.
  • School preparation: ground-floor classrooms, reachable hooks and taps, steps at basins and toilets, lowered door handles (important for emergency exits), seating that keeps the child at peer eye-level with foot support, extra rest breaks on long walks — and modify PE rather than excluding the child from it.
  • Its review rhythm: therapy review every 4 months in the first year, every 6 months to age 3, then yearly until school.
Mind the date

Westmead is from 2010 — it predates vosoritide, navepegritide, the 2020 AAP report, and routine infant MRI screening. Use it for what it is uniquely good at (positioning, handling, development, school setup), and use the AAP 2020 and International Consensus documents for the medical surveillance schedule.

6. AAP Health Supervision report (2005) — the predecessor

Citation: Trotter TL, Hall JG; AAP Committee on Genetics. Health supervision for children with achondroplasia. Pediatrics. 2005;116:771–783.

Why it's here: historical context only. It was the standard for fifteen years and is still cited in older materials your doctors may have on file. It has been fully superseded by the 2020 revision (entry 1) — if a clinician quotes the 2005 version, gently point them to the update.

Growth charts & measurement tools

This matters more than it sounds: a child with achondroplasia plotted on ordinary CDC or WHO growth charts will always look "abnormal" — short for age, heavy for height, large-headed — which causes both false alarms (needless obesity or hydrocephalus worries) and missed problems (a genuinely concerning trend hidden inside "off the chart"). Every guideline agrees: plot on achondroplasia-specific charts, at every visit, from birth. Bring printed charts to appointments if your clinic's electronic system doesn't have them built in.

Developmental screening tools

Standard developmental screeners are the wrong ruler here. The Westmead therapy guidelines state it outright: standardized developmental assessments should not be used for children with achondroplasia — they will flag motor "delay" that is entirely expected and safe. The International Consensus Statement (Rec 27) likewise requires achondroplasia-specific developmental screening tools. In practice that means:

  • The Westmead achondroplasia-specific milestone norms (entry 5 above) for gross motor, fine motor, speech, and feeding, ages 0–5 — built partly on the Australian developmental research of Ireland and colleagues (2007) and the motor-development work of Fowler and colleagues (1997).
  • The AAP 2020 report's guidance to use achondroplasia-specific milestone expectations — for example, independent walking is commonly expected by around age 2 to 2½.

Both are digested age-by-age on our First Years and Childhood pages.

Landmark papers by topic

These are the studies your specialists are thinking of when they make recommendations. Each entry: what it found, in one or two sentences.

Natural history, mortality & the encouraging trend

Read these in order and a clear story emerges: historical cohorts showed roughly a 10-year reduction in average life expectancy, driven largely by causes that are now preventable — infant brainstem compression, untreated sleep apnea, and cardiovascular disease — and more recent cohorts show clear improvement with modern proactive care.

  • Hecht et al., American Journal of Human Genetics, 1987 (701 people, 5,828 person-years). Found mortality raised at all ages, and that in children under 4, brainstem compression caused half of the sudden deaths — the paper that launched universal infant craniocervical screening.
  • Wynn et al., American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A, 2007 (793 people, 42-year follow-up). Confirmed the historical ~10-year average reduction in life expectancy and flagged heart-disease deaths at ages 25–35 running more than 10× the general population — the reason adult cardiovascular care now gets so much attention.
  • Simmons et al., Birth Defects Research Part A, 2014 (Texas registry, 106 births 1996–2003). All four deaths in the cohort occurred in the first year of life — infancy remains the highest-risk window, which is why the first-year screening schedule is non-negotiable.
  • Hashmi et al., American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A, 2018 (the CLARITY mortality study: 855 patients, 4 centers, 12,117 person-years since 1986). Only 12 deaths — far fewer sudden infant deaths than the 1980s data. Screening works. The new pattern to counsel about: cardiovascular deaths in adults and motor-vehicle accidents in ages 5–24 (see vehicle adaptations on Everyday Life).
  • Hoover-Fong et al., CLARITY natural history study, Genetics in Medicine, 2021 (>1,300 US patients across 60 years, four centers). The database that anchors modern complication and surgery rates — when you see a percentage on this site, there's a good chance it traces here.
  • LIAISE, Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases, 2023 (186 patients, 13 European sites). Lifetime-burden study: complications follow a U-shape (infancy and over-40s); 37.9% of adults report moderate-to-extreme pain — adult follow-up must not lapse.

Foramen magnum & decompression surgery

  • White et al., Skeletal Dysplasia Management Consortium, American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A, 2016. Eleven experts, 22 consensus recommendations on evaluating and treating foramen magnum stenosis in infancy — the screening exam, when to use sleep studies and MRI, how to tell harmless big ventricles (ventriculomegaly) from true hydrocephalus, and when decompression surgery is indicated. This framed the "screen every infant" standard the AAP adopted.
  • EAF foramen magnum principles, 2023 — see the core-guidelines section above: one routine MRI at 3–6 months; exams every 3–4 months in year one.
  • ESPR/ESNR opinion paper, Pediatric Radiology, 2024. European radiology societies' protocol advice: fast MRI sequences, sedation-sparing techniques, and what exactly to measure — useful to hand to your local radiology department.
  • Danielpour et al., Journal of Neurosurgery, 2007 and Mukherjee et al., Journal of Neurosurgery: Pediatrics, 2014. Showed that flexion–extension ("dynamic") MRI can reveal cord compression and spinal-fluid-flow changes invisible on a standard scan — better identifying who actually needs surgery.

The screening debate, honestly stated: all modern guidelines agree every infant needs craniocervical assessment — exam, sleep study, and imaging. They differ slightly on universal MRI: the International Consensus and the EAF recommend a routine MRI for all infants in the first months, while the AAP calls for neuroimaging in all as part of the assessment (MRI preferred) with urgency guided by the exam and sleep-study findings. How many infants end up needing decompression surgery? Roughly 7–13% of infants in the first 2 years at experienced centers; around 20% lifetime in one large US cohort, with wide variation between centers (5–43%). Both over-treatment and under-treatment are real risks — which is why every document repeats the phrase "experienced center."

Sleep & breathing

  • EAF sleep-disordered breathing principles, 2025 (core-guidelines section above) — the most detailed and current document on the topic.
  • AAP 2020: an overnight sleep study, with end-tidal CO₂ monitoring and read by a pediatric pulmonologist, in the newborn period or first months for every infant; repeat whenever apnea symptoms appear at any age.
  • International Consensus 2022: a sleep study within the first year for all infants — no later than age 2 in any case (Recs 30, 98); notably, a normal sleep study should not by itself rule out decompression when clinical and MRI criteria for compression are met (Rec 99).
  • The background data: obstructive sleep apnea affects roughly half or more of children (studies range from about 50% to 80%) and around 60% of adults; central apnea is less common than families fear, but when present it is a red flag for brainstem compression rather than an airway problem.

Anesthesia & surgery safety

  • White et al., American Journal of Medical Genetics Part A, 2017. The Skeletal Dysplasia Management Consortium's peri-operative best practices: pre-operative lung evaluation and sleep study, cardiac and neurological evaluation, cervical-spine imaging before anesthesia, and planning for a difficult airway at intubation and extubation.
  • AAP 2020 anesthesia checklist: extreme care manipulating the neck (uncontrolled extension during intubation can injure the spinal cord at the narrow foramen magnum); dose medications by size and weight, not age; expect difficult IV access; avoid spinal or epidural anesthesia in general unless imaging shows adequate canal space and there are no neurologic signs.
  • International Consensus (Recs 83, 115–117): anesthesia only by staff experienced with achondroplasia; minimize head and neck movement; have difficult-airway equipment and video laryngoscopes ready; thorough pre-assessment including snoring/OSA history.

Print one of these before any planned procedure — including dental surgery — and make sure the anesthesia team has seen it. More on this on the care checklist.

Pregnancy & delivery

  • Savarirayan, Rossiter, Hoover-Fong et al., American Journal of Obstetrics & Gynecology, 2018. Eleven-expert consensus with 26 recommendations on prenatal diagnosis, counseling, and delivery planning for skeletal dysplasias.
  • The core guidance for a pregnant woman with achondroplasia (2018 guidelines + International Consensus): pregnancy is usually possible and babies are usually healthy, but plan ahead — pre-conception review, obstetrician-led care, an anesthesia consult during the pregnancy, respiratory monitoring in the third trimester, and any delivery from about 32 weeks onward by planned caesarean section at a hospital with critical-care facilities (before 32 weeks, individualized expert assessment). Careful fluid management matters because maternal blood volume is smaller than average.
  • For an average-height mother carrying a baby with achondroplasia: vaginal delivery is often possible, though caesarean may be needed for the baby's head size (AAP 2020).

The growth-modifying drug trials

A reminder before the citations: this is educational background, not a treatment recommendation — decisions about starting, continuing, or stopping any medicine belong with your child's own specialist team. Our Treatments page walks through the options in plain English.

  • Savarirayan et al., The Lancet, 2020 — the pivotal vosoritide phase 3 trial: 121 children aged 5–18; daily vosoritide (15 µg/kg — the label now uses weight-band dosing, with younger and lighter children receiving a higher per-kg dose) increased growth velocity by +1.57 cm/year versus placebo, with generally mild side effects, mostly injection-site reactions.
  • Savarirayan et al., Genetics in Medicine, 2021 — the extension study: the growth effect persisted and remained stable at 2 years, and in later reports out to 5+ years. On final adult height, lead with the regulators' position: the effect on final height has not yet been established. Long-term extension data are encouraging, and you may see projections of eventual height gain (GeneReviews quotes modeled lifetime gains of roughly 26 cm in males and 22 cm in females if treatment starts at birth) — those are unvalidated model projections, not observed results.
  • Savarirayan et al., The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, 2024 — the infant and toddler randomized trial (ages 3–59 months, 75 children): height gains and reassuring safety in the youngest patients; the basis for the FDA's October 2023 expansion of vosoritide to children of all ages with open growth plates. A separate randomized study in infants at risk of needing decompression surgery is ongoing.
  • International vosoritide implementation consensus, Nature Reviews Endocrinology, 2025 — 64 statements on how to actually run treatment: start as early as possible after genetic confirmation; caregiver injection training; growth checks every 3–6 months by age; home blood-pressure monitoring not needed; stop when growth velocity falls below ~1.5 cm/year with growth-plate closure on X-ray, when height goals are met or clearly not being met, or for intolerance. The US prescribing label carries the official weight-band dosing tables.
  • Navepegritide (YUVIWEL): the phase 3 ApproaCH trial showed superiority over placebo in growth velocity at week 52 (+1.78 cm/year in ages 5 and up in pooled reporting) and improved leg alignment at 2 years; FDA accelerated approval came on 27 February 2026 for children aged 2+ with open growth plates, dosed once weekly. A clinical summary is at the Pediatric Endocrine Society.
  • Infigratinib (oral, in trials — not approved): the phase 3 PROPEL 3 trial reported positive topline results in February 2026 (roughly +2.1 cm/year height-velocity difference), and regulatory submissions are planned for the second half of 2026. It is not yet approved anywhere — topline press releases are not the same as peer-reviewed publication or approval.
  • Best single review integrating the new drugs into care: "Approach to the Patient with Achondroplasia," Journal of Clinical Endocrinology & Metabolism, 2025 (free at PMC).

Important context every trial paper agrees on: these medicines increase growth and may improve body proportions; whether they reduce the need for decompression or spine surgery is still being studied. None of them replaces the monitoring schedule in the core guidelines above.

Specialist clinics & how to get referred

Every major guideline says the same thing in its first few recommendations: care should be anchored at a skeletal dysplasia center — a clinic where geneticists, neurosurgeons, orthopedists, pulmonologists, and ENT specialists see achondroplasia regularly and work as one team. Your local pediatrician remains essential for everyday care; the specialist center sets the surveillance plan and handles the big decisions.

Getting referred — and second opinions from a distance

  • Ask directly. Any pediatrician or GP can refer to a genetics or skeletal dysplasia clinic; the EAF diagnosis-and-referral paper and International Consensus Rec 24 say referral should happen as soon as achondroplasia is suspected — you do not need to wait for problems. If you meet resistance, the phrase "the AAP and the International Consensus Statement both recommend referral to a skeletal dysplasia center at diagnosis" tends to work.
  • Distance is not a dealbreaker. Many families see the specialist center once or twice a year (or less, in adulthood) while everyday care stays local — the guidelines' visit rhythms (every 2–4 months in infancy, annually in childhood, every 3–5 years for adults per GeneReviews) can be split between local and specialist visits. Ask any center whether they offer telehealth appointments or will review records and imaging for a remote second opinion before you commit to travel — many do.
  • Patient organizations can help you find the right door: Little People of America maintains a medical advisory board and medical-resource FAQs; Beyond Achondroplasia maintains the center directory above. More on the community itself on our Wellbeing & Community page.

Staying current without getting lost

Achondroplasia research is moving faster now than at any point in history — two approved drugs since 2021, a third in late-stage trials, and new guidelines appearing almost yearly. Here is how to keep up without drowning or being misled.

Sources that update themselves

  • GeneReviews is revised continuously (most recently April 2026) — check it first when you wonder whether a fact is still current.
  • The CLARITY registry (the Achondroplasia Natural History Study — four US centers, more than 1,300 patients across 60 years) keeps producing the papers that update complication and surgery rates; searching PubMed for "CLARITY achondroplasia" surfaces the newest ones.
  • Reference sites for quick answers: MedlinePlus Genetics, NIH GARD, and the NORD report.

Searching PubMed sensibly

  • Search pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov for achondroplasia plus one topic word ("achondroplasia sleep apnea", "achondroplasia foramen magnum") rather than long phrases.
  • Use the filters on the left: "Free full text" shows only what you can read in full without paying, and the "Guideline" / "Practice Guideline" and "Randomized Controlled Trial" article-type filters separate the strongest evidence from the noise.
  • Sort by "Most recent" once a year and skim titles — that alone keeps most families current.
  • Abstracts are free even when full papers are not; and for papers with a "PMC" badge, the full text is free at PubMed Central.

Conferences and community channels

Little People of America's national conference brings its medical advisory board — many of the guideline authors above — into the same building as families, with medical workshops and clinics; it is many families' single best annual update (and the community side matters just as much — see Wellbeing & Community). In Europe, the patient organizations partnered with the European Achondroplasia Forum play a similar role, and the 2026 plain-language overview of the International Consensus Statement was co-written with patient advocates precisely to keep families in the loop.

A caution about preprints, press releases, and headlines

Drug-company "topline results" announcements (like the February 2026 infigratinib PROPEL 3 press release) and preprints have not been through peer review, and news stories routinely round "increased growth velocity in a trial" up to "cure." Before changing any expectation or decision, wait for the peer-reviewed paper and the regulator's decision — and bring the question to your specialist team, who will usually have seen the same headline. Our Treatments page is updated to reflect what is actually approved versus what is still in trials.
