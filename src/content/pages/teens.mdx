Start with the conclusion of the International Consensus Statement's expert panel — 55 specialists from 16 countries — which is worth keeping on the fridge: children with achondroplasia **can thrive and become adults with independent and fulfilling lives**. Intelligence is typical, puberty arrives on schedule, and the teenage jobs of building identity, autonomy, and relationships are the same as anyone else's. What is different is visibility (a teen with achondroplasia cannot blend into a crowd on a hard day), a handful of medical items that deserve regular checking, and a transition to adult health care that works far better when it is planned early rather than stumbled into.

The teen years have three quiet projects running underneath the everyday drama: **finishing growth safely**, **locking in the habits that protect adult health** (weight, activity, knowing the spine warning signs), and **transferring ownership of the condition** — its story, its vocabulary, its care — from parent to young person. Everything on this page serves one of those three.

## Puberty: on time and on track

Achondroplasia changes how bones grow (through the *FGFR3* gene — see [Understanding It](/understanding)), not how hormones work. So puberty itself is normal:

- **Timing is typical.** A 2025 European study (a conference abstract; puberty onset recorded in 60 adolescents) found puberty began at about **age 11.0 in girls and 12.8 in boys** — within the usual range. Sexual development, menstruation, and fertility follow the ordinary timetable, and menstrual patterns in women with achondroplasia match those of average-height women.
- **The growth spurt is smaller.** Because the growth plates respond abnormally to growth signals, the adolescent spurt adds less height than in average-height teens — but growth continues until the growth plates close at the usual age, at the end of puberty.
- **Check-ups stay roughly yearly.** The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) keeps adolescents in routine health supervision — with formal hearing assessment ideally every year — and GeneReviews advises review at least annually through the growing years, with attention to weight, spine symptoms, hearing, sleep, and psychosocial wellbeing — more on each below.
- **Orthodontics is common.** Dental crowding related to a smaller midface often needs orthodontic care in the teen years; regular dental review is part of the routine (International Consensus Statement, Rec 65).

<Callout type="info" title="Good to know">
  If your teen is asking "when will I stop growing?" — the honest answer is: at the same age as their classmates, when the growth plates close at the end of puberty. That timing also matters for treatment decisions, because growth-plate medications only work while the plates are open (see [below](#treatment-decisions-that-surface-in-the-teen-years) and [Treatments](/treatments)).
</Callout>

## Final height: what to expect

Published averages for final adult height vary slightly by cohort, but all fall in the range of roughly **4'0" to 4'5" (122–132 cm)**:

| Source                               | Men                          | Women                       |
| ------------------------------------ | ---------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| GeneReviews (US growth curves)       | 129.9 ± 6.25 cm (about 4'3") | 122.4 ± 5.9 cm (about 4'0") |
| Merker et al. 2018 (European cohort) | about 132 cm                 | about 124 cm                |
| MedlinePlus (NIH)                    | 131 cm (4'4")                | 124 cm (4'1")               |

Two details help make sense of the numbers. First, the shortening is mostly in the upper arms and thighs — the pattern doctors call **rhizomelic**, or "root-limb," shortening (see the [glossary](/glossary)) — while the trunk is near-average length. That is why a teen with achondroplasia often sits at typical height at a desk or dinner table even though standing height is not typical. Second, these are averages with real spread: individual adults land above and below them, and achondroplasia-specific growth charts (such as the European charts at [achondroplasia-growthcharts.com](https://www.achondroplasia-growthcharts.com/)) are the right way to track where a particular teen is heading — standard charts tell you nothing useful.

Some adolescents are still within the treatment window for a **growth-plate medicine** — daily vosoritide (Voxzogo) or, in the US since February 2026, weekly navepegritide (Yuviwel); an oral option, infigratinib, is awaiting an FDA decision. Once the plates close, none of them can work. Details are [below](#treatment-decisions-that-surface-in-the-teen-years). See [Treatments](/treatments) for the full, balanced picture, including what regulators say about final height.

## Identity, body image, and mental health

First, the honest frame: many teens with achondroplasia develop strong self-confidence, particularly with supportive adults and community connection, and their social and emotional functioning is often within or near the typical range. But adolescence also raises the volume on visibility — being stared at, photographed without consent, asked intrusive questions, or treated as younger than they are — and the research says families should take mental health seriously rather than assume it will sort itself out.

### What the studies actually show

- Studies of adults with achondroplasia report **lower average self-esteem and higher rates of anxiety and depression** than the general population. In a small US quality-of-life study (25 adults), 56% had a diagnosed psychiatric illness — roughly **three times the general-population rate**. A larger 2025 study in the US, UK, and Norway found a past psychiatric diagnosis in 31% of adults with achondroplasia or hypochondroplasia, and current depression and anxiety symptoms well above general-population rates. The exact figures vary, but the direction is consistent across studies.
- GeneReviews (2026) recommends discussing **social adjustment at every visit**, and the International Consensus Statement recommends psychological support be offered routinely — so if your teen's doctor asks about mood, that is guideline-driven good practice, not an alarm.
- Here is the genuinely hopeful finding, from the landmark Gollust study (2003): after controlling for demographics, **lower self-esteem and perceiving achondroplasia as more serious were more strongly linked to lower quality of life than the condition itself**. Self-esteem is something families, schools, and communities can actively build — height is not the lever; confidence is.

### What anxiety and depression look like in a teen

Anxiety can show up as fear or worry, but also irritability, sleep trouble, headaches, and stomachaches (including dread before medical visits). Depression can look like persistent sadness, loss of interest in things they used to enjoy, appetite or sleep changes, and negative self-talk. Ordinary teenage moodiness passes; these patterns persist.

### When to seek therapy

Seek a professional — ideally one experienced with disability or visible difference — when symptoms last for weeks, interfere with school, sleep, or relationships, or involve any thoughts of self-harm. There is no threshold of "sick enough": the International Consensus Statement recommends psychological support be offered and signposted **across the lifespan** as routine care (Recs 129–130), not reserved for crises.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Mental health emergency — act now">
  Talk of suicide or self-harm, giving away possessions, sudden withdrawal, or saying others "would be better off without me" requires immediate action. In the US or Canada, call or text **988**; in the UK or Ireland, Samaritans **116 123**; in Australia, Lifeline **13 11 14**; elsewhere, your local emergency number. Do not wait for a scheduled appointment. More on urgent situations: [Warning Signs](/warning-signs).
</Callout>

### The single most useful move: meeting other little people

Adults with achondroplasia repeatedly describe peer connection as the most formative psychosocial resource of their youth — seeing successful adults built like them, often for the first time. The consensus panel agrees: resilience and self-esteem are best fostered *together with peers and advocacy groups* (Rec 130). Concretely, for teens:

- **Little People of America (LPA)** runs teen programming at its week-long national conference — 2,000–3,000 people with dwarfism and their families, with workshops, medical clinics, sports, and dances — plus about 70 local chapters. First-Time Conference Attendee Grants lower the cost barrier.
- **Dwarf sports organizations** — the Dwarf Athletic Association of America (DAAA) in the US and the Dwarf Sports Association UK — offer competitive sport with peers (more under [sports](#sports-and-physical-activity-whats-safe)).
- Condition-aware **summer camps** and equivalents of LPA worldwide (Restricted Growth Association and Little People UK in Britain, Short Statured People of Australia, and many others) run their own youth events. See [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing) for the full directory.

There is no obligation — some teens engage online only, or not at all — but the near-universal report from first-time conference attendees is that it is transformative. A reasonable approach: try one regional event before deciding it isn't for you.

### Identity, language, and representation

Let your teen lead on terminology. "Little person" (LP), "person with dwarfism," "dwarf," and "person of short stature" are all accepted; most people simply prefer their name. The word **"midget" is a slur**, rooted in 19th-century freak-show exhibition. Many adults regard dwarfism as an identity and a culture, not a defect — LPA has been called one of the most important incubators of disability pride in the US, and October is Dwarfism Awareness Month. Representation now includes Emmy-winning actor Peter Dinklage, Paralympic champion Ellie Simmonds, writer and educator Sinéad Burke (the first little person on a *Vogue* cover), and young Australian actor and anti-bullying figure Quaden Bayles — role models many teens find genuinely useful. Your teen will also encounter a live debate inside the community between a medical frame and an identity frame around new growth treatments; both views are held by thriving adults, and [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing) unpacks it.

## Dating and relationships

Adults with achondroplasia date, marry, and raise families. Partner choice — another little person or an average-height partner — is personal, and both are common. The lower marriage rates reported in older research (such as the Gollust 2003 survey) reflect social barriers, not any lack of capacity for relationships — and confidence plus community access shift the odds. LPA events are, unabashedly, a place many members meet partners.

Dating as a visibly different teenager takes courage anywhere, and there is no script that removes the awkwardness of adolescent romance for anyone. What helps is the same thing that helps everywhere else on this page: solid self-esteem, real friendships, and peers who have been there — older teens and adults in the LP community talk frankly about dating in a way no parent or doctor can.

### Owning your genetic story

Part of becoming an adult with achondroplasia is knowing your own genetics — not because a teenager needs a reproductive plan, but because the knowledge is empowering and it belongs to them, not just to their parents. The AAP explicitly advises clinicians to make sure adolescents have *the vocabulary and the understanding of the genetic nature of achondroplasia*, and the International Consensus Statement recommends that discussion of inheritance and reproductive options be **offered from adolescence onward** (Recs 7 and 81). The facts a teen should own:

- Achondroplasia is autosomal dominant. With an average-height partner, each pregnancy has a **50% chance of achondroplasia and 50% chance of average stature**.
- If both partners have achondroplasia: 25% average stature, 50% achondroplasia, and **25% homozygous achondroplasia** (a double dose of the gene change), which is usually lethal in the newborn period. Genetic counseling exists precisely to walk through options here, and prenatal testing is available — details in [Understanding It](/understanding) and, for pregnancy itself, [Adults & Aging](/adults).
- About 80% of people with achondroplasia were born to average-height parents through a spontaneous new mutation — no one caused it.

## Independence: driving, tech, and doing it yourself

### Driving is absolutely achievable

The main adaptation is **pedal extenders**, which bring the accelerator and brake within reach — sometimes with a built-up floor so the feet have somewhere to rest between pedals — and let the driver sit further back from the wheel. Seat cushions or boosters improve sight lines over the wheel, and some drivers add a small-diameter steering wheel. The licensing process itself is the standard one for any new driver; the adaptive part works like this:

- A **certified driver rehabilitation specialist (CDRS)** can assess your teen behind the wheel and prescribe the right adaptations — find one via [aded.net](https://www.aded.net) in the US. Many US state vocational-rehabilitation agencies help pay for vehicle adaptations.
- **Airbag safety matters** because sitting close to the steering wheel raises airbag-injury concern. In the US, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) allows drivers of short stature to apply for an **airbag on-off switch** (NHTSA: 1-888-327-4236). Discuss it with the adaptive-driving specialist: pedal extenders fix leg reach, but short upper arms often still prevent keeping the recommended 25–30 cm (10–12 inches) between breastbone and steering wheel, which is why deactivation is commonly needed rather than a last resort.
- **Take the safety side seriously.** A 2018 US study found that about half of deaths in people with achondroplasia aged 5–24 were from accidents, including car crashes, and its authors advised clinicians to counsel on vehicle safety. A properly fitted, professionally assessed setup is part of that.
- Parent-built resources such as Beyond Achondroplasia's [car adaptations page](https://www.beyondachondroplasia.org/en/pages/44-resources/daily-life/76-car-adaptations) collect practical product experience from families.

### Tech is a leveller

A smartphone works exactly the same at any height — banking, shopping, ride-hailing, smart-home controls for lights and locks, navigation. For a generation of teens with achondroplasia, much of daily logistics has quietly moved into reach. That is not a substitute for an accessible physical environment, but it is real independence, cheaply won.

### The everyday toolkit

Step stools, reachers, lowered light switches, lever taps, adapted toilets, smaller keyboards — the same environmental adaptations that built independence in childhood keep paying off, and an occupational therapist can do home (and later workplace) assessments. The consensus statement recommends **regularly reassessing adaptive-equipment needs** through adolescence (Rec 70) because bodies, schools, and ambitions all change. The full practical catalogue lives at [Everyday Life](/daily-living).

## School: self-advocacy and accommodations

The big shift in secondary school is who does the advocating. In primary school, parents briefed the teacher; by the mid-teens, the goal is a young person who can name their condition, ask for what they need, and decline what they don't — a skill that will serve them at university, at work, and in every doctor's office for the rest of their life.

### Scripts that work

The AAP has long advised preparing children for the questions and curiosity of others, and by adolescence the scripts should be the teen's own. Short, factual, and delivered with a shrug beats a lecture: *"I have achondroplasia — my bones grow differently. I'm sixteen, same as you."* For requests: *"I need the lab stool from the storeroom for this bench,"* or *"Can we swap this locker for a lower one?"* Rehearsing these at home sounds artificial and works anyway — ready-made answers give the teen control of the interaction.

### The equipment dilemma — name it out loud

The consensus statement flags something families see constantly: **some teens avoid modified equipment at school because they fear teasing**. Take that fear seriously rather than overriding it. Talk it through: which aids genuinely earn their keep (a footrest that stops legs dangling all day, a stool that makes the science bench usable), which have lower-profile alternatives, and which the teen can reasonably skip. An aid that preserves joints and energy is worth a conversation; a teen who feels ownership of the decision will actually use the equipment. Ask their view at every review — the consensus recommends exactly that (Rec 70), alongside dedicated professional support for adjustment, coping strategies, and school and employment planning (Rec 73).

### Accommodations for exams, labs, and the building

- The classics still apply: manageable doors, reachable locks and handles, a regular-sized desk with a footrest (feet should never dangle), step stools where needed, independent restroom access.
- For laboratories, workshops, and practical exams: stable stools at benches, equipment within reach, and extra time where physical setup is part of the task — arrange these through the school's formal accommodation process, not ad hoc on exam day.
- In the US, achondroplasia is recognized as a disability under the ADA (a status LPA embraces), and Section 504 plans are the standard vehicle for school accommodations. Equivalent duties exist elsewhere — the UK Equality Act 2010, for example. Put requests and incidents in writing.

### If bullying happens

UK research (British Journal of Sociology of Education, 2022) found that school bullies directly recycle hostile cultural tropes about dwarfism — and that **friendship networks were the crucial protection**. So invest in the friendships, brief the school early, and use the machinery when needed: disability-based bullying can violate Section 504/ADA protections in the US, schools must respond, and documented incidents get acted on where verbal reports evaporate. Loop in a counselor early — the AAP's adolescent guidance explicitly includes a focus on bullying and psychological health. More strategies: [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing).

### Extracurriculars: yes

Drama, debate, music, coding, student government, and sport (next section) build exactly the confidence and friendship networks the research says are protective. The only activities needing a second thought are the physically high-risk ones below.

## Sports and physical activity: what's safe

Staying active is not optional in achondroplasia — the consensus statement recommends exercise **throughout the lifespan** to maintain strength, healthy body composition, and flexibility (Recs 56 and 72), and a small 2024 study (16 adults) found moderate physical activity went with better general health and vitality in adults with achondroplasia. Adolescence is where lifelong habits either stick or don't.

The constraint to respect is the spine and the craniocervical junction — the opening at the base of the skull (the foramen magnum) and the naturally narrow spinal canal. That anatomy is why certain impacts and loading patterns are treated with caution:

| Category                | Examples                                                                                                                       | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Encouraged**          | Swimming, cycling                                                                                                              | The two options the AAP specifically emphasizes — low-impact, joint-friendly, easy to continue for life.                                                                                                          |
| **Generally avoided**   | Collision sports, competitive gymnastics, trampolines                                                                          | Advised against by the AAP because of spinal risks — impacts and hyperflexion/hyperextension of the neck and back.                                                                                                |
| **Ask your team first** | Heavy weight training, martial arts, horse riding, high-impact running sports — anything that loads or jars the neck and spine | Published guidance doesn't rule on every sport individually. Get individual clearance from a clinician who knows your teen's spine and neck imaging before starting — the answer is often yes with modifications. |

And competitive doors are open: the **Dwarf Athletic Association of America (DAAA)** and **Dwarf Sports Association UK** run organized sport for short-statured athletes, feeding the World Dwarf Games and Paralympic sport — Ellie Simmonds won the first of her five Paralympic golds at age 13. For a teen, sport with LP peers does double duty: fitness and community at once.

## Health monitoring in the teen years

Adolescence is medically quieter than infancy — no more monthly head measurements — but it is emphatically not a time to drift out of specialist follow-up, because the habits and baselines set now shape adult health. GeneReviews advises review **at least annually** through the growing years, and the AAP keeps adolescents in routine health supervision (with hearing assessment ideally yearly) — ideally with a team experienced in skeletal dysplasia. Pain is common even at this age: in a 2026 interview study of 15 young people with achondroplasia aged 12–20, 83% reported pain and 77% had been diagnosed with sleep apnea (a small sample, but a useful reminder to ask). The 2026 UK consensus guidelines for children and young people also cover planned transfer to adult care. What each visit should cover (the [Care Checklist](/checklist) has the full age-by-age version):

| Check                           | Why it matters in adolescence                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Weight and eating habits        | The consensus calls obesity "a major problem" and this is often where it takes off — see below.                                                                                        |
| Spine and nerve symptoms        | Spinal stenosis symptoms can begin in the teen years; learn the red flags now (below).                                                                                                 |
| Pain — and its knock-on effects | The consensus (Rec 68) says monitor not just pain, but its effect on **mood, self-care, education, and leisure**. A teen whose grades or social life dip may be hurting, not slacking. |
| Sleep and snoring               | Obstructive sleep apnea affects 50–80% of children and about 60% of adults; it can recur after childhood treatment.                                                                    |
| Hearing                         | A lifetime of middle-ear trouble adds up; keep periodic audiology checks.                                                                                                              |
| Mood and social adjustment      | Ask about mood, friendships, and school at every visit (GeneReviews 2026); offer psychological support routinely.                                                                     |
| Dental/orthodontic review       | Crowding from midface differences frequently needs orthodontics now.                                                                                                                   |

### Weight: the teenage fork in the road

Excess weight gain in achondroplasia often starts in childhood and consolidates in adolescence, and it is the single most consequential modifiable risk: in adults it worsens spinal stenosis, joint pain, and sleep apnea, and drives cardiovascular risk. Older cohorts showed average life expectancy about 10 years shorter, partly from heart disease in young and middle-aged adults; childhood survival has since improved, but whether adult survival has is not yet known (see [Adults & Aging](/adults)). Blood pressure, sleep apnea, and weight are the parts of that risk you can act on. Two practical points from the consensus (Rec 71):

- **Standard BMI charts mislead in achondroplasia** (short limbs inflate BMI). Insist on achondroplasia-specific weight and BMI references — the Hoover-Fong US charts (to age 18) or the Merker European charts (to age 20). There are no adult equivalents, so in adulthood the focus shifts to weight trend and waist size ([Nutrition & Exercise](/nutrition#bmi-problem)).
- Healthy-eating education should **involve the whole family** — a teen cannot out-discipline a kitchen stocked against them.

### Spinal stenosis: learn the symptoms now, even though it's usually later

People with achondroplasia are born with a narrow spinal canal (the bony tunnel holding the spinal cord and nerves). With age and wear it can narrow enough to squeeze the nerves — **spinal stenosis**, the defining medical issue of adulthood. In the Norwegian population study, the median age of first symptoms was 33, but the range started at age 10 — **some people have symptoms as teenagers**. Because surgical outcomes are consistently better the sooner treatment follows symptom onset, the smartest thing a teenager can do is simply know the pattern:

- Aching, burning, numbness, tingling, or weakness in the **buttocks and legs**, brought on by walking or standing and — the classic clue — **relieved by sitting, squatting, or leaning forward** (doctors call this neurogenic claudication).
- Legs that feel heavy or give way; shrinking walking distance; persistent low-back pain with leg symptoms.
- Any such symptoms warrant referral to a spine service experienced with achondroplasia (Rec 69) — usually with MRI. Do not let anyone dismiss it as "growing pains."

<Callout type="emergency" title="Seek urgent care — same day — if…">
  New trouble controlling urine or bowels, numbness in the groin or saddle area, rapidly worsening leg weakness, or a foot that drags or drops. These can signal **cauda equina syndrome**, a surgical emergency where delay risks permanent damage. Full list: [Warning Signs](/warning-signs).
</Callout>

### Sleep apnea: re-check, don't assume

Many teens had adenoids and tonsils out in childhood — 25–40% of children with achondroplasia undergo adenotonsillectomy — but obstructive sleep apnea can persist or return, helped along by weight gain. Loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, unrefreshing sleep, morning headaches, daytime sleepiness, or slipping concentration all justify a fresh overnight sleep study (Rec 76/98); residual OSA is usually treated with CPAP (Rec 103). Untreated apnea quietly damages mood, school performance, and long-term heart health.

### Hearing: don't coast

Years of ear infections and eustachian-tube trouble leave many people with conductive hearing loss — around 40% of people with achondroplasia have functionally important loss, and in a Norwegian study half of adults under 45 already had some (mostly mild) loss, yet hearing aids are underused. Keep periodic audiology checks through the teen years (the consensus advises a low threshold for assessment, Rec 78) — a teen who seems inattentive may simply not be hearing the back row of the classroom.

## Treatment decisions that surface in the teen years

<Callout type="info" title="A reminder before this section">
  This is educational background, not medical advice. Decisions about growth-plate medication or limb-lengthening surgery are individual, consequential, and belong with your teen, your family, and a skeletal-dysplasia team who know you — never with a website.
</Callout>

### Growth-plate medicines: a closing window \{#vosoritide-a-closing-window}

All of these medicines work only while the growth plates are open, so for a teenager the window is closing — once the plates fuse at the end of puberty, they can no longer add height. As of October 2026:

- **Vosoritide (Voxzogo)** — a daily injection. Approved in the US for all ages with open growth plates (accelerated approval), in the EU from 4 months, and in Japan and Australia. It adds roughly 1.5–2 cm per year of extra growth while taken. The FDA is reviewing an application to convert it to full approval using long-term data, with a decision due by 28 February 2027. It is **not licensed in Great Britain** (Northern Ireland follows the EU licence).
- **Navepegritide (Yuviwel)** — a once-weekly injection. FDA accelerated approval on 27 February 2026 for ages 2 and over with open growth plates; in its main trial it added about 1.5 cm per year versus placebo. It is still under review in Europe.
- **Infigratinib** — a once-daily pill. In a 2026 trial of 114 children aged 3–17 it added about 1.7 cm per year versus placebo; the company submitted its FDA application in mid-2026. It is not approved anywhere yet.

These figures come from separate trials, not head-to-head comparisons. On the question everyone asks — final adult height — **the effect has not yet been established** for any of them; long-term vosoritide data are encouraging but come from comparisons with untreated groups rather than randomized trials. Daily injections are a real burden for some teens — in a 2026 interview study, caregivers described children resisting nightly injections — so how a medicine is given is a fair part of the decision. The full discussion, including availability by country and what is in trials, is on the [Treatments](/treatments) page.

### Limb lengthening: the decision that tends to arrive now

Extended limb lengthening — multiple operations over several years, typically done in adolescence — can add 15–30+ cm of height, and it is lengthy, painful, and carries real complication risks. Two anchor points for any family weighing it:

- The International Consensus Statement advises that the possibility be discussed with the patient and family, with **psychological consultation before undertaking it** (Rec 62), and that such surgery be done only in centers with real achondroplasia expertise (Rec 64).
- **Little People of America's position** is that it is an individual choice best deferred until the young person can genuinely participate in the decision — and that most people with achondroplasia do not choose it.

Notice what both anchors have in common: the teen's own voice at the center. A fifteen-year-old being lengthened for someone else's comfort is a very different situation from a fifteen-year-old who has met adults who chose each path and made their own call. The [Treatments](/treatments) page covers techniques, timelines, complication rates, and questions to ask a surgical team.

## Preparing for adult care, college, and work

### The pediatric-to-adult transition is a known danger zone

Children with achondroplasia are usually followed closely by skeletal-dysplasia teams; adults very often are not. In a European Achondroplasia Forum survey (2022), 8 of 15 provider centers said patients did not transition to *any* adult multidisciplinary team, no patient advocacy group rated the transition experience as good, and half rated it poor or very poor. The fix is to start early and be deliberate: the International Consensus Statement recommends **dedicated transition appointments in later adolescence** (Rec 131) to hand over care, teach the young adult their own history and red-flag symptoms, and identify adult providers — and to assess practical and psychosocial needs before any change in education or work (Rec 132).

### The transfer package a late teen should leave home with

- Know your **diagnosis and gene result**, and be able to explain them in one sentence.
- Know the **spinal stenosis red flags** (above) cold — they may not matter for 15 years, and then matter enormously.
- Know the **anesthesia precautions**: any surgery, ever, needs an anesthesia team told about the narrow craniocervical junction (avoid neck hyperextension), potentially difficult airway, and dosing by weight and body size. Carry a summary card; details in [Adults & Aging](/adults) and the [Medical Library](/medical-library).
- Have **adult primary care established before leaving pediatrics**, plus copies of key records and imaging.
- Understand your **insurance/benefits** situation, and carry an emergency information card.
- Take up the **genetic counseling offer** (Recs 7, 81) — even if reproduction feels a decade away, owning the information now beats scrambling later.

### College and work: the rights are real

In the US, dwarfism is a recognized condition under the Americans with Disabilities Act: employers with 15+ employees may not discriminate against a qualified person and must provide reasonable accommodations — which are typically cheap (step stools, adjustable-height desks, reachers, small keyboards). The Job Accommodation Network ([askjan.org](https://askjan.org)) gives free accommodation advice. The UK Equality Act 2010, the EU Employment Equality Directive, and Australia's Disability Discrimination Act 1992 provide broadly equivalent protection. LPA also offers **college scholarships** and assistance grants. The working details — disclosure decisions, accommodation requests, workplace setups — are covered in [Adults & Aging](/adults) and [Everyday Life](/daily-living).

## For parents: the art of stepping back

You have spent a decade being the expert, the advocate, and the scheduler. The teen years are when that job description deliberately shrinks — not because you stop caring, but because a 25-year-old who can run their own care is built between 13 and 18. What the evidence and the community consistently recommend:

- **Same chores, same expectations, same curfews.** Treat your teen according to age, not size — and insist that relatives, teachers, and strangers do the same. This has been the AAP's advice since early childhood and it matters more than ever now. A 16-year-old the size of a younger child still gets 16-year-old responsibilities and 16-year-old freedoms.
- **Engineer independence, don't perform it.** Step stools, reachers, lowered switches, pedal extenders when driving age arrives — the environment should let your teen do things alone, because competence is where confidence comes from.
- **Move to the back of the appointment.** Let your teen answer the doctor's questions, keep their own symptom notes, and eventually be seen partly alone — that is transition preparation, not rudeness.
- **Follow their lead** on terminology, on which aids they will use at school, on whether and how to attend LP events. Offer, don't impose.
- **Keep an eye on mood without pathologizing adolescence** — you now know the difference between a rough week and a persistent pattern, and you know where the door to help is.
- **Mind the rest of the family.** Siblings' adjustment deserves ongoing attention (the research on adult siblings is reassuring — the lasting effects reported were more often empathy, maturity, and closeness), and your own support network still matters. See [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing).

The destination is worth naming: an adult with achondroplasia who knows their body, owns their story, advocates without apology, and keeps their own specialist follow-up. Every step back you take on schedule is a step they take forward. Next: [Adults & Aging](/adults).
