<Callout type="info" title="The headline rule">
  Children and adults with achondroplasia should get every vaccine on their country's standard schedule. No vaccine is off-limits because of achondroplasia, and none should be skipped or delayed because of it. The International Consensus Statement (2022) says children with achondroplasia should follow the same national vaccination schedules as their average-height peers. Because breathing problems are more common in achondroplasia, protection against respiratory infections (flu, RSV, pneumococcus, COVID-19) is especially worth getting right.
</Callout>

<Callout type="caution" title="Policy checked on 5 October 2026">
  Vaccine advice is changing fast, especially in the United States, where it is being challenged in court. What follows was accurate on 5 October 2026. Always check your country's current schedule and talk to your pediatrician or GP; when national bodies and professional societies disagree, ask your clinician which one they follow and what your insurance or health service covers.
</Callout>

## Standard immunization schedule — no changes needed \{#standard-schedule}

The International Consensus Statement (2022) recommends the standard national schedule for children with achondroplasia, without modification. (The AAP's 2020 health-supervision report doesn't address vaccines separately, which in practice means the routine schedule applies.) Depending on your country, the routine schedule usually includes diphtheria/tetanus/pertussis, polio, Hib, measles-mumps-rubella, chickenpox (varicella), hepatitis B, pneumococcal, rotavirus, meningococcal vaccines and HPV, plus seasonal vaccines.

Two schedule details differ between countries and are worth knowing:

- **MMR and chickenpox.** Since January 2026 the UK gives a combined MMRV vaccine at 12 months and 18 months (the second dose moved earlier from 3 years 4 months), plus a fourth 6-in-1 dose at 18 months. In the US, separate MMR and chickenpox shots are recommended for the first dose at 12–47 months unless parents prefer the combined vaccine.
- **Hepatitis B at birth.** The US schedule currently in force and the AAP both recommend a hepatitis B dose within 24 hours of birth. Some other countries start hepatitis B in the first months instead.

There is no evidence that any vaccine works less well or causes more side effects in achondroplasia, and no evidence that achondroplasia affects the immune system. Live vaccines (MMR, chickenpox) carry the same small risks as in any child.

### The US situation in more detail

- **January 2026:** the CDC issued a new childhood schedule that cut routine recommendations from 17 to 11, moving hepatitis A and B, rotavirus, flu, meningococcal, COVID-19 and RSV protection to "high-risk" or "shared decision-making" categories.
- **16 March 2026:** a federal judge (AAP v. Kennedy) paused that schedule, the appointment of 13 new members of the CDC's vaccine advisory committee (ACIP), and the committee's 2025 votes on flu vaccine preservatives (thimerosal), COVID-19, and the hepatitis B birth dose. The earlier schedule is back in force. A separate May 2025 change — which dropped the routine COVID-19 recommendation for healthy children and pregnant women — was not paused.
- **Since then:** ACIP has not met. The government has appealed; the First Circuit Court of Appeals hears arguments on 6 October 2026, and its decision could change the CDC schedule again.
- **Professional societies** (AAP, AAFP, ACOG) now publish their own schedules, which recommend broader routine protection than the current CDC COVID-19 guidance.

In practice: ask your pediatrician which schedule they follow, and check with your insurer what is covered. As of October 2026, insurance and Vaccines for Children coverage still follow the CDC recommendations in force.

## Vaccines that are especially important in achondroplasia \{#especially-important}

These deserve particular attention because respiratory illness can hit harder in a child with a smaller chest, narrower airways, and often sleep apnea. (Sleep apnea affects roughly half to three-quarters of people with achondroplasia, according to the International Consensus.) There are no studies measuring how much worse flu, RSV or COVID-19 are in achondroplasia specifically; the advice below is based on these known risk factors.

### Annual influenza (flu) vaccine

Flu can cause pneumonia and other chest infections, and a child with less breathing reserve or untreated sleep apnea has less margin when that happens. The flu vaccine is the main yearly vaccine worth prioritizing.

- **Who is offered it:** in the US (both the CDC schedule in force and the AAP), flu vaccine is recommended every year for everyone from 6 months of age. In the UK, all children aged 2–3 and school children up to Year 11 are offered it; children 6 months to 2 years are offered it only if they are in a clinical risk group. If your child is under 2 in the UK, ask your GP or consultant whether their breathing or sleep problems put them in a risk group.
- **Shot or nasal spray:** the nasal spray (live attenuated vaccine) is fine for most children from age 2 and is the usual choice in the UK. It isn't used in children aged 2–4 who have had wheezing or asthma in the past 12 months, and long-term lung conditions are a reason to check first. If your child has significant breathing problems, recent wheeze, or lung disease, ask whether the injected vaccine is the better choice.
- **Pregnancy:** flu vaccine during pregnancy also protects the baby in the first months of life, before they can be vaccinated themselves.

**Don't forget household contacts.** Vaccinating parents, grandparents, siblings, and regular caregivers ("cocooning") reduces the chance of bringing flu into the home. This matters most for babies under 6 months, who are too young for the vaccine.

### RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) protection

RSV is the leading cause of bronchiolitis and infant hospitalization. It is usually a manageable winter illness, but in a baby with a small rib cage and narrow airways it can cause serious chest infection needing oxygen. There are now three ways to protect a baby:

- **RSV vaccine in pregnancy (Abrysvo):** antibodies cross the placenta and protect the newborn. Timing differs by country: 32–36 weeks in the US (during the months before and during RSV season), from 28 weeks in the UK and Australia. A baby whose mother was vaccinated at least two weeks before birth usually doesn't also need an antibody injection — confirm with your pediatrician.
- **Long-acting antibody injections — nirsevimab (Beyfortus) and clesrovimab (Enflonsia):** a single injection gives a baby ready-made antibodies for their first RSV season (neither is a vaccine). In the US, the AAP and ACOG recommend either product for babies under 8 months whose mother wasn't vaccinated in pregnancy. Clesrovimab was approved by the FDA in June 2025 on the strength of its own trials. Only nirsevimab is used for a second season in children at continued high risk; achondroplasia on its own isn't one of the listed second-season conditions, so ask your team whether your child's breathing history qualifies.
- **Palivizumab (Synagis):** the older monthly injection. It is no longer routinely recommended and was discontinued in the US on 31 December 2025. In the UK, nirsevimab has replaced it for high-risk babies.

Availability depends heavily on where you live (see [How advice differs by country](#by-country)). In the UK, for example, a baby with achondroplasia born at term is protected through the pregnancy vaccine rather than an infant antibody.

<Callout type="tip" title="Timing matters — ask early">
  RSV season in the Northern Hemisphere typically runs October–March; in the Southern Hemisphere roughly April–September. In the US: if your baby is born during RSV season, ask for the antibody before you leave the hospital (it is recommended within the first week of life). If your baby is born outside the season, ask for it shortly before the season starts — in early autumn. Ask about the pregnancy vaccine at your prenatal visits too. Don't assume a particular brand will be in stock; either antibody is fine.
</Callout>

### Pneumococcal vaccines

*Streptococcus pneumoniae* causes pneumonia, meningitis, and ear infections. Conjugate pneumococcal vaccines are part of every standard infant schedule (in the US, PCV15 or PCV20 at 2, 4, 6 and 12–15 months), and that standard schedule is enough for most children with achondroplasia.

- Children with recurrent serious pneumococcal infections should have their vaccination record reviewed with their pediatrician.
- **Adults:** in the US, a pneumococcal conjugate vaccine is now recommended for all adults aged 50 and over (PCV20 or PCV21 alone, or PCV15 followed by PPSV23 a year later), and for some younger adults with certain medical conditions. Adults with achondroplasia who have restrictive lung disease or other long-term conditions should ask whether they qualify earlier.

### COVID-19 vaccines

Limited breathing reserve and sleep apnea are general risk factors for more severe respiratory illness, but there are no studies of COVID-19 severity in achondroplasia specifically. Achondroplasia is not on any country's formal COVID-19 high-risk list, so whether your child qualifies depends on their individual health and your local rules.

- **US:** the 2026–27 vaccines (approved August 2026) are licensed for people aged 65 and over and younger people with a qualifying high-risk condition. For young children, only Moderna's Spikevax is available from 6 months; Pfizer's Comirnaty starts at 5 years, and Novavax and mNEXSPIKE at 12 years. The CDC recommends COVID-19 vaccination for children who are not immunocompromised through shared decision-making with a clinician; the AAP recommends it for all children 6–23 months and for older children at higher risk; ACOG recommends it in pregnancy.
- **UK:** in autumn 2026, children are offered the vaccine only if they are immunosuppressed.
- **Elsewhere:** check your national or provincial guidance.

There are no known interactions between COVID-19 vaccines and vosoritide (Voxzogo) or navepegritide (Yuviwel), though none have been formally studied.

## Managing respiratory infections when they happen \{#respiratory-infections}

Despite best vaccination efforts, respiratory infections will happen. In achondroplasia, they warrant a lower threshold for getting help than in average-height children.

### When to escalate sooner

Contact your medical team sooner when an infant or child with achondroplasia has:

- **Fast or labored breathing** — sucking in between or under the ribs or at the neck, nasal flaring, grunting, or breathing clearly faster than usual for the child
- **Oxygen saturation persistently below 92%** on a home oximeter your team has recommended — or any desaturation alarm on a prescribed home monitor
- **Poor feeding during an illness** — an infant who is not feeding at all for 4–6 hours needs medical assessment
- **Unusual floppiness or lethargy** beyond typical illness drowsiness
- **Any worsening of known sleep apnea signs** during an illness — longer pauses, more labored breathing during sleep

Standard "watch and wait at home for 3 days" advice doesn't apply in the same way to an infant with achondroplasia who has a chest infection. Call sooner, and tell the team about the diagnosis — the smaller chest and possible sleep apnea change the risk.

### CPAP during illness

Adults and older children who use CPAP for sleep apnea should keep using it through respiratory illnesses — this is not the time to skip it. If a blocked nose makes the mask hard to use, a humidifier, saline nasal rinse, or a short course of decongestant (on medical advice) may help. If CPAP truly can't be tolerated during a severe illness, contact the sleep team or your doctor for advice.

### Antibiotics: when they are and are not appropriate

Most respiratory infections in children are viral — antibiotics don't help and can cause harm. The main exception worth knowing in achondroplasia is bacterial middle-ear infection (ear pain, fever, a red bulging eardrum or discharge): because ear disease is so common, treating clear-cut ear infections promptly helps protect hearing. Ordinary colds — runny nose, mild cough, no ear pain, little or no fever — are treated the same as in any child: fluids, comfort, and watchful waiting.

## High altitude considerations \{#altitude}

A small rib cage means less breathing reserve, and that matters at altitude, where there is less oxygen in the air. There are no achondroplasia-specific studies or agreed altitude limits, so the advice here is the same as for anyone with reduced breathing reserve:

- For infants with known restrictive lung disease or sleep apnea, discuss any planned trip to high altitude, or a long flight, with your pulmonologist or sleep team before you go.
- Older children and adults with borderline breathing function or untreated sleep apnea should take high-altitude travel seriously and ask their doctor first.
- Any symptom that appears at altitude — unusual breathlessness, worse sleep, new low oxygen readings — needs prompt medical review, and descent if it is significant.

## Tobacco and air quality \{#smoking}

Tobacco smoke and air pollution hit hardest in people with less breathing reserve, and that includes secondhand smoke. Keeping children away from cigarette smoke and vaping is especially important given smaller lungs and the higher rate of breathing problems; the AAP (2020) asks clinicians to discuss smoking with teenagers with achondroplasia, as with any adolescent. Adults with achondroplasia who smoke have one more reason to stop.

On air quality: during periods of wildfire smoke, high ozone, or heavy pollution, children with achondroplasia are best kept indoors with filtered air where possible — the same advice given to children with asthma. A HEPA air purifier in the bedroom is a reasonable investment for families in wildfire-prone regions.

## Respiratory monitoring in achondroplasia \{#monitoring}

Beyond immunizations, regular respiratory checks catch problems before they become crises:

| Check                         | Timing                                                                                                                                     | Who                                        |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------ |
| Sleep study (polysomnography) | In the first months of life (the UK 2026 guidelines say by 6 months); repeat when symptomatic or after airway surgery                    | All infants; all ages when symptoms arise  |
| Pulmonology consultation      | When restrictive lung disease signs appear; early in pregnancy for women with achondroplasia; for infants with persistent fast breathing   | As indicated                               |
| Oxygen saturation monitoring  | Home pulse oximetry may be recommended for infants with known sleep apnea or infants at risk who can't get a sleep study quickly          | As directed by care team                   |
| Spirometry (lung function)    | Not routine in children; used when clinically indicated; needs size-adjusted reference values to be interpreted correctly                 | When restrictive lung disease is suspected |

<Callout type="tip" title="Thinking about a home pulse oximeter?">
  Some families of children with diagnosed sleep apnea keep a pulse oximeter at home to track oxygen levels during illnesses. Before buying one, ask your pediatrician or sleep team which device to use and which readings should prompt a call or a visit. Cheap fingertip devices sold for adults often don't read accurately on babies and small children, which need a proper pediatric probe, and pulse oximeters can read falsely high on darker skin. Treat the number as one clue: how your child looks and breathes matters more.
</Callout>
