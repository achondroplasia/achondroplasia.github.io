## Start where you are

<StartPaths />

<HomeIntro>
<div class="home-intro__text">

Achondroplasia is the most common form of dwarfism — a difference in how bones grow, caused by a single-letter change in one gene (*FGFR3*). It is nobody's fault: nothing a parent did or didn't do causes it, and nothing could have prevented it. It does not touch intelligence, personality, or the capacity to learn — children with achondroplasia grow into adults who study, work, drive, marry, raise families, and live full, independent lives. The medical complications that do come with it are, in the great majority of cases, manageable — and many of the serious ones are preventable — when the right checks happen at the right ages.

This site collects what the published clinical guidelines, the peer-reviewed research, and the dwarfism community itself know — in plain English, in one place, for free, with a source for every claim.

</div>

<Callout type="tip" title="If you're here because of a new diagnosis">
  You are not alone, and you have time. Here is a sensible first hour on this site:

  - **Breathe.** This is a livable, well-understood condition. Around 80% of babies with achondroplasia are born to two average-height parents, so almost every family starts exactly where you are: knowing nothing. The learning curve is real, but it has been climbed hundreds of thousands of times.
  - **[Understand what it is](/understanding)** — the gene, why it happened, what it does and doesn't mean, and the questions every parent asks first.
  - **If your child is a baby, [read about the first years (0–2)](/first-years)** — this is the one stage where specific extra tests genuinely matter most, and where a few simple handling rules protect your baby.
  - **[Connect with other families](/wellbeing)** — parents consistently say that meeting other families, and adults with achondroplasia, was the single most helpful early step.
  - **[Build the care plan](/checklist)** — a visit-by-visit checklist of what should happen at every age, to take to your pediatrician.

## 2026 research highlights \{#research-2026}

Achondroplasia research moved fast in 2026. Here is what changed since the last major guideline cycle, so you can have an informed conversation with your care team.

<Grid>
  <Card kicker="Drug approval" title="Navepegritide (Yuviwel) — FDA approved Feb 2026">
    A second growth drug is now approved: a once-weekly injection (vs. vosoritide's daily), with fewer injection-site reactions and no pre-injection food requirement. Approved for ages 2+ with open growth plates. The two drugs work on the same pathway and are alternatives, not additions. [Full details →](/treatments#navepegritide)
  </Card>

  <Card kicker="Drug trial" title="Infigratinib — positive Phase 3, FDA filing submitted 2026">
    A once-daily *oral pill* (BridgeBio's infigratinib) reported the largest growth velocity difference of any achondroplasia trial and the first statistically significant improvement in body proportions. Not yet approved, but BridgeBio submitted its FDA application in September 2026. [Full details →](/treatments#pipeline)
  </Card>

  <Card kicker="New guideline" title="Hypochondroplasia diagnosis consensus — 2026">
    The first international Delphi consensus recommendations on *diagnosing* hypochondroplasia were published in Nature Reviews Endocrinology (2026) — important because borderline cases between achondroplasia and hypochondroplasia affect treatment eligibility and surveillance intensity. [How they differ →](/understanding#differential)
  </Card>

  <Card kicker="Sleep guideline" title="EAF sleep-disordered breathing guidance — 2025">
    The European Achondroplasia Forum published updated guiding principles for managing sleep-disordered breathing across the lifespan — covering the full spectrum from infant central apnea through adult OSA, with clearer indications for polysomnography timing and repeat testing. [In the Medical Library →](/medical-library)
  </Card>

  <Card kicker="New pipeline" title="TYRA-300 (dabogratinib) — Phase 2 started 2025">
    A next-generation FGFR3-selective oral inhibitor designed with even less off-target activity than infigratinib. First child dosed in the BEACH301 trial in 2025; preclinical data suggest possible benefits for foramen magnum and spinal dimensions. Years from any approval. [Pipeline →](/treatments#pipeline)
  </Card>

  <Card kicker="Consensus overview" title="2026 plain-language Consensus Statement overview">
    A free, plain-language overview of the 2022 International Consensus Statement's 160 recommendations was published in Orphanet Journal of Rare Diseases in 2026 — making the full guideline accessible without a journal subscription. [Find it in the Medical Library →](/medical-library)
  </Card>
</Grid>

## Is something urgent? \{#urgent}

<Callout type="emergency" title="Seek urgent care if…">
  - A **baby** has pauses in breathing during sleep, turns blue or gray around the lips, or becomes suddenly, extremely floppy or unresponsive — call emergency services now. These can be signs of pressure on the brainstem at the base of the skull, the most dangerous complication of infancy and the reason the first-year checks matter so much.
  - **New weakness, numbness, or tingling in the legs**, or a clear drop in how far someone can walk — at any age — needs prompt medical review for spinal cord or nerve compression.
  - **New loss of bladder or bowel control** is an emergency sign of spinal cord compression — go to the emergency department.

  These are examples, not the full list. See [Warning signs & emergencies](/warning-signs) for every age, symptom by symptom.
</Callout>

## About this guide \{#about}

This site is a plain-English synthesis of the major published guidance on achondroplasia: the American Academy of Pediatrics clinical report *Health Supervision for People With Achondroplasia* (2020), the *International Consensus Statement* on lifelong achondroplasia care (2022 — 55 experts from 16 countries, 160 recommendations), the *GeneReviews* chapter, the European Achondroplasia Forum's guidance, the Westmead therapy guidelines, resources from Little People of America and other community organizations, and the peer-reviewed research behind them. The [Medical Library](/medical-library) lists and annotates all of it, and every page ends with its own numbered sources.

**It is educational, not medical advice.** It cannot replace clinicians who know you or your child — ideally a team experienced with skeletal dysplasias. Use it to understand, to prepare questions, and to know what good care looks like.

The guide is open source and free of advertising. If you spot an error or something out of date, corrections are welcome — the full text lives [on GitHub](https://github.com/achondroplasia/achondroplasia.github.io).
