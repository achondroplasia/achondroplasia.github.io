<Callout type="info" title="Why a dedicated page matters">
  Pain in achondroplasia is systematically undertreated. One reason is that clinicians unfamiliar with the condition may attribute pain to "just part of the condition" — a phrase to push back on firmly. Another is that the standard approach to back pain in the general population (rest, opioids, repeat prescriptions) is the wrong approach here. This page explains what the published literature actually supports and what questions to bring to your care team.
</Callout>

## Where the pain comes from \{#sources-of-pain}

Pain in achondroplasia is almost always multifactorial — several overlapping sources contributing simultaneously. Identifying which source is driving each component of pain is the first step, because the treatments are different.

| Source                                           | What it feels like                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Peak age                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Neurogenic claudication from lumbar stenosis** | Aching, burning, heaviness, or weakness in the buttocks and legs brought on by walking or standing, relieved by sitting or bending forward. The most common adult pain pattern. See [Adults & Aging](/adults#spinal-stenosis).              | 30s onward; median symptom onset age 33 in Norwegian cohort; affects \~68% of adults |
| **Lumbar radiculopathy**                         | Sharp, shooting, burning, or electrical pain radiating from the lower back into one leg, often following a dermatomal pattern. Caused by nerve root compression at a specific spinal level.                                                 | Any adult age; often the first neurological symptom                                  |
| **Chronic lower back pain**                      | Deep aching in the lower back, often worse with prolonged sitting or standing. Driven by the exaggerated lumbar lordosis (sway-back) characteristic of achondroplasia, which creates abnormal loading on the lumbar facet joints and discs. | 20s onward; often the earliest adult pain complaint (40–70% prevalence)              |
| **Neck and upper back pain**                     | Aching at the base of the skull and upper cervical spine, and/or across the upper back and shoulders. Driven by compensating for a disproportionately large, heavy head on a shorter neck — the head-neck muscles are in constant tension.  | Young adults through midlife                                                         |
| **Knee pain**                                    | Medial (inner) or anterior (front) knee pain, often associated with bowed legs (genu varum). Altered gait mechanics from bowing create uneven joint loading that accelerates cartilage wear.                                                | Any age in adults with significant bowing; worse after physical activity             |
| **Headaches**                                    | Variable — tension-type headaches from neck muscle strain, or morning headaches from sleep apnea and nocturnal hypoxia. Headaches in achondroplasia warrant medical evaluation, not routine acceptance.                                     | Any age                                                                              |
| **Hip and joint pain**                           | Deep groin or lateral hip pain, often related to altered pelvic tilt from the lordosis. Joint hyperlaxity may contribute to instability pain.                                                                                               | Any adult age                                                                        |

## Assessing pain properly \{#assessment}

The International Consensus Statement (2022) recommends that pain be assessed at *every medical encounter* using a structured tool — not as an afterthought, not as "any pain today?", but with a tool that captures where the pain is, how severe it is, and how it affects function and daily life. The 2026 International Achondroplasia Forum guiding principles on orthopaedic outcomes reinforce this, naming chronic pain and health-related quality of life among the core outcomes clinicians should actively monitor — not only when a patient raises it. In a 2025 study across three countries, almost all adults with skeletal dysplasia (99%) reported some pain.

The **Brief Pain Inventory (BPI)** is the standard validated tool used in research and recommended for clinical practice. It takes about 5 minutes, asks patients to rate their worst, least, average, and current pain on a 0–10 scale, and asks how pain has interfered with seven life areas (general activity, mood, walking, work, relationships, sleep, and enjoyment of life). You can find it at the MD Anderson Pain Research Group website and complete it yourself before an appointment — it turns a vague pain complaint into a documented record that forces a clinical response.

**What to document between appointments:**

- Pain location (draw it on a body diagram if helpful)
- Pain character (aching, burning, stabbing, shooting, heaviness, numbness)
- Triggers (walking distance before leg symptoms; posture; time of day)
- Relieving factors (sitting, bending forward, lying down, heat, ice)
- Worst and average pain score in the past week
- Impact on walking distance, sleep, and daily activities

This record is invaluable when you see a new clinician, when assessing whether treatment is helping, and when making the case for more aggressive management.

## The multimodal approach: why single interventions fail \{#multimodal}

The most common reason adults with achondroplasia report that pain management "doesn't work" is that they received a single intervention — usually a prescription for an NSAID or, worse, an opioid — rather than a coordinated plan addressing multiple dimensions of pain simultaneously. The International Consensus Statement is explicit that management should be **multidisciplinary**, incorporating physical, pharmacological, and psychological components. Here is what each layer does:

### Layer 1: Physical / rehabilitative

- **Weight management** — Extra abdominal weight deepens the lumbar curve and adds load to the spine. The 2026 International Achondroplasia Forum principles list weight management as a core way to minimise pain, dysfunction, and surgical complications. No trial has measured exactly how much weight loss helps in achondroplasia, but it is the main pain factor you can change yourself.
- **Core strengthening** — The deep trunk muscles (transversus abdominis, multifidus) act as the spine's internal brace. Strengthening them through physical therapy reduces the mechanical load on the lumbar facets and discs. Modified Pilates and clinical physiotherapy core programs are both used. Important: avoid spinal extension exercises (back extensions, cobras) that increase lumbar lordosis and compress the already-tight posterior structures. Flexion-based exercises are generally better tolerated.
- **Hip flexor stretching** — Tight hip flexors (iliopsoas) tilt the pelvis forward and worsen the lumbar lordosis. A daily psoas stretch (low lunge or supine knee-to-chest) is a simple maintenance practice that many adults with achondroplasia find helpful for low back pain.
- **Swimming and cycling** — Low-impact aerobic exercise builds cardiovascular fitness and is often well tolerated by people with back pain, because it does not jar the spine. See [Nutrition & Exercise](/nutrition#exercise).
- **Heat and cold** — Not glamorous, but genuinely effective for muscular pain. Heat (20 minutes, warm not hot) relaxes the chronically tense paraspinal muscles before exercise or physical therapy. Cold (15 minutes, cloth-wrapped ice pack) reduces inflammation after activity. Both are low-risk options with no medication side effects.

### Layer 2: Pharmacological

<Callout type="caution" title="Medication decisions belong with your prescriber">
  The following describes the published evidence base for pain medications used in spinal stenosis and musculoskeletal pain — it is educational, not prescribing advice. Every medication decision must be made with a clinician who knows your full health history, other medications, and kidney/liver/cardiac status.
</Callout>

- **NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac):** the usual first-line medicine for back and joint pain in US (American College of Physicians) and UK (NICE) guidance. Effective for flares. For long-term use, a stomach-protecting medicine (a proton-pump inhibitor such as omeprazole) is often co-prescribed, and kidney function should be checked periodically. NSAIDs can raise blood pressure and heart risk — relevant because high blood pressure is common in adults with achondroplasia — so discuss this with your prescriber.
- **Gabapentin or pregabalin (gabapentinoids):** used for *neuropathic* pain — the burning, tingling, shooting, electrical quality of pain from nerve compression. But the evidence for nerve pain from the back is weak: a 2017 trial found pregabalin no better than placebo for sciatica, and NICE advises against gabapentinoids for sciatica and low back pain. **Breathing caution:** in 2019 the US FDA warned that these medicines can cause serious breathing problems in people with respiratory risk factors — especially when combined with opioids or other sedating medicines. Sleep apnea is common in achondroplasia, so make sure your prescriber knows your sleep-study results before starting one. Other side effects: sleepiness, dizziness, weight gain (which works against the spine).
- **Duloxetine (Cymbalta):** an SNRI antidepressant with evidence in chronic musculoskeletal pain, including osteoarthritis and low back pain. Guidelines differ: the American College of Physicians lists it as a second-line option for chronic low back pain, while NICE (UK) advises against antidepressants for low back pain. It can also treat co-existing depression or anxiety — worth discussing with your prescriber.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline, nortriptyline) at low dose:** taken at night, they improve sleep architecture disrupted by chronic pain and reduce central sensitization. Lower side-effect burden at sub-antidepressant doses (10–25 mg nightly) than at full antidepressant doses. They are recommended for neuropathic pain (NeuPSIG, 2015), but NICE advises against them for low back pain itself; they can cause drowsiness and dizziness, which raise fall risk.
- **Topical agents (diclofenac gel, lidocaine patches):** for localized joint pain (knee, hip), topical NSAIDs deliver adequate tissue concentrations with minimal systemic exposure. Lidocaine patches address localized neuropathic pain. Both are useful for people who cannot tolerate oral medications.

### The opioid question — answered honestly

<Callout type="caution" title="Opioids and achondroplasia: a specific caution">
  NICE (UK) advises against opioids for chronic low back pain and chronic sciatica, and the CDC (2022) says non-opioid treatments are preferred for chronic pain. In achondroplasia there is an additional, condition-specific concern: **opioids suppress respiratory drive**, and most adults with achondroplasia have obstructive sleep apnea (59% in the Norwegian population study). Opioids worsen nocturnal respiratory obstruction — they relax the pharyngeal muscles, impair the arousal response to hypoxia, and can cause dangerous oxygen desaturation during sleep even at modest doses in people who already have compromised sleep breathing. If an opioid is being considered — for example, post-surgically or for an acute severe exacerbation — an up-to-date sleep study result should inform the prescriber, and the patient should be monitored closely. Long-term opioid prescribing without concurrent sleep apnea management is not appropriate in achondroplasia. The risk is higher still when opioids are combined with gabapentin, pregabalin, benzodiazepines, or other sedating medicines.
</Callout>

### Layer 3: Interventional

- **Epidural steroid injections (ESIs):** a steroid injected around the nerve roots. For **spinal stenosis** the evidence is disappointing: in a large 2014 trial (LESS), adding steroid to a local anesthetic gave little or no extra benefit for leg pain or function, and NICE advises against epidural injections for neurogenic claudication from central stenosis. They may help some people with acute, severe sciatica from a single trapped nerve root. In achondroplasia the narrow canal makes placement harder and raises the risk of a dural puncture, so imaging guidance and an experienced clinician are essential. ESIs never treat the stenosis itself.
- **Facet joint injections and medial branch blocks:** for pain arising from the facet joints (the small paired joints at the back of each vertebra), a diagnostic injection with local anesthetic can confirm the source. If positive, radiofrequency ablation (RFA) — using heat to temporarily interrupt the pain-carrying nerve — can provide relief for some months; how long varies, and the nerve regrows.
- **Nerve root blocks:** targeted around specific nerve roots for localized radiculopathy.
- **Spinal cord stimulation (SCS):** for carefully selected patients with intractable lumbar stenosis pain who are not surgical candidates or who have residual pain after surgery, SCS (a small implanted pulse generator that modifies pain signals) is sometimes used. There are no published data in achondroplasia, and placing the leads in an already-narrow canal is a specific concern — so it is a last-resort option, considered only at an experienced center.

### Layer 4: Psychological

This layer is not a suggestion that pain is "all in the head." Chronic pain — from any cause — changes the nervous system. The brain becomes sensitized to pain signals, amplifying them even when the original structural cause has not changed. This phenomenon, called **central sensitization**, is why chronic pain often responds poorly to purely structural treatments (injections, surgery) once it has been present for years. Addressing it requires psychological tools:

- **Pain-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT):** the most evidence-based psychological treatment for chronic pain. CBT helps reframe catastrophic pain thoughts, build tolerance for uncomfortable activities, and set functional goals. It does not reduce the underlying pain signal directly, but consistently reduces pain-related disability and distress — meaning a person in the same amount of pain lives a fuller life. Seek a clinical psychologist who specializes in chronic pain, not general CBT.
- **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT):** an evidence-based variant of CBT specifically developed for chronic conditions — it focuses on building a meaningful life around, rather than in spite of, pain. Increasingly the preferred approach in chronic pain.
- **Mindfulness-based pain reduction:** an 8-week structured program (MBSR) with published evidence for moderate pain reduction and significant improvement in pain-related quality of life. Available in-person and via validated online programs.
- **Sleep treatment as pain treatment:** the bidirectional relationship between sleep and pain is one of the most robust in pain medicine — poor sleep worsens pain, and pain worsens sleep. Treating sleep apnea with CPAP, and addressing insomnia with cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), directly improves pain outcomes. This loop is particularly important in achondroplasia where sleep apnea is prevalent.

## Finding the right pain specialist \{#specialist}

The most effective pain management for achondroplasia happens at the intersection of skeletal dysplasia expertise and pain medicine expertise — a combination rarely found in one clinician. The practical approach:

- **Start with your skeletal dysplasia center:** ask for a referral to a pain specialist who has treated patients with spinal stenosis from skeletal causes. They do not need to have treated achondroplasia specifically, but they need to understand that the canal is congenitally narrow and behaves differently from age-related stenosis in average-height patients.
- **Look for multidisciplinary pain clinics:** the best pain management happens in clinics that have a physician (pain specialist or physiatrist), a physiotherapist, a psychologist, and a nurse coordinator working together on the same patient — not separate referrals that never communicate.
- **Bring your full imaging history:** the pain specialist needs to see your MRI reports (or ideally the images) to understand the degree of stenosis and which levels are affected. Bring reports from your skeletal dysplasia center if the pain specialist is new to you.
- **Be explicit about the anesthesia constraints:** any interventional procedure — ESI, facet injection, RFA — involves positioning and possibly sedation. The achondroplasia anesthesia precautions ([Warning Signs](/warning-signs#surgery-checklist)) apply even to minimally invasive procedures.

## Pain through the life stages \{#pain-by-age}

### Children and adolescents

Chronic pain is not only an adult problem. Norwegian population data found symptomatic spinal stenosis beginning as young as age 10. Children with bowed legs may experience knee pain from altered mechanics. The approach in younger patients:

- Physical therapy and activity modification are the mainstays — no opioids, no spinal injections in children except in specialist settings.
- Bowed-leg correction (guided growth surgery or osteotomy) often dramatically relieves knee pain — see [Childhood](/childhood) and [Treatments](/treatments).
- Treat sleep apnea early — its contribution to pain amplification is the same in children as in adults.

### Pregnant adults

Pregnancy in women with achondroplasia commonly worsens lower back and pelvic pain as the centre of gravity shifts and the already-exaggerated lordosis increases. Several common pain medicines need care in pregnancy — in particular, the US FDA advises avoiding NSAIDs such as ibuprofen from 20 weeks onward unless a doctor specifically advises otherwise, and opioids and gabapentinoids need specialist discussion. Options: physiotherapy, aquatic therapy (pool walking is excellent in pregnancy), supportive lumbar belts, and careful paracetamol/acetaminophen use under obstetric guidance. Any new neurological symptoms during pregnancy (leg weakness, bladder changes) require urgent assessment — see [Adults & Aging](/adults#pregnancy).

### Older adults

Pain becomes more complex with age as multiple sources overlap. A pain assessment in an older adult with achondroplasia should identify which components are neuropathic (stenosis-related) and which are nociceptive (joint wear) — because the treatments differ. Fall risk must also be assessed, as sedating pain medications (gabapentin, pregabalin, amitriptyline) and muscle weakness from stenosis both increase fall risk — and older age is itself one of the FDA's risk factors for breathing problems with gabapentinoids. See [Adults & Aging](/adults#aging).

<Callout type="tip" title="One practical tool: the pain diary">
  Keep a daily pain diary for at least four weeks before any specialist appointment: date, pain location, severity (0–10), triggers, and what helped. Apps such as PainScale, Manage My Pain, or even a simple spreadsheet work. A four-week diary transforms "my back hurts a lot" into "my average pain is 6/10, worst with walking more than 200 meters, better with sitting; my walking distance has decreased from 400 to 150 meters in six months." The second description is far more likely to get you the right tests.
</Callout>
