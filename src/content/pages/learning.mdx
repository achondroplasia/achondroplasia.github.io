<Callout type="info" title="The key distinction that changes everything">
  Many of the academic challenges children with achondroplasia face are *secondary* to medical issues — especially hearing loss and sleep apnea — rather than an intrinsic learning difficulty. A child who can't hear clearly in a noisy classroom, or who is chronically tired from disrupted sleep, will underperform regardless of ability. Checking and treating these first, alongside any extra school support, is the right sequence. The accommodations on this page work best when the underlying causes are also addressed.
</Callout>

## What "normal intelligence" actually means in practice \{#intelligence}

The AAP (2020) says people with achondroplasia are of normal intelligence, and expects motor delay but not social or cognitive delay in young children. That means the same wide range of ability you find in anyone — some children are very academic, some less so. It doesn't mean every child will learn at the same pace as their peers. One small study (14 children aged 6–15) found average scores within the normal range but lower than average on verbal IQ, working memory, arithmetic, attention and executive function, with a lot of variation between children; the authors suggested individual assessment when there are concerns. Several conditions that are more common in achondroplasia can make learning harder:

- **Hearing loss from middle-ear disease** — middle-ear infections and fluid are very common, and conductive hearing loss affects around 1 in 6 children, according to the International Consensus. A child learning to read and speak while hearing through a fog of middle-ear fluid is working against a real barrier, not a cognitive one. Treating it (with ear tubes, hearing aids, or better classroom acoustics) helps a great deal. See [Childhood](/childhood#ears-hearing-speech) for the full story.
- **Obstructive sleep apnea** — affects roughly half to three-quarters of people with achondroplasia. Poor-quality sleep can affect attention, memory, and mood; in the general population, children who snore or have other sleep-disordered breathing symptoms are more likely to be rated as inattentive or hyperactive.
- **Fatigue from physical challenges** — a shorter stride means more effort per distance, and back or joint pain can start to affect older children and teens. A child managing physical fatigue has less energy left for schoolwork.
- **Speech and language delay** — about 20% of children with achondroplasia have delayed speech (International Consensus), often linked to hearing. Untreated speech delay at 2 can put a child behind at 5, even when underlying ability is intact.

The practical implication: if a child with achondroplasia is struggling at school, the *first questions* include "Has their hearing been tested recently? Has sleep apnea been checked and treated? Are they getting enough sleep? Are they in pain?" — alongside, not instead of, looking at learning itself.

## Speech and language: the foundation \{#speech-language}

Speech and language delays are common in young children with achondroplasia. **Fluctuating hearing loss from middle-ear fluid** is a major driver, so hearing should always be checked when speech is slow. With hearing managed and early help where needed, language usually catches up well.

### The numbers

- About **20% of children with achondroplasia have speech delay**, according to the International Consensus Statement.
- Achondroplasia-specific developmental charts show that early communication skills are less delayed than later ones, such as combining words. The International Consensus recommends checking speech milestones against these achondroplasia-specific charts rather than standard ones.
- The AAP (2020) recommends a yearly hearing assessment through childhood; the UK 2026 guidelines recommend audiology at 1 year and every 6 months through the preschool years.

### When to refer for speech-language therapy

The AAP (2020) recommends a **speech evaluation no later than age 2** for every child with achondroplasia, and says that if speech is delayed, hearing loss from middle-ear fluid should be ruled out. Ask for an earlier referral if words aren't appearing by 12–15 months, your child isn't combining words when expected on the achondroplasia charts, speech is hard for unfamiliar adults to understand at 3, or your child isn't following age-appropriate instructions.

A speech-language pathologist (speech and language therapist) who has worked with children with chronic ear disease will understand the difference between a delay and a disorder and adjust goals accordingly. Ask your skeletal dysplasia center for a referral.

### What speech-language therapy covers

- **Receptive language** — understanding vocabulary, instructions, and concepts.
- **Expressive language** — building vocabulary, sentence length, and storytelling.
- **Articulation** — clear production of sounds, affected by hearing (you can't copy what you can't hear) and possibly by the smaller mouth and jaw.
- **Feeding and swallowing** — for infants with feeding difficulties. In achondroplasia this matters because feeding or swallowing problems can be an early sign of compression at the base of the skull, and a good therapist will flag it for medical review rather than treating it as an isolated feeding delay.
- **Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)** — picture boards, apps, or devices that support communication while speech develops. Ask your therapist whether AAC could help; it is commonly used alongside speech therapy.

## Attention, ADHD, and executive function \{#attention-adhd}

There's little research on attention difficulties or ADHD in achondroplasia. The small 2016 study above found lower average attention and executive-function scores, but we don't know how common ADHD itself is. Several medical factors can produce attention problems that look like ADHD:

- **Sleep apnea:** in the general population, children with snoring and other sleep-disordered breathing symptoms are more often rated as inattentive and hyperactive, and these problems often improve when the sleep problem is treated. In the largest randomized trial (CHAT), removing tonsils and adenoids improved parent- and teacher-reported behavior, but didn't improve formally tested attention and executive function over 7 months. So treating sleep apnea is important, but it isn't guaranteed to resolve attention problems.
- **Hearing loss** makes it harder to filter background noise, stay focused on a speaker, and follow multi-step spoken instructions — all things that look like inattention.
- **Fatigue** from pain, poor sleep, and physical effort reduces the energy available for sustained focus.

A child with achondroplasia can also have ADHD, like any child. The aim is an accurate picture: **hearing and sleep should be checked, and treated where needed, as part of any ADHD evaluation**, by a professional who knows the achondroplasia context. That shouldn't mean months of delay before a child with clear difficulties gets help.

### What ADHD evaluation in achondroplasia looks like

- A recent hearing test, with any hearing loss treated or accounted for.
- A check for sleep apnea symptoms, with a sleep study if they are present, and treatment where needed.
- Standardized rating scales (for example Conners or NICHQ Vanderbilt) completed by parents and teachers, covering more than one setting.
- Where needed, a psychological or neuropsychological assessment, with tests set up for the child's size (seating height and reach, breaks for fatigue) and interpreted with the physical context in mind.

### Executive function support

Whether or not ADHD is formally diagnosed, many children benefit from explicit support for **executive function** — the brain's organizing and planning skills:

- Breaking tasks into smaller, explicit steps written on a list or whiteboard.
- Visual schedules and timers for transitions.
- A dedicated, low-distraction workspace at home and preferential seating in class (near the front, away from windows and hallways).
- Explicit teaching of organizational strategies — how to plan a homework task, how to check finished work — that many children pick up on their own but some need taught.

## Early intervention: birth to age 3 (US) \{#early-intervention}

In the US, babies and toddlers can get free or low-cost developmental services (physical therapy, occupational therapy, speech therapy, family support) through **IDEA Part C early intervention**, run by each state. Children qualify if they have a developmental delay or a diagnosed condition with a high probability of causing one; many states accept achondroplasia under this second route, but rules vary. Services are written into an Individualized Family Service Plan (IFSP). Ask your pediatrician for a referral, or contact your state's early intervention program directly — you don't need to wait for a delay to appear. Around age 3, the team should help plan the move to preschool services under an IEP or 504 plan if your child still needs support.

## IEP vs. 504 Plan: a practical guide \{#iep-504-deep-dive}

In the United States, school-age children with disabilities are protected by two federal laws. Many families are confused about which applies. Here is a summary:

| Feature                                   | 504 Plan                                                                                                                                                                                                  | IEP (Individualized Education Program)                                                                                                                                                                     |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Law**                                   | Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (civil rights law)                                                                                                                                                  | Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)                                                                                                                                                         |
| **Eligibility**                           | Any physical or mental impairment that substantially limits a major life activity (walking, hearing, learning, caring for oneself)                                                                       | One of 13 disability categories (relevant ones include orthopedic impairment, hearing impairment, speech or language impairment, and other health impairment) *and* a need for special education         |
| **What it provides**                      | Mostly **accommodations** — changes to *how* a student accesses learning (more time, a different seat, step stools, a laptop) — but it can also include related services and special education when needed | **Specially designed instruction + related services** (speech therapy, OT, PT, counseling) + accommodations. Specific measurable goals are set and reviewed at least yearly.                              |
| **Who writes it**                         | School team (often a teacher, school counselor or 504 coordinator, and others who know the child)                                                                                                        | Formal IEP team including parents, general education teacher, special education teacher, and any specialists involved                                                                                     |
| **For most children with achondroplasia** | ✅ Often the right fit — physical accommodations (step stools, restroom access, adjusted seating, extra time, reduced writing load)                                                                       | ✅ When speech-language therapy, occupational therapy, or specialized instruction is also needed — often driven by speech delay from hearing loss or significant fine motor difficulties                   |
| **How to request it**                     | Written request to the school principal or 504 coordinator, citing the diagnosis and specific needs. <Callout type="tip" title="Always put requests in writing, and keep copies">
  Put every request in writing — email creates an immediate record. Under IDEA, the school must give you written notice when it agrees to or refuses an evaluation or a change to services; under Section 504, schools must have procedures that keep you informed and let you challenge decisions. Keep a folder of every request, response, evaluation report, plan, and meeting note. Having documentation is one of the most effective tools when disagreements arise. You are entitled to copies of your child's records.
</Callout>

<Callout type="caution" title="US enforcement has changed (checked 5 October 2026)">
  Your child's rights under IDEA and Section 504 haven't changed, but the agencies enforcing them have. Since June 2026, day-to-day oversight of special education programs (the Office of Special Education Programs) has moved from the Department of Education to the Department of Health and Human Services, and the Office for Civil Rights, which handles disability-discrimination complaints against schools, has moved to the Department of Justice. Expect slower responses to complaints. Your state's Parent Training and Information Center or disability Protection & Advocacy agency can help you navigate the current process.
</Callout>

## Outside the United States \{#outside-us}

Every country organizes school support differently. In brief:

- **England:** most children get help through **SEN support** in school. Children who need more than that can have an **Education, Health and Care (EHC) plan**, after an EHC needs assessment requested from the local council. A 2026 Schools White Paper proposes Individual Support Plans for most children with additional needs, with EHCPs kept for those needing more intensive support. This needs new legislation, and the government says no changes to support given by existing EHCPs will begin before September 2030.
- **Scotland:** support comes under additional support for learning law; children with complex or multiple needs requiring support from more than one agency may get a **Co-ordinated Support Plan (CSP)**. Enquire is the national advice service.
- **Wales:** children with additional learning needs have a right to an **Individual Development Plan (IDP)** under the ALN system.
- **Northern Ireland:** schools use a staged approach, and children with the most complex needs may get a **statement of special educational needs** from the Education Authority.
- **Australia:** the Disability Standards for Education 2005 require schools to make reasonable adjustments, in consultation with the student and family. The Australian achondroplasia guidelines (2023) include age-by-age school and community guidance.
- **Canada:** each province and territory sets its own special education rules; ask your school about an IEP or its local equivalent.

The UK 2026 achondroplasia guidelines suggest planning with nurseries and schools before your child starts, considering an individual education and healthcare plan in primary school, a psychology referral around age 7, and reviewing exam access arrangements (extra time, rest breaks, a scribe or laptop) in secondary school.

## The accommodation list: what to ask for \{#accommodations-list}

A well-written 504 plan (or IEP, EHC plan, or local equivalent) for a child with achondroplasia typically includes several categories of accommodation. Use this as a starting point — not every child needs every item, and you may find things to add based on your child's specific situation:

### Physical access

- Step stools at desk, sink, toilet, blackboard/whiteboard, drinking fountain, and coat hooks
- Classroom located on the ground floor, or elevator access confirmed and functional
- Door handles lowered or a chain/rope extender added for emergency egress (critical — see [Everyday Life](/daily-living#school-work))
- Chair and desk sized appropriately, or a standard desk with a secure footrest so feet never dangle
- Extra passing time between classes (short stride means more time to cover distance)
- Rest break or seating permitted on field trips, PE, and any long walking activities
- Canteen/cafeteria: tray assistance if needed; accessible counter height

### Hearing and sensory access

- Preferential seating — front and center of the classroom, facing the teacher, away from doors and windows
- Personal FM system or sound-field amplification system if audiologist recommends it — a microphone worn by the teacher transmits directly to a receiver worn by the child, making the teacher's voice stand out from background noise
- Teacher to face the child when speaking (important for lip-reading and auditory clarity)
- Written instructions provided for any complex multi-step task
- Quiet exam environment — a separate room or small group setting significantly reduces noise-related listening load

### Writing and fine motor

- Reduced writing load — core ideas over volume; permission to write in point form where appropriate
- Access to a keyboard/laptop for longer written tasks (particularly beneficial given hypermobile finger joints that tire with prolonged handwriting)
- Extra time on written assessments (typically 25–50% extended time)
- Oral alternatives to written responses for some assessments
- Pencil grips or weighted pen trials on recommendation of occupational therapist

### Fatigue management

- Permission to leave class 2 minutes early to avoid crowded hallways (reduces navigation time and physical jostling)
- A second set of textbooks at home (heavy backpack is a real physical issue for short stature)
- Scheduled rest breaks during long assessment sessions
- Rolling backpack or locker access permitted at any time (not locked to one break period)

### Physical education

- Adaptive PE — full participation in modified activities; documentation of excluded activities (trampolines, contact sports, diving) with safe alternatives provided
- Participation in all non-excluded activities fully and without a note for each session (a standing plan is better than daily medical notes)
- Seating available for any activity requiring extended standing

## Speech-language services at school \{#speech-at-school}

If a speech-language delay has been identified, school-based speech-language therapy is available as a related service under an IEP — at no cost to the family. School-based SLP is not the same as private SLP, and the two are often used in combination:

- **School-based SLP** typically focuses on skills that directly affect classroom learning: following verbal instructions, participating in classroom discussion, intelligible speech for peer interaction. Sessions are usually 30–45 minutes, 2–3 times per week during the school day. The SLP works with the classroom teacher.
- **Private SLP** (covered by insurance where the policy permits) can offer more intensive, individualized sessions, particularly useful for catching up language skills or for specific articulation goals. Private and school-based SLP can and should be coordinated to avoid duplication.
- The school must provide SLP if the child qualifies for an IEP and speech-language needs are identified in the evaluation. You do not have to pay for it.

## Occupational therapy at school \{#occupational-therapy}

Occupational therapy (OT) at school focuses on the fine motor, sensory, and self-care skills that enable school participation. For children with achondroplasia, OT may address:

- Handwriting endurance and grip adaptations (pencil grips, appropriate seating height and desk height)
- Keyboard skills and assistive technology introduction
- Toileting independence at school — a critical milestone, and an OT can consult with the school on equipment setup (step stools, seat adaptors, wiping aids)
- Scissor and craft skills — hypermobile finger joints and shorter fingers make standard scissors harder; loop scissors or spring-loaded scissors help
- Self-management of clothing (dressing for PE, fasteners) when reach and joint differences make this harder

School-based OT is available under an IEP as a related service. As with SLP, it requires a formal evaluation to establish eligibility.

## Assistive technology for learning \{#assistive-tech}

Assistive technology (AT) encompasses any tool that helps a student with a disability access learning more effectively. For children with achondroplasia, the most useful categories are:

  <Card title="Voice-to-text software">
    Dictation tools (Apple Dictation, Google Docs Voice Typing, Dragon NaturallySpeaking) convert spoken language to text, removing the physical writing burden entirely. Effective for children whose cognitive output is strong but writing pace is slow. Requires practice and a quiet environment.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Word prediction">
    Software that predicts the next word as the student types (Co:Writer, WordQ, Read\&Write) reduces the number of keystrokes for written tasks and helps children who have accurate ideas but slow or effortful written output.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Text-to-speech">
    Software that reads text aloud (Natural Reader, Read\&Write, Snap\&Read) supports reading comprehension for children whose decoding is effortful — particularly if hearing loss affected early literacy instruction.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Digital note-taking">
    Tablets with stylus input or keyboards allow children to take notes without the fatigue of prolonged handwriting. Many schools now have 1:1 device programs; an IEP or 504 can formalize the right to use a device when others cannot.
  </Card>

  <Card title="FM amplification systems">
    A microphone worn by the teacher transmits directly to a receiver worn by the child. For children with mild or fluctuating hearing loss (from recurrent ear fluid), these remote-microphone systems can make speech much easier to follow in noisy classrooms. Ask your audiologist, and request one through the school plan if it is recommended.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Adapted stationery">
    Weighted pens, pencil grips, slanted writing boards, spring-loaded scissors, and rulers with handles — low-tech tools that reduce the physical burden of fine motor tasks. Inexpensive and often highly effective.
  </Card>

## Higher education and university \{#college}

Adults with achondroplasia go on to college, university, and every kind of career; the AAP (2020) encourages families to set education and career goals high. Higher education brings different rules:

- **The legal framework changes (US).** The K-12 obligation to identify and proactively provide support (under IDEA) does not apply to colleges and universities; Section 504 and the Americans with Disabilities Act still do. In higher education, the student must disclose their disability to the institution's disability services office and request accommodations proactively. The institution must then provide reasonable accommodations, but it is the student's responsibility to initiate the process.
- **What to bring:** a letter from your physician documenting the diagnosis and any functional limitations (hearing loss, fine motor limitations, fatigue, joint limitations), plus a history of accommodations used in school. The disability services office uses this to determine equivalent accommodations.
- **Common university accommodations:** extended time on exams, a separate quiet exam room, permission to use a laptop, note-taking assistance, priority registration (so you can select classes with accessible buildings and reasonable distances between them), accessible housing assignment, and a parking permit.
- **Physical access:** the Americans with Disabilities Act requires universities to provide accessible facilities. This includes accessible bathrooms, accessible lecture hall seating (step stools if needed), elevator access, and reasonable modifications to shared housing. Report any inaccessible facility to the disability services office immediately — they are obligated to address it.
- **Self-advocacy is the skill:** the most important preparation for university is a student who can explain their needs clearly and confidently. The teen years are the time to build this muscle. Practice in high school by having the student speak for themselves in IEP and 504 meetings, by age and ability.

## When to investigate further: distinguishing hearing/sleep from true learning differences \{#when-to-worry}

The decision tree, simplified:

1. **Is hearing normal and well managed?** A hearing test within the past year (the AAP recommends yearly testing), with any fluid or hearing loss treated. If not → address hearing, and reassess academic performance.
2. **Is sleep apnea treated or ruled out?** Ask about symptoms (snoring, pauses, restless sleep, daytime sleepiness) and arrange a sleep study if they are present. If apnea is found → treat it, and reassess.
3. **Are physical fatigue and pain being managed?** If back or joint pain is affecting school performance → arrange medical review, and reassess.
4. **Is there still a significant academic gap, or were the difficulties clear from the start?** Then a formal psychoeducational evaluation by a neuropsychologist or educational psychologist is appropriate. This will provide standardized test scores, identify specific areas of strength and difficulty, and form the basis for targeted intervention and formal eligibility for support services.

What a proper psychoeducational assessment covers in achondroplasia: cognitive ability testing (IQ — usually in the normal range); academic achievement in reading, writing, and mathematics; processing speed; working memory; auditory processing (important given the hearing history); and executive function. Request that the evaluator is aware of the achondroplasia diagnosis and adjusts administration and interpretation accordingly — particularly for timed subtests where physical fatigue or writing speed may artificially depress scores.

<Callout type="tip" title="A word on labeling">
  A formal diagnosis (whether ADHD, auditory processing disorder, or a specific learning disability) unlocks legal protections and school support that "struggling" without a label does not. Parents sometimes resist a label for fear of stigma; this is understandable but often counterproductive — the accommodation the label unlocks is worth more than the label costs. Equally, make sure hearing and sleep have been considered before an ADHD diagnosis is settled, so the diagnosis reflects your child's actual profile. The goal is the most accurate understanding of what is making school hard, and the most targeted intervention — not the fastest label.
</Callout>
