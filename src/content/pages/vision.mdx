<Callout type="info" title="The summary">
  None of the main achondroplasia guidelines — the International Consensus Statement (2022), the AAP report (2020), or the UK guidelines (2026) — recommends routine specialist eye checks, and eye problems in achondroplasia have been described mostly in individual case reports. So: make sure your child has the standard vision screening every child gets, see an eye specialist promptly for any squint or vision worry, and know the urgent signs below. Children being watched for raised brain pressure should have their optic nerves checked as their neurosurgical team advises.
</Callout>

## Eye checks: what to expect \{#ophthalmology-schedule}

Because there is no achondroplasia-specific eye schedule, the practical plan follows standard child vision screening (as set out, for example, in the American Academy of Ophthalmology's pediatric guidelines), plus extra checks when raised pressure is a concern:

| Age                            | What should happen                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Birth / first weeks**        | Red reflex check as part of the newborn exam — same as any baby. A white or absent reflex needs urgent eye referral.                                                                                                                                              |
| **Infancy and preschool**      | Routine vision and eye-alignment checks at standard well-child or health-visitor reviews, and vision screening at the usual preschool ages in your country.                                                                                                       |
| **School age**                 | Standard school or optometrist vision checks. Prescriptions can change quickly in school-age children who wear glasses.                                                                                                                                            |
| **Any time: symptom-driven**   | A new squint, eyes not working together, double vision, headaches, brief greyings of vision, or worsening vision in one eye — prompt eye review, without waiting for the next routine check.                                                                      |
| **If raised pressure is a concern** | Children with hydrocephalus, or being monitored for raised pressure or foramen magnum compression, may need regular optic-nerve checks — your neurosurgical team will advise how often.                                                                    |
| **Adulthood**                  | Standard adult eye care — a routine optometrist check every couple of years, or as advised.                                                                                                                                                                        |

## Urgent eye signs: the setting-sun sign and papilledema \{#urgent-eye-signs}

Two eye findings need urgent — not routine — medical review. Know them and act on them.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Tier 1 — seek same-day urgent review for these eye signs">
  **"Setting-sun" sign:** the eyes are pushed downward so that a rim of white is visible above the colored part of the eye, like a sun setting. In a baby with achondroplasia, this is a classic sign of raised pressure inside the head, for example from [hydrocephalus](/first-years#big-head). It needs same-day urgent assessment and imaging, not a routine appointment.

  **Papilledema:** swelling of the optic disc (where the optic nerve meets the back of the eye) caused by raised pressure inside the head. It can't be seen without an eye examination. Symptoms that suggest raised pressure in an older child or adult include headaches (especially on waking), brief greyings or blackouts of vision lasting seconds, a whooshing sound in the ears in time with the heartbeat, and in advanced cases loss of vision. If any of these appear, seek same-day medical review.
</Callout>

**In babies, a normal eye exam doesn't rule out raised pressure.** Before the skull bones fuse, the head can expand to absorb some of the extra pressure, so papilledema often doesn't develop until late. In infants, watch instead for a fast-growing head, a tense or bulging soft spot (fontanelle), vomiting, irritability or sleepiness — see [Head Size & Hydrocephalus](/first-years#big-head).

Papilledema matters for another reason: pressure that stays high for a long time — even without obvious symptoms — can gradually damage the optic nerve and cause permanent loss of side vision. That's why children with known hydrocephalus or suspected raised pressure need their optic nerves checked as part of their monitoring.

## Vision monitoring in hydrocephalus and raised pressure \{#vision-and-pressure}

Hydrocephalus needing treatment is uncommon in achondroplasia today. In a US study of 1,374 people, 9% had been treated for it over 60 years, but in the most recent decade the four centers treated fewer than 6% of their patients (2.9% on average). For children who do have hydrocephalus or are being monitored for raised pressure, the eye team works alongside neurosurgery:

- Regular dilated examination of the back of the eye to look for papilledema or signs of long-term optic-nerve damage (a pale optic disc).
- Visual field testing (simple tests in young children, computerized field testing from about age 7) to detect loss of side vision.
- Optical coherence tomography (OCT) — a quick, painless scan of the back of the eye that can detect and track subtle optic-disc swelling.
- The treatment for optic-nerve damage from raised pressure is treating the cause (a shunt or endoscopic third ventriculostomy for hydrocephalus, or decompression surgery at the foramen magnum when that is the problem) — not eye surgery.

## Strabismus (misaligned eyes) \{#strabismus}

Strabismus — a squint, crossed eyes or wandering eye — means the two eyes don't point in the same direction at the same time. It has been reported in children with achondroplasia, but no good-quality study has measured how common it is, so we can't give a reliable figure. Inward turning (esotropia) and outward turning (exotropia) are both possible.

### Why it matters: amblyopia risk

When one eye is persistently misaligned, the brain starts ignoring the image from that eye to avoid double vision. If this continues during early childhood, when vision is still developing (roughly up to age 7–8), that eye's vision may never develop fully — this is **amblyopia** ("lazy eye"). Amblyopia is the commonest cause of reduced vision in one eye in children, and it is largely preventable with early treatment. This is why a squint is not just a cosmetic issue.

The good news: when strabismus and amblyopia are found early and treated consistently, most children achieve good vision.

### Treatment

- **Glasses:** many squints in children are driven by uncorrected long-sightedness, which makes the eyes work harder to focus and pulls them inward. Glasses often straighten the eyes without any further treatment.
- **Patching or eye drops:** if amblyopia has developed, the stronger eye is covered with a patch for part of each day, or blurred with atropine eye drops, to make the weaker eye work. It's tedious but effective, and it has to be done consistently while vision is still developing.
- **Surgery:** if glasses alone don't fully straighten the eyes, surgery on the eye muscles can realign them. It is done under general anesthesia, so the full [anesthesia checklist](/warning-signs#surgery-checklist) for achondroplasia applies, including airway precautions.
- **Prism glasses:** sometimes used in older children and adults with a small remaining misalignment.

## Refractive errors

Refractive errors — needing glasses or contact lenses to see clearly — include short-sightedness (myopia), long-sightedness (hyperopia), and astigmatism (blur from an unevenly curved eye surface). Some reports suggest they may be more common in achondroplasia, but there is no reliable data on rates. They are easily corrected with glasses; the main challenges are spotting them early, before amblyopia develops, and keeping glasses on young children.

### Glasses in children with achondroplasia: practical notes

Standard children's frames may sit poorly on a flatter nasal bridge — the nose is the main anchor point for most frames. Some practical solutions:

- Frames with **cable temples** (arms that curve around the ear) grip better without relying on the nose.
- **Adjustable silicone nose pads** and "Asian-fit" or low-bridge frames are designed for flatter nasal bridges and often work well.
- **Straps or headbands** hold glasses in place reliably in babies and toddlers.
- An optician experienced in fitting children with craniofacial differences is the best resource. Frames that won't stay on won't correct anything.
- Contact lenses can be considered from adolescence, following standard guidance — there are no achondroplasia-specific reasons to avoid them.

## Nystagmus

Nystagmus is an involuntary, rhythmic movement of the eyes. It isn't a typical feature of achondroplasia, so if it appears it needs a full eye and neurological assessment to find the cause. Nystagmus itself is not painful.

## Vision in adulthood \{#adult-vision}

There's no evidence of higher rates of glaucoma, macular degeneration or other adult eye diseases in achondroplasia, so standard adult eye care applies. Two points to know:

- **Leftover squint:** adults who had a squint in childhood may have a small remaining misalignment. If it causes double vision or bothers you cosmetically, adult squint surgery is available and effective.
- **High short-sightedness:** as for anyone with high myopia, there is a higher risk of retinal detachment. Seek urgent eye review for new flashes of light, a shower of floaters, or a shadow over part of your vision. Laser eye surgery (LASIK, PRK) is available by the same criteria as for anyone; it doesn't usually need a general anesthetic.

## What to tell the ophthalmologist \{#what-to-tell-ophthalmologist}

A brief written summary helps any eye specialist who hasn't seen achondroplasia before:

<Callout type="tip" title="Briefing note for the eye team">
  **Diagnosis:** achondroplasia (FGFR3 skeletal dysplasia). **Key points:** (1) Eye findings in achondroplasia are not well characterized; please perform a standard full assessment, including cycloplegic refraction and alignment. (2) The nasal bridge is flat — standard spectacle frames often don't sit securely, so frame selection matters. (3) If there is a history of ventriculomegaly, hydrocephalus, foramen magnum compression, or suspected raised intracranial pressure, please examine and document the optic discs and alert the neurosurgical team if papilledema is suspected. Note that papilledema may be absent in infants with open sutures. (4) Any procedure under general anesthesia needs difficult-airway precautions — please brief the anesthetic team in advance. (5) "Setting-sun" eye deviation in an infant needs same-day escalation.
</Callout>

## Why achondroplasia might affect the eyes \{#why-eyes-affected}

This is less certain than the practical advice above. Achondroplasia changes the shape of the skull base and midface, which form the bony eye sockets, so it is plausible that eye alignment could be affected — but this hasn't been well studied. The clearer link is pressure: narrowing at the skull base (the foramen magnum and the openings that drain blood from the head) can contribute to enlarged fluid spaces and, in a minority, hydrocephalus. Raised pressure is what causes the urgent eye signs described above.
