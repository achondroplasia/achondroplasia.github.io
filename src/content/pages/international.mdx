## Getting access when not yet approved: the options \{#access-routes}

Regulatory approval does not always equal access — reimbursement (insurance/government funding) is a separate step. And before approval, several pathways may make a drug available:

### Compassionate use / Named-patient access

Most regulatory agencies (FDA, EMA, MHRA, TGA) have a mechanism for providing unapproved medicines to individual patients with serious conditions when no approved alternative exists. The process typically requires:

- A physician at a specialist center submitting a formal compassionate-use application on the patient's behalf
- The manufacturer agreeing to supply the drug (usually free of charge)
- Regulatory agency approval (usually a faster process than full approval)

In the EU, the EMA's "compassionate use" mechanism under Article 83 of Regulation 726/2004 applies to medicines that have a pending or approved marketing authorization application. BioMarin and Ascendis both have access programs in countries where their drugs are not yet approved — your treating physician can contact the companies directly.

### Early access / Managed access programs

In the UK, the medicines regulator (MHRA) runs an **Early Access to Medicines Scheme (EAMS)** for promising medicines before they are licensed, and NICE can use **Managed Access Agreements** to fund a drug while more data are collected. Neither route has been used for vosoritide: it is not licensed in Great Britain, and NICE's appraisal is suspended. For now, UK families' main route to growth drugs is a clinical trial.

### Clinical trial participation

Enrolling in an active clinical trial gives access to the experimental drug at no cost. See the [Research & Trials page](/research) for how to find and evaluate open trials in your region.

### Paying out-of-pocket / medical tourism

List prices for vosoritide and navepegritide run US$300,000–500,000 per year. This is not a realistic option for most families. Medical tourism — travelling to a country where the drug is approved and reimbursed — is not practically feasible for long-term chronic medications. What is feasible: travelling to a country with a compassionate-use program to begin access there, then working to establish access domestically. Patient organizations in your country have experience navigating this.

## Traveling internationally for specialist care \{#traveling-for-care}

Families in countries without specialist centers or approved drugs sometimes consider traveling for care. Key guidance:

- **For drug access:** in general, obtaining a multi-month supply of a drug in one country and bringing it across borders is legally complicated and often prohibited. The better route is establishing ongoing compassionate access in your home country, with the specialist center as the applicant.
- **For specialist evaluation:** a one-time trip to a major center for evaluation, imaging review, and a written management plan is a realistic option for families with means. The written plan can then be implemented by local providers. Johns Hopkins, CHOP, and Sheffield all see international patients; RCH Melbourne sees patients from across the Asia-Pacific region.
- **For surgery:** spinal surgery, cervicomedullary decompression, and orthopedic procedures in achondroplasia should be done at centers with extensive dysplasia experience. Traveling to a specialist center for a specific surgery and then recovering locally under local care — with detailed written handover notes — is a pattern that families in underserved regions use successfully.
- **Medical tourism risks:** "medical tourism" for unproven treatments (limb-lengthening in facilities that do not specialize in dysplasias, or unproven cell therapies) carries real risks. See the scam section on the [Treatments page](/treatments#what-doesnt-work).
- **Documentation for travel:** carry complete medical records in English (or with translations), including your FGFR3 genetic report, MRI reports, sleep studies, and a summary letter from your treating physician covering anesthesia precautions. Use the [ER quick card](/warning-signs#er-card) from the Warning Signs page as a travel document to hand to any emergency team.
