<Callout type="info" title="Real trials never charge you for the experimental drug">
  This one rule filters out virtually every scam. Legitimate clinical trials, registered on government databases and sponsored by pharmaceutical companies or universities, provide the study drug free of charge to participants. If anyone asks you to pay for an experimental treatment, it is not a legitimate clinical trial.
</Callout>

## Why research participation matters \{#why-matters}

The achondroplasia treatment landscape changed dramatically between 2021 and 2026 — from zero approved growth-modifying drugs to three in various stages of approval. None of this happened without the families and individuals with achondroplasia who enrolled in the trials. The three-phase progression from early safety studies to definitive randomized trials requires thousands of patient-years of participation, and in a rare condition affecting about 1 in 25,000 births, every participant matters.

Beyond growth drugs: natural history studies, quality-of-life registries, surgical outcome databases, and diagnostic studies all depend on participation. These studies do not involve any experimental drug — they are observation and data collection — and they form the evidence base that shapes every clinical guideline, every reimbursement decision, and every future trial.

## Types of studies and what each involves \{#types}

| Study type                          | What happens                                                                                                                                                                                                                          | Risk level                                     | Access to drug?                                                       |
| ----------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Natural history / observational** | No drug given. Researchers collect data on how the condition progresses — measurements, imaging, sleep studies, quality-of-life surveys — over time. Often the entry point that qualifies families for later interventional trials.   | Very low — same as standard medical care       | No (data collection only)                                             |
| **Phase 1**                         | First-in-human safety and dosing study. Small group of participants (often 10–50). Goal: is it safe, and what dose is tolerable? Usually open-label (everyone receives the drug).                                                     | Higher — unknown drug in humans for first time | Yes (everyone receives drug)                                          |
| **Phase 2**                         | Larger group (50–200). Tests whether the drug shows signs of working at the doses identified in Phase 1. May be randomized (some get drug, some get placebo) or open-label.                                                           | Moderate                                       | Usually yes, at least to some participants                            |
| **Phase 3 (Pivotal)**               | The decisive randomized trial. Large group (100–1000+). Randomized and usually double-blind (neither participant nor researcher knows who gets drug vs. placebo). This is what regulators use to make approval decisions.             | Better characterized than earlier phases       | 50–66% of participants typically receive drug; others receive placebo |
| **Open-label extension (OLE)**      | After the blinded phase ends, everyone is offered the active drug. Generates long-term safety data. Often continues for years.                                                                                                        | Low (drug already characterized)               | Yes — all participants                                                |
| **Registry / patient database**     | Long-term collection of real-world data from people receiving standard care or approved treatments outside a trial. No randomization, no placebo. Contributes to post-approval safety surveillance and natural history understanding. | Very low                                       | Standard clinical care only                                           |

## Currently active studies (August 2026) \{#current-trials}

<Callout type="caution" title="Verify current status before contacting study sites">
  Trial status changes — studies open, pause, reach enrollment, and close regularly. Always verify current status on [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov) (US) or [clinicaltrialsregister.eu](https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu) (EU). Search "achondroplasia" and filter by status: "Recruiting."
</Callout>

### Growth-modifying drug trials

- **PROPEL 3 Long-term Extension — Infigratinib (BridgeBio):** Children who completed the Phase 3 PROPEL 3 trial (published NEJM 2026) may be eligible for the open-label extension, continuing the drug while long-term data are collected. NCT number: check ClinicalTrials.gov for current PROPEL extension status. FDA NDA submitted September 2026; EU MAA targeted for Q4 2026; launch anticipated mid-2027 if approved.
- **BEACH301 — TYRA-300 / Dabogratinib (Tyra Biosciences):** Phase 2 trial of the next-generation FGFR3-selective oral inhibitor. First child dosed 2025. Ages 3–10 with open growth plates. Multiple US and international sites. NCT06842355. Recruiting.
- **Vosoritide infant at-risk trial (BioMarin):** Studying vosoritide in infants who are at risk of requiring cervicomedullary decompression — the key question of whether early treatment reduces surgery rates. NCT04554940. Check status.
- <span id="coach" />**COACH — Navepegritide + long-acting growth hormone combination (Ascendis):** A small (21 children), open-label phase 2 proof-of-concept study — not randomized, not yet a standard of care option. Peer-reviewed 52-week results (*European Journal of Endocrinology*, 2026) reported an annualized growth velocity around 8.4–8.8 cm/year on the combination, versus roughly 5–6 cm/year typically seen on either drug alone, plus gains in arm span and body proportion, with no new safety concerns through 78 weeks. The study's own stated purpose is to help design a future, larger pivotal trial — it is a promising early signal, not proof the combination works or is safe long-term.

### Natural history and quality-of-life studies

- **CLARITY-2 / CLARITY extension:** the ongoing natural history successor to the original CLARITY study (1,374 patients, 60-year database at Johns Hopkins, Nemours, UTHealth, and University of Wisconsin). Contact the Greenberg Center at Johns Hopkins to inquire about enrollment.
- **LISA (Lifetime Impact Study for Achondroplasia):** multinational observational quality-of-life study including Latin American cohorts. Contact your national patient organization or the coordinating site.
- **PROPEL 1 natural history run-in:** the observational run-in study that preceded the infigratinib trials collected natural history data from untreated children. Check whether an observational successor is open.

### Finding trials in your region

- **ClinicalTrials.gov:** the US registry; all trials with US sites must register here; most international trials also register voluntarily. Search: `achondroplasia`, filter Recruiting, filter your country. [Direct link](https://clinicaltrials.gov/search?cond=achondroplasia\&status=RECRUITING)
- **EU Clinical Trials Register:** [clinicaltrialsregister.eu](https://www.clinicaltrialsregister.eu) — covers EU trial sites
- **WHO ICTRP:** the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform aggregates registries from 19 countries: [who.int/clinical-trials-registry-platform](https://www.who.int/clinical-trials-registry-platform)
- **LPA Medical Advisory Board:** vets and announces trials to the community — check [lpaonline.org](https://www.lpaonline.org)
- **Beyond Achondroplasia:** publishes research news and trial information in multiple languages: [beyondachondroplasia.org](https://www.beyondachondroplasia.org)
- **The Chandler Project:** US family organization that tracks and explains research developments: [thechandlerproject.org](https://thechandlerproject.org/achondroplasia)

## How to evaluate a trial: 15 questions to ask \{#evaluating}

Before enrolling any family member in any study, get clear answers to these questions from the research team:

### About the study

1. **What is the study trying to find out?** What is the primary endpoint — what does the study measure to determine if the drug "works"?
2. **What phase is this study?** Phase 1 (safety only), Phase 2 (early efficacy), Phase 3 (definitive), or OLE?
3. **Is the study registered?** Find the NCT number (ClinicalTrials.gov) or EudraCT number (EU). If it is not registered, do not proceed.
4. **Who sponsors it?** The pharmaceutical company? A university? A government agency? Is this sponsor reputable?
5. **Has it been reviewed by an Institutional Review Board (IRB) or Ethics Committee?** The answer must be yes. Ask for the ethics committee name.
6. **Is there a Data Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB)?** This is an independent group that reviews safety data during the trial and can stop it if harm is found. Absence is a red flag.

### About participation

7. **What is the chance of receiving placebo, and for how long?** A blinded 12-month placebo-controlled trial in a growing child is a real consideration. What happens after the blinded period — is there an OLE?
8. **What tests and procedures are involved?** How often? How many blood draws, MRIs, sleep studies, clinic visits?
9. **Who pays for study-related costs?** Study drug, study-specific tests, and travel to study sites should be covered by the sponsor — not you. Get this in writing.
10. **What is the withdrawal policy?** You must be able to withdraw at any time without penalty. If the answer is anything else, do not proceed.
11. **Will participating affect our ability to access approved drugs?** Some trial exclusion criteria prevent concurrent use of approved treatments; some OLEs transition participants to commercial product after the trial.

### About safety and outcomes

12. **What are the known and theoretical risks?** For a new drug, what side effects were seen in pre-clinical studies and Phase 1/2?
13. **What monitoring is provided?** How will the team watch for side effects? Who to call if something happens at home?
14. **If my child is harmed by the study drug, what happens?** Study-related injury should be covered by the sponsor; get this in writing before signing.
15. **Will we get the results?** Ethical research shares results with participants. Ask when and how results will be communicated.

## Practical aspects of trial participation \{#practical}

- **Travel and logistics:** many skeletal dysplasia trials require visits to specialist centers that may be far from your home. Study coordinators can usually help with travel logistics; major sponsors often provide travel reimbursement. Remote/telehealth visits have become more common since COVID-19 and may reduce some visit burden.
- **School and work impact:** frequent clinic visits during school terms affect attendance. Request a visit schedule in advance so absence letters can be planned. Many families cluster visits around school holidays.
- **The emotional reality:** trial participation can be motivating and meaningful — and it can also be exhausting. Frequent appointments, uncertainty about which arm of the trial you are on, and the possibility that results are disappointing are all real. Connect with other trial families through LPA and the disease-specific patient organizations — peer support is as important during trial participation as during standard care.
- **After the trial ends:** ask before enrolling what happens when the study ends. Is there an OLE? Is the company committed to pursuing approval? What happens if the trial is discontinued for business reasons (as happened with Pfizer's recifercept in 2022)? These answers are never certain, but getting a sponsor's position in writing at the outset manages expectations.

## Contributing without a drug trial \{#non-drug-research}

Not every family wants to participate in a drug trial — and that is entirely legitimate. Non-interventional research is equally valuable and much lower burden:

- **Patient registries:** contributing longitudinal data (measurements, imaging, survey responses) to registries like CLARITY-2 or LISA costs only your time and shapes the guideline development that benefits everyone with achondroplasia.
- **Qualitative and patient-experience research:** researchers studying quality of life, patient-reported outcomes, body image, and lived experience actively need perspectives from people with achondroplasia and their families. These studies typically involve surveys or interviews — no medical procedures, no drugs.
- **Genetic and biobank studies:** contributing a blood sample or saliva swab to a biobank (stored for future research use) is a one-time low-burden contribution. Academic medical centers and commercial registries (e.g., GeneDx, Invitae research programs) run these.
- **Social media and community research:** some research groups analyze public social media data (with IRB approval and de-identification) to understand patient-community conversations. Participating in these does not require any active involvement — but knowing they exist helps explain why community discourse is increasingly studied.

<Callout type="tip" title="One thing that helps the entire field: attending national conferences">
  LPA's annual national conference regularly includes a medical clinic and a research day where investigators from the major achondroplasia centers are present, explain active studies, and can meet with families. The combination of clinical consultation, research information, and peer community is unique. Many trial investigators describe LPA national conference as the single most efficient research-outreach event available in this rare disease space.
</Callout>
