<Callout type="info" title="The bottom line up front">
  Bite problems (malocclusion) affect more than half of children with achondroplasia, according to the International Consensus — mainly because the upper jaw is small and set back. The UK 2026 guidelines recommend a check with a paediatric dentist by 12 months and a review by an orthodontist familiar with achondroplasia at about age 10. The good news: most dental problems are treatable, and in the small number of published cases, orthodontic treatment started before the end of growth corrected the bite without surgery.
</Callout>

## Why achondroplasia affects the teeth \{#why-different}

The key anatomy: the skull in achondroplasia has two differently affected parts. The vault (top and sides of the skull) grows almost normally, because it forms by intramembranous ossification — a process not greatly impaired by overactive FGFR3. But the **skull base** (the bottom layer the face sits on) grows by cartilage-based (endochondral) ossification, the same process that is slowed everywhere else. The result: a relatively small skull base produces a relatively small, set-back **midface** — the cheekbones, nose, and upper jaw (maxilla). The lower jaw (mandible) is affected too: a 2023 3D imaging study found both jaws set back and a shorter lower jaw than average, with the differences becoming more marked with age. But the upper jaw is usually the more set back of the two.

This mismatch — an upper jaw that is more set back than the lower — is the root cause of most of the dental issues on this page. Teeth growing in a small upper jaw tend to be crowded, misaligned, and poorly positioned relative to the lower arch. The same study found that the more set back the jaws were, the more severe a child's obstructive sleep apnea tended to be.

## Malocclusion: the bite problems \{#malocclusion}

Malocclusion means a bite that does not line up properly. The characteristic patterns in achondroplasia flow directly from the skull-base anatomy:

### Class III malocclusion (underbite)

A frequently reported pattern: the lower teeth sit in front of the upper teeth when biting, because the upper jaw is more set back than the lower. Exact rates aren't known — published reports are small — but malocclusion of some kind affects more than half of children with achondroplasia. The bite discrepancy ranges from mild (a slightly edge-to-edge relationship) to severe (the lower teeth clearly in front). The midface tends to look more set back with age, so the bite can worsen through childhood and adolescence.

### Crossbite

When upper back teeth fall inside the lower back teeth rather than outside — a consequence of the narrow upper jaw. Both posterior (back) and anterior (front) crossbites occur. Palatal expansion (a fixed orthodontic device that gradually widens the upper jaw) can address this while the upper jaw is still growing; it works best at younger ages, before the midline suture of the palate matures in the teenage years.

### Open bite

When front teeth do not meet at all when the back teeth are closed — common when mouth breathing is also present (below), which can alter the resting tongue position and change how the teeth erupt.

### Overjet vs. overbite reversal

In typical dentitions, the upper front teeth sit slightly in front of and overlap the lower front teeth. In achondroplasia the relationship is often reversed: lower teeth protrude (negative overjet) and the lower front teeth close over the upper (reverse overbite). This can cause enamel wear on the upper front teeth and difficulty biting into food.

## Crowding and late eruption \{#crowding}

Teeth are broadly normal-sized, but the jaw they sit in is smaller. The predictable result is **dental crowding**: teeth that overlap, rotate, and push each other out of position. Crowded teeth are harder to clean, increasing the risk of decay and gum disease, and they often require extraction of teeth (including permanent teeth in some cases) to make room for orthodontic alignment.

### Delayed tooth eruption

Some families notice teeth coming through later than average, and crowding can make permanent teeth emerge late or in an unusual order. Eruption timing hasn't been well studied in achondroplasia, so ask your dentist if you are worried. If crowding is a concern, your dentist may suggest a **dental panoramic X-ray (OPG)** in the early mixed-dentition phase (around ages 6–8). It gives the treating dentist and orthodontist a map of all developing permanent teeth and helps time any extractions or orthodontic interventions.

### Supernumerary and retained teeth

Baby teeth that don't fall out on time, and occasionally extra (supernumerary) teeth, can complicate crowding. An OPG shows these and guides the treatment sequence.

## Mouth breathing and its dental consequences \{#mouth-breathing}

Narrow nasal passages (from midface hypoplasia) and the very high prevalence of [obstructive sleep apnea](/childhood#sleep) in achondroplasia mean that many children and adults breathe predominantly through the mouth rather than the nose. Mouth breathing has its own dental and facial consequences:

- **Altered tongue posture:** a mouth-breather's tongue rests on the floor of the mouth rather than pressing against the roof. That upper tongue pressure is what naturally widens the upper arch; without it, the arch narrows further and crowding worsens.
- **Dental caries (tooth decay):** a dry mouth from mouth breathing reduces saliva flow, which is the mouth's main protection against decay-causing bacteria. This is why mouth breathers may be at higher risk of cavities.
- **Gum disease:** for the same reason — dry, irritated gums are more susceptible to gingivitis.
- **Anterior open bite development:** mouth breathing with a low tongue position can cause the front teeth to erupt less completely, creating or worsening an open bite.

**The implication:** treating sleep apnea (adenotonsillectomy, CPAP) is not only a respiratory and neurological intervention — it has real downstream dental benefits. Tell your orthodontist about any history of sleep apnea and whether it has been treated, because it affects how the orthodontic plan is timed and designed.

## Orthodontic treatment: what to expect and when \{#orthodontics}

Orthodontic treatment in achondroplasia broadly follows the same phases as in the general population, with timing shaped by the jaw discrepancy. The evidence is thin: a 2026 systematic review found only 13 published reports (16 treated people, mostly single case reports). In that small group, five children aged 10–16 treated with jaw-guiding (orthopaedic) appliances followed by braces had a stable bite at least two years later without extractions or surgery, while most people treated after growth had finished needed extractions or jaw surgery.

### Phase 1 (early interceptive) — approximately ages 6–10

The goal of early treatment is to address problems that *worsen* if left untreated until all permanent teeth are in — primarily crossbites and severe crowding. Common Phase 1 appliances:

- **Palatal expander (RPE or Hyrax):** a fixed device bonded to the upper back teeth, activated daily by a key to widen the upper jaw while the midpalatal suture is still open. Works best at younger ages, before the suture matures in the teenage years. Can correct a posterior crossbite and create room for crowded teeth.
- **Space maintainers:** if baby teeth are lost early, a fixed spacer prevents neighboring teeth from drifting into the gap before the permanent tooth is ready.
- **Selective extractions:** removing specific baby teeth to guide erupting permanent teeth into better positions.

Not everyone needs Phase 1; the orthodontist's assessment determines who benefits from early intervention. The AAP (2020) suggests discussing future braces and the possible need for palatal expansion with families from the preschool years.

### Phase 2 (comprehensive) — typically ages 12–18

Full fixed braces or aligners after most or all permanent teeth have erupted. In achondroplasia the Class III bite complicates this phase: standard orthodontics can align the teeth within each arch, but it cannot change the skeletal position of the jaws. **When the jaw discrepancy is mild to moderate,** orthodontics alone (sometimes with extraction of upper teeth to allow the upper arch to compensate somewhat) gives a functional, acceptable result. **When the discrepancy is severe,** orthodontics alone cannot achieve a normal bite — which leads to the next section.

### The timing constraint: jaw growth must be complete

The lower jaw (mandible) keeps growing until the mid-to-late teens. **Definitive orthodontic treatment or jaw surgery should generally wait until jaw growth is complete**, confirmed by comparing two jaw X-rays (lateral cephalograms) taken 6–12 months apart. Starting too early means the bite can relapse as the jaw continues to grow.

## Orthognathic (jaw) surgery \{#jaw-surgery}

When the jaw discrepancy is skeletal and severe enough that orthodontics alone cannot produce a functional bite, **orthognathic surgery** (literally "straight jaw" surgery) moves the bones of the upper jaw, the lower jaw, or both into better positions. This is elective surgery on the jaw skeleton, done in a hospital by a maxillofacial surgeon working in coordination with an orthodontist.

For achondroplasia specifically:

- A common operation is **Le Fort I maxillary advancement** — moving the upper jaw forward and/or downward to better match the lower jaw.
- **Distraction osteogenesis of the midface** (gradual forward pulling using an internal or external device, similar in principle to limb lengthening) has been reported mainly in young children with severe airway obstruction; in the 2026 review, two preschool children treated this way were able to have their tracheostomy tubes removed. It is not a routine alternative for bite correction.
- Jaw and midface surgery has also been used to treat severe obstructive sleep apnea in a small number of people with achondroplasia.
- Timing is after jaw growth is complete and after orthodontic preparation is in place (braces are worn before surgery to level and align the teeth within each arch).
- Postoperative orthodontic refinement typically runs for 6–12 months after surgery.

**Honest caveats:** The published evidence for jaw surgery in achondroplasia is limited to case reports and small series — there are no randomized trials. In the 2026 review, five people had jaw surgery after growth was complete, but long-term stability was documented in only two of the eight post-growth patients. Choose an experienced center and go in with realistic expectations. The decision belongs in a joint conversation between a maxillofacial surgeon, an orthodontist, and the patient and family — with psychological support, since this is major elective facial surgery.

**Growth-promoting treatments:** a small 2025 study found a slight change towards a more typical face shape in children aged 5–10 on vosoritide. Whether this changes dental or orthodontic needs is not yet known.

**Anesthesia planning is mandatory.** The achondroplasia airway (midface hypoplasia, limited neck extension, small nasal passages) makes any oral or maxillofacial procedure involving general anesthesia higher-risk. Every procedure requiring sedation or general anesthesia — including routine dental procedures under GA for young children — must be done at a center where the anesthesiology team is familiar with the skeletal dysplasia airway. See [the anesthesia checklist on the Warning Signs page](/warning-signs#surgery-checklist).

## What to tell your dentist — at every appointment \{#what-to-tell-your-dentist}

Many general dentists will have treated few, if any, patients with achondroplasia. A short, matter-of-fact briefing at the first appointment sets up every subsequent visit. The key points to convey:

- **"My child has achondroplasia — a skeletal dysplasia that affects jaw and facial bone growth. The midface is smaller than typical, which means crowding and a misaligned bite are expected, not unusual for this population."**
- **Anesthesia alert:** if any procedure might require sedation or general anesthesia — including dental work under GA, which is common in young children who cannot cooperate — the anesthesiologist must be briefed about the difficult airway (midface hypoplasia, small nasal passages, limited neck extension). See the [emergency card on the Warning Signs page](/warning-signs#er-card). Written documentation helps.
- **Sleep apnea history:** whether it has been diagnosed and treated, because it affects mouth breathing, saliva flow, and orthodontic planning.
- **Hearing history:** mention any hearing loss, so the dentist can face your child and speak clearly during treatment.
- **Current medications:** if the child is on vosoritide (Voxzogo) or navepegritide (Yuviwel), mention it. No interactions with local anesthetics or common dental drugs are known, though none have been formally studied. Both can cause a temporary drop in blood pressure; with vosoritide, eating and drinking before the injection helps, so it's worth planning appointment times around the daily dose.

<Callout type="tip" title="Practical tip: request referral to a pediatric dentist experienced with special needs">
  Pediatric dentists (paediatric dental specialists) — particularly those who practice in hospital or academic settings — see more patients with craniofacial and skeletal conditions and will be more familiar with achondroplasia than a typical community dentist. Ask your skeletal dysplasia clinic for a dental referral by your child's first birthday (the UK 2026 guidelines recommend a paediatric dentist check by 12 months), before dental problems develop. The [community organizations](/wellbeing#organizations) (LPA, Beyond Achondroplasia) often maintain informal referral lists built from families' own experience.
</Callout>

## Daily oral hygiene — adapted for small hands and hypermobile fingers \{#home-care}

Children with achondroplasia have shorter fingers and hypermobile finger joints, which can make the fine motor task of toothbrushing harder. A few practical adaptations:

- **Electric (powered) toothbrushes** reduce the manual dexterity required and are as effective as, or better than, manual brushing — particularly for children who struggle with the coordination of circular brushing. Start with a soft, child-sized electric brush around age 3.
- **Build-up the brush handle** with foam tubing (available from occupational therapy suppliers) if grip is a problem for older children and adults.
- **Water flossers** (Waterpik-type devices) are an effective alternative to string floss for people with limited dexterity; they are particularly useful in crowded dentitions and around fixed orthodontic appliances.
- **Fluoride toothpaste:** UK guidance is a smear of toothpaste with at least 1,000 ppm fluoride under age 3, a pea-sized amount from age 3, and standard family-strength paste (1,350–1,500 ppm) for older children and anyone at higher risk of decay. Prescription high-fluoride pastes (2,800 or 5,000 ppm) are for older children and adults at high risk — your dentist will advise.
- **Parental involvement** in tooth cleaning should continue until the child can reliably clean their own teeth — in achondroplasia, fine motor development follows its own timetable; some children need parental assistance for tooth cleaning longer than same-age peers.

## Dental considerations in adulthood \{#adults}

Adults who did not receive orthodontic treatment in childhood — or who had treatment but experienced relapse — may present with significant malocclusion that affects chewing efficiency, jaw joint (TMJ) comfort, and facial appearance. Options include:

- **Removable clear aligners** (e.g., Invisalign) for mild to moderate crowding and bite issues in adults — particularly useful for adults who prefer not to wear visible fixed braces.
- **Fixed adult orthodontics** for moderate-to-severe cases, combined with maxillofacial surgical consultation if jaw surgery is also indicated.
- **Prosthodontic restoration** (crowns, bridges, implants) for worn or damaged teeth from long-term malocclusion.
- **TMJ assessment** if there is jaw clicking, pain, limited opening, or headaches — chronic malocclusion can load the jaw joint asymmetrically.
- **Dry mouth management:** adults with untreated obstructive sleep apnea may have significant xerostomia (dry mouth), increasing cavity risk. In addition to addressing the apnea itself, saliva substitutes, prescription-fluoride toothpaste, regular fluoride varnish applications, and dietary counseling (reducing sugary snacks) all help.

## Dental procedures and anesthesia \{#anesthesia-dental}

Many young children — and some adults — have dental treatment done under general anesthesia or intravenous sedation. In achondroplasia this is higher-risk than in the general population and must be planned carefully:

- Midface hypoplasia means bag-mask ventilation is often difficult (the mask does not seal well over a flat nasal bridge).
- The nasal passages are narrow — nasal intubation, which is preferred in many oral surgical procedures because it keeps the oral cavity clear, may require a smaller tube than expected.
- Neck extension for intubation must be minimized because of the narrow [foramen magnum](/understanding#fgfr3).
- Where possible, **maxillofacial procedures requiring GA should be done at a hospital center** (not a standalone community dental clinic) with a pediatric or adult anesthesiologist who has experience with craniofacial or skeletal dysplasia cases.
- The [pre-surgery anesthesia checklist on the Warning Signs page](/warning-signs#surgery-checklist) applies fully to dental procedures under GA.

<Callout type="caution" title="Caution: nitrous oxide (laughing gas) and conscious sedation">
  Nitrous oxide (laughing gas, used for anxious dental patients) is widely used, but there are no achondroplasia-specific safety data, and any sedation can relax the airway — a particular concern with untreated sleep apnea. The treating team should know the diagnosis and have resuscitation equipment accessible. Oral sedation (midazolam), IV sedation, and general anesthesia all invoke the full airway precautions described on the [Warning Signs page](/warning-signs). Always inform the treating dentist and any sedationist of the diagnosis before the appointment — not at the appointment.
</Callout>
