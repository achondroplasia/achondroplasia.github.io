## Take a breath: the overview \{#overview}

Start with what matters most: **your baby's intelligence is normal.** Achondroplasia is a difference in how bones grow, not in how the brain learns. The overwhelming majority of babies with achondroplasia come through these first two years healthy — walking, talking, laughing, and heading into childhood with every prospect of a full, independent life.

So why does this page exist, and why is it long? Because the few serious risks of achondroplasia cluster in infancy, and — this is the crucial part — **almost all of them can be found and treated before they cause harm, if the right checks are done on time.** Historical studies of unmonitored infants found an excess risk of sudden death of roughly 2–7.5% in early life, almost entirely from compression of the brainstem at the base of the skull. With modern, proactive screening and treatment, that risk falls to as little as **0.3%** (GeneReviews, citing a US multicenter mortality study). The vigilance is not pessimism; it is precisely what turns a once-feared risk into a preventable one.

The single most important message: **standard well-baby care is not enough.** A baby with achondroplasia needs a specific schedule of extra tests and specialist visits in years 0–2, plus specific handling and seating rules at home. All of them are laid out below, and summarized age-by-age on the [care checklist](/checklist).

One more thing many new parents need to hear: about 80% of babies with achondroplasia are born to two average-height parents, from a brand-new (de novo) genetic change that nobody caused and nothing in pregnancy could have prevented. Nothing you did, ate, or didn't do made this happen. Achondroplasia occurs in about 1 in 21,000 to 1 in 28,000 births (\~4.7 per 100,000; older sources quote 1 in 15,000–40,000) — you are far from alone, and there is a well-worn path here. The [Understanding It](/understanding) page covers the genetics, and the [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing) page covers finding other families — which the international consensus explicitly recommends, because meeting families further down the road helps.

## The first month & your care team \{#first-month}

### Confirming the diagnosis

Achondroplasia is usually recognizable at or before birth (prenatal ultrasound signs generally do not appear until around 24–26 weeks): shortening of the upper parts of the arms and legs (rhizomelic shortening), a large head (macrocephaly) with a prominent forehead, a flattened nasal bridge (midface hypoplasia), a narrow chest relative to the head, short fingers held in a three-pronged "trident" position, and a trunk of near-normal length. The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends confirming the diagnosis with **skeletal X-rays**, which show a characteristic pattern (a square pelvis with narrow sacrosciatic notches, short vertebral pedicles, and rhizomelic long-bone shortening). A ***FGFR3* gene test** is confirmatory and straightforward, because virtually everyone with achondroplasia has the same single change in the gene (most commonly called G380R); testing is especially recommended when the picture is atypical, and it distinguishes achondroplasia from look-alike conditions such as hypochondroplasia. More on the gene and inheritance on [Understanding It](/understanding).

### Baseline tests every newborn with achondroplasia should have

Per the AAP (2020) and the International Consensus Statement, the first weeks should include:

- A full physical and **neurologic examination** — including feeding ability, any choking or gagging with feeds, pauses in breathing during sleep, blueness of the lips with feeding or sleeping, symmetry of limb movements, and muscle tone.
- An overnight **sleep study (polysomnography)** — recommended by the AAP in the newborn period for every infant, read by a pediatric sleep specialist and including carbon-dioxide monitoring, specifically to look for unusual *central* apnea (details below).
- **Imaging of the craniocervical junction** — the area where the skull meets the spine (CT or, preferably, MRI — see the next section for timing and sedation).
- Length, weight, and head circumference plotted on **achondroplasia-specific growth charts** — never standard charts.
- Confirmation of the **newborn hearing screen**, with formal audiology follow-up of any failed screen.
- **Car seat and handling guidance before hospital discharge** (the safe-handling section below is exactly this).
- Referral to a **skeletal dysplasia clinic** or clinician experienced with achondroplasia *as soon as the diagnosis is made* — the consensus statement makes this its own recommendation — plus genetic counselling and connection to family support such as Little People of America.

### The team you will build by age two

It sounds like a lot, and at first it is. A typical core team: your pediatrician or family doctor (the "medical home") plus a clinical geneticist or skeletal dysplasia specialist, a pediatric neurosurgeon and/or neurologist, a lung/sleep specialist (pulmonologist), an ear-nose-throat (ENT) surgeon and audiologist, a pediatric orthopedist for the spine and legs, and physical, occupational, or speech therapists as needed. Most families find visits front-loaded: intense in year one, then steadily spacing out. The [checklist page](/checklist) keeps the whole schedule in one place.

## The base of the skull (foramen magnum) \{#foramen-magnum}

If you learn one piece of anatomy as a parent, make it this one.

### What it is and why infants are most at risk

The opening at the base of the skull (the **foramen magnum**) is the hole through which the brainstem and upper spinal cord pass — the region that controls breathing. The skull base grows by the same cartilage-based (endochondral) growth that the *FGFR3* change disrupts, so in achondroplasia this opening is small at birth and its growth plates fuse early. The brainstem and cord, meanwhile, are normal size — a tight fit. In some infants the opening becomes tight enough to press on the junction of brainstem and cord (**cervicomedullary compression**). That pressure can cause **central sleep apnea** (the brain fails to send "breathe" signals), injury to the upper spinal cord (high cervical myelopathy), and — historically, in unscreened infants — sudden unexpected infant death. Infancy is the window of highest risk because the head is heaviest relative to the body, neck muscles are weakest, and the opening is at its relatively smallest.

Some perspective on the numbers, because they sound scarier without context. Some degree of narrowing (stenosis) is nearly universal — in one imaging series only 2 of 36 infants had none — and severe stenosis appears in roughly 30–50% of screened infants depending on the series (European Achondroplasia Forum, 2023). Yet most babies never need surgery: decompression is performed in roughly **7–13% of infants in the first 2 years at experienced centers**; around **20% lifetime** in one large US cohort, with wide variation between centers (from about 5% to over 40%). Specialist referral centers, which see the most-affected babies, report higher rates — over half of the children in one 2026 Italian referral cohort. Hold both truths at once: *most babies will not need surgery, but every baby needs the screening* — because many babies with significant compression have **no symptoms at all**.

### Symptoms parents can watch for

- Prolonged pauses in breathing during sleep, especially central apnea or exaggerated stop-start ("periodic") breathing
- Choking or gagging with feeds; blue lips while feeding or sleeping; poor weight gain even though feeding seems adequate
- Floppiness (hypotonia) beyond what is usual for achondroplasia, or new weakness
- Sustained ankle clonus (rhythmic beating of the foot when it is flexed), overactive or asymmetric reflexes, muscle spasms in the legs
- Early hand preference (before 12 months) — it can signal one-sided weakness
- Excessive irritability, or loss of previously gained skills

### Exactly what screening should happen

- **A neurologic check at every single visit.** The International Consensus Statement calls evaluation for cervicomedullary compression "mandatory at each medical evaluation" in infants and young children. This is a hands-on exam: tone, reflexes, symmetry, feeding, breathing history.
- **A sleep study (polysomnography) in the first months of life.** The AAP recommends it in the newborn period as part of the initial work-up (or at diagnosis, whenever diagnosis comes later); the international consensus asks for it **within the first year of life for all infants** (one of its recommendations adds "no later than 2 years of age"); the 2026 UK consensus says **by 6 months**, and accepts a simpler overnight breathing study (respiratory polygraphy, without brain-wave recording). One honest caveat from the European Achondroplasia Forum: a normal sleep study does *not* rule out stenosis — the correlation between sleep-study results and compression is imperfect, so the exam and imaging are still needed.
- **MRI of the craniocervical junction in the first months of life, including the whole brain.** MRI is the preferred imaging test per the consensus statement: it directly shows the brainstem, spinal cord, and the fluid (CSF) space around them, and the consensus advises considering it during the first months of life *even in babies with no symptoms*, with whole-brain images included to give a baseline for the ventricles. The European Achondroplasia Forum recommends routine MRI at 3–6 months for all infants, and the 2026 UK consensus recommends MRI of the brain *and* spine for every infant **within 3 months of birth or diagnosis**. The AAP is worded more conditionally: it lists neuroimaging in the newborn work-up "provided it can be performed safely," and says it is clearly indicated when the history, exam, or sleep study suggests a problem — so some US centers image every baby and some image selectively. Everyone agrees a baby with symptoms needs MRI promptly. CT with thin cuts can measure the size of the opening against published achondroplasia norms and may avoid sedation, but it cannot show the cord itself well.
- **About sedation:** MRI may require sedation or brief general anesthesia, but many young infants can be scanned with a **"feed-and-wrap"** technique — fed, swaddled, and scanned while naturally asleep, with no drugs at all. This works best up to about 6 months, especially on fast scanners, which is one practical argument for early imaging. Some centers add flexion–extension MRI views, which can reveal compression that only appears when the neck moves.

### Reading the MRI: the AFMS score

Many centers — and the UK consensus — now report the MRI using the **Achondroplasia Foramen Magnum Score (AFMS)**, a 0–4 scale. In plain terms: **0** means no narrowing; **1** means narrowing but the cushion of spinal fluid around the cord is still there; **2** means that fluid cushion is lost but the cord keeps its shape; **3** means the upper cord is flattened, without any change in the cord tissue itself; **4** means the cord shows a signal change on the scan — a sign it is being affected. Up to half of infants score 3 or 4. A score of 3 usually means closer monitoring and a repeat MRI within 6–12 months (in one series about 40% moved to a 4 within a year); a 4 usually leads to a decompression discussion. If your baby's report gives a score, ask what it means for the plan.

**When screening should trigger a prompt referral to a pediatric neurosurgeon** (per the AAP): an abnormal neurologic exam (marked floppiness, weakness, sustained clonus, asymmetric reflexes, choking or gagging with feeds); poor weight gain despite adequate intake; a sleep study showing oxygen levels dipping below 85% and/or central apnea beyond what is expected in a healthy newborn; or imaging showing a markedly small foramen magnum, deformation or signal change of the upper cord, or absence of the fluid cushion (CSF) around the cord.

### Decompression surgery: what it is and how it goes

When true symptomatic compression exists, neurosurgeons perform a **cervicomedullary (foramen magnum) decompression** — removing a small rim of bone at the skull base (sometimes with a patch to expand the covering membrane, called duraplasty) to give the brainstem room. The decision rests on the *combination* of exam + sleep study + MRI, never on a single finding in isolation — and, per the consensus, the absence of central apnea on a sleep study should not by itself rule out surgery when the exam and MRI clearly call for it. MRI-confirmed compression even without symptoms warrants a neurosurgical evaluation.

Outcomes are good for most children: GeneReviews describes marked neurologic improvement after decompression, and modern single-center series report resolution of central apnea and normalization of motor development in the large majority. It is still major surgery near the brainstem, and complications happen in a minority — in one 2026 referral-center cohort about 1 in 5 children had a complication such as a spinal-fluid leak, wound infection, or breathing problem, and a small number died. That is why it belongs at an experienced pediatric neurosurgical center, with the decision shared between the neurosurgeon, the wider team, and your family — one more reason the early skeletal-dysplasia-clinic referral matters.

## The big-head question \{#big-head}

**Every baby with achondroplasia has a big head — a big head alone is not a disease.** Macrocephaly is a universal feature, and brain MRI very commonly shows extra fluid over the brain surface (benign extra-axial fluid) and mildly enlarged fluid chambers (ventriculomegaly). The AAP is explicit that this expected finding should not be misread as a reason to place a shunt, and the consensus statement states plainly that asymptomatic ventriculomegaly does not require treatment. The likely mechanism for both the harmless fluid and the rare true problem is narrowing of the skull-base channels for the veins (the jugular foramina), which raises venous pressure and slows reabsorption of cerebrospinal fluid.

**True hydrocephalus** — progressive, pressure-raising fluid buildup that needs treatment — occurs in **less than 5%** of people with achondroplasia (the 2026 UK consensus puts it at about 3% of infants). The classically quoted rate of shunt placement is about 4%, and recent series report even lower rates of symptomatic hydrocephalus now that over-shunting has been recognized; some centers use an endoscopic procedure (third ventriculostomy) instead of a shunt in suitable cases.

### How it is monitored

- **Head circumference at every visit — monthly during the first year** — plotted only on **achondroplasia-specific head charts**. A big number is expected; the alarm sign is the head *crossing upward through achondroplasia percentiles* or growing unexpectedly fast for this population.
- Baseline brain imaging soon after diagnosis (the whole-brain portion of the early MRI does this job), so later scans have a comparison.

**Signs of raised pressure that warrant urgent imaging and neurosurgical referral:** head circumference accelerating across the achondroplasia-specific percentiles; a bulging, tense, or hard soft spot (fontanelle — note it normally stays open late in achondroplasia, sometimes to age 5–6); unusual lethargy or irritability; vomiting; poor weight gain; eyes deviated downward ("setting-sun" sign); or marked developmental delay or regression. One caution from the neurosurgical literature: classic hydrocephalus symptoms can be muted in achondroplasia, and slowly creeping developmental delay may be the only clue — which is exactly why the structured visit schedule exists.

## Sleep & breathing \{#sleep-breathing}

Babies with achondroplasia are at risk for **two different kinds of sleep apnea**, with different causes and different treatments:

1. **Central sleep apnea** — the brain's breathing drive is interrupted, caused by cervicomedullary compression at the foramen magnum (previous section). It is uncommon as the dominant diagnosis (\~3% in one large pediatric cohort) but it is the dangerous one in early infancy, and its treatment, when significant, is neurosurgical decompression.
2. **Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)** — the airway itself is narrow: midface hypoplasia, small nasal passages, relatively large tonsils and adenoids in a small throat, and low muscle tone. In a 2026 Australian cohort of 80 children with achondroplasia, sleep studies found sleep-disordered breathing in 85%, and obstructive apnea was the main type in about 4 out of 5 of those; across the literature, 10–85% require treatment for OSA at some point. Critically, **about 1 in 5 children in that cohort had no symptoms reported beforehand** — history alone is a poor predictor, which is why the sleep study is universal, not reserved for snorers.

### What a sleep study involves for a baby

Polysomnography is an overnight recording, usually in a pediatric sleep lab: painless surface sensors measure breathing effort and airflow, oxygen saturation, exhaled carbon dioxide (end-tidal CO2), heart rate, and sleep state. Nothing pierces the skin. For achondroplasia the AAP specifies that the study include CO2 monitoring and be interpreted by a pediatric sleep/lung specialist, because the thing being looked for — central apnea — is subtler than ordinary snoring. Longitudinal infant data show obstructive indices often improve across the first year and can worsen in the second, so repeat studies are common and expected, not a sign something went wrong. Guidelines differ on routine repeats: the AAP and the international consensus repeat studies when symptoms or earlier results call for it, while the 2026 UK consensus suggests regular studies every 6–12 months up to age 4.

### What parents may notice

Loud snoring; pauses followed by gasps or deep "catch-up" sighs; struggling or retracting breathing (sucking in at the ribs or neck); frequent night waking; poor feeding or poor weight gain. A note on sweating: sweating alone, especially during sleep, is common and normal in achondroplasia — it matters when paired with labored breathing.

### Treatments, by cause and severity

Watchful monitoring with repeat sleep studies; removal of the adenoids and tonsils (**adenotonsillectomy** — the consensus first-line treatment for OSA, with a follow-up sleep study 2–4 months after surgery); CPAP for residual obstruction; supplemental oxygen in selected infants; treating reflux (it aggravates airway obstruction); and, rarely, tracheostomy for severe multi-level obstruction. For scale: 25–40% of children with achondroplasia undergo adenotonsillectomy; counting all pharyngeal surgeries over a lifetime, around 45% have at least one. Central apnea, by contrast, prompts craniocervical imaging and neurosurgical evaluation, not throat surgery. More on airway and breathing treatments on the [Treatments](/treatments) page.

### The chest and lungs

The rib cage is small in infancy, with catch-up growth over the first two years; most children have no notable lung problems, and **restrictive lung disease occurs in under 5% of children younger than 3** (living at high altitude worsens it). Watch for persistent fast breathing, poor weight gain, or signs of respiratory fatigue, and involve a pediatric pulmonologist early if they appear. Respiratory infections can hit harder in the first two years because lung reserve is smaller: keep **immunizations on the normal national schedule** (the consensus is explicit — no modified schedule), ask about RSV protection — the international consensus specifically says antibody protection against RSV can be considered for babies with achondroplasia, because of their smaller lung reserve — and avoid tobacco smoke and knowingly infected contacts.

## Safe handling & positioning \{#safe-handling}

This is the section to print, tape to the fridge, and hand to every grandparent and babysitter. The logic behind every rule is the same: the head is large and heavy, the neck muscles are relatively weak, the ligaments are lax, and the foramen magnum is small — so **uncontrolled head movement and slumped, C-curled postures are the enemy** in the first year. The AAP's warning about head-flopping devices is unusually blunt, noting a risk of death when the cervicomedullary junction is compromised — even in infants whose neurologic exams were normal. These rules come from the AAP (2020), the International Consensus Statement, and the Children's Hospital at Westmead therapy guidelines (2010), which are the most practical, detailed source on the subject.

<SafeSeating caption="In the first year, keep your baby's back straight and head supported: firm, slightly reclined seats are fine; anything that curls the body into a C is not." />

### Always do

- **Support the head and neck with a hand every time you lift or carry.** Minimize both chin-to-chest flexion and backward over-extension; prevent "head bobbling." Educate *all* carers.
- **Back to sleep, on a firm flat surface** — no wedges, positioners, or soft bedding. Standard safe-sleep rules apply fully, and "flat" matters even more here, because a flexed neck can narrow both the airway and the space at the foramen magnum.
- **Feed with the back straight and head and neck in line**, supported by firm pillows or a supportive feeding position; give your own back support during breast- or bottle-feeding. Burping against your shoulder (you leaning back against cushions) is a good position; on the lap, give firm lower-back support.
- **Choose strollers, infant seats, and carriers with a firm, solid back** and minimal padding, keeping the back straight and the neck supported. Recline them as far as possible at first, moving more upright only as head control improves.
- **Car seat: rear-facing as long as possible**, with a firm back, reclined initially. Rear-facing gives the best protection and head-spine angle for a child with a large head, low tone, and lax ligaments; the consensus adds to keep using well-fitted seats as long as possible per local safety standards. Have the installation checked by a certified child-passenger-safety technician. Very small infants, or those with breathing concerns who fail a pre-discharge car-seat tolerance screen, may temporarily need a **car bed** (a crash-tested flat restraint) — ask the care team before hospital discharge. The 2026 UK consensus goes a step further and mentions **lie-flat car seats and buggies** as supportive equipment for young babies; ask your team which option they recommend locally.
- **Never let the baby sleep unattended in a car seat** — or any sitting device. The AAP flags this specifically for achondroplasia, and the consensus statement names the risk in plain words: *positional death*. Low tone plus a large, heavy head can let the airway kink shut in a slumped sleeping position. Transfer a sleeping baby out of the car seat when you arrive.

### Never do

- **No pulling to sit, and no lifting by the arms** — ever, including during play and dressing. The head must not flop back on an unsupported neck; and the elbows (which normally lack some extension in achondroplasia — a bony limit that must not be stretched, splinted, or casted) should not bear lifting forces.
- **No baby slings or pouches, at any time.** The AAP says avoid them in achondroplasia: they curl the spine into a C and let the head flop. Westmead's guideline and the 2026 UK consensus agree.
- **No mechanical or battery swings** (at minimum until solid head control; many centers say avoid entirely in year one), and **no bouncers, rockers, door jumpers (e.g., Jolly Jumpers), or baby walkers** — all of them put force through an unsupported spine or allow uncontrolled head movement.
- **No umbrella-style strollers, soft canvas seats, or anything that curls the baby into a "C."** C-sitting flexes the airway and rounds the lower spine (the kyphosis story, next section).
- **No propped, unsupported sitting** before the baby's own trunk muscles can hold the position — and avoid prolonged sitting of any kind in year one.
- **Don't carry the baby curled up for more than a few minutes** at a time, and avoid over-extending the neck during tummy time.

### Westmead's seating rules, with the actual numbers

| Equipment / situation                                   | The rule                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Supported reclined sitting (infant seat with firm back) | Usually start around **2–3 months**; recline about **30° back from upright-vertical toward the ground**; limit to **10–20 minutes** at a time while awake and alert. Reduce the recline gradually as trunk strength grows. |
| Car capsules, car seats, strollers                      | Firm back; **reclined as much as possible initially**; move more upright over time as head control improves.                                                                                                               |
| Bath seats                                              | Firm back, reclined **at least 30°** from vertical initially. (Never leave a baby unattended in water, as with any infant.)                                                                                                |
| High chairs (feeding)                                   | Firm back, reclined to roughly **60° from horizontal** during feeding, until the child sits independently with good trunk control.                                                                                         |
| Lap sitting / burping                                   | Firm lumbar (lower-back) support; against-the-shoulder burping with the carer leaning back is a good position.                                                                                                             |

<Callout type="tip" title="Tip for families">
  Make a one-page "how we hold our baby" sheet for grandparents, friends, and daycare: support the head, no lifting by the arms, no slings or swings, firm-backed seats only, never asleep in the car seat unattended. Most well-meaning adults have handled dozens of babies and will default to habits that are fine for other infants but not for yours. A calm, written list beats re-explaining at every visit — and the [Everyday Life](/daily-living) page has more practical equipment ideas.
</Callout>

## Protecting the back (kyphosis) \{#kyphosis}

Watch a young baby with achondroplasia slump forward in sitting and you will see an outward, rounded bend where the mid-back meets the lower back. This **thoracolumbar kyphosis** develops in nearly all infants with achondroplasia. It happens because trunk muscles are hypotonic, the head is heavy, ligaments are lax, and the vertebrae are slightly wedge-shaped — when the baby slumps, the spine curls.

**The natural history is mostly good news:** the curve is flexible in most babies and **improves or resolves in most children once they begin standing and walking**, typically during the second year. Some rounding remains in up to about **30%** (international consensus), and roughly **10–15%** have a curve that keeps progressing and needs specialist follow-up (2026 consensus overview). The reason clinicians care about the persistent minority is long-term: constant pressure on the front of soft young vertebrae can wedge them permanently, and a fixed kyphosis narrows an already-narrow spinal canal — which matters decades later for [adult spinal stenosis](/adults). Westmead's guideline puts it simply: the wedging is usually preventable. That is what all the seating rules above are actually for.

### Prevention — the daily program

- **Do not encourage independent sitting or standing** until the child has the back strength to hold an extended, upright posture on their own. Yes, this means sitting is *deliberately* delayed — spine protection is the priority, and it is one reason gross-motor milestones come later (next section). In the first couple of years, preventing deformity takes precedence over hitting "normal" gross-motor timelines.
- **Tummy time is therapy.** Prone play actively builds the trunk-extensor muscles that will eventually hold the spine straight; use foam wedges if needed, and avoid neck hyper-extension. Floor play in general — prone, supine, side-lying — is the engine of development here.
- **Firm back support in every seat and during every feed** until independent sitting; avoid flexed, C-shaped postures; aim to keep the spine's normal curves.
- **Aquatic physiotherapy** can help build strength from around 6 months, once head control and breathing status allow.
- Note the loop: delayed motor-skill acquisition is itself associated with kyphosis progression, so development and spine care go hand in hand — one more reason for therapist input, not a reason for blame.

### Monitoring and treatment

Establish care with a **pediatric orthopedist** to follow the spine clinically, with sitting or standing spine X-rays if the curve looks significant. Per the consensus statement, a kyphosis that has not resolved within about a year of walking, or that progresses in a walking child (curves of 20–40° a year after mobilization warrant careful follow-up), should be referred to an experienced pediatric spinal team. If a significant curve persists or stiffens, **bracing with a TLSO-type brace** (a molded torso jacket) is considered; surgery is rarely needed in early childhood. Two normal findings that need no treatment: the "sway back" (lumbar lordosis) that appears when walking begins, and the outward-turned legs and feet of infancy, which self-correct within about 6 months of starting to bear weight. Bow-legs (genu varum) are common and mostly managed later — see [Childhood](/childhood).

## Feeding, reflux & growth \{#feeding}

Feeding position is a safety issue, not a comfort preference: straight back, head and neck aligned, firmly supported. With attention to positioning, **breastfeeding works fine for most babies with achondroplasia**. Expect some differences that are normal for this condition: **smaller feed volumes** (a smaller stomach in a smaller body) and often **noisy bottle feeding** from small airways and low tone.

<Callout type="caution" title="Caution: choking with feeds is never &#x22;just messy eating&#x22;">
  Choking, gagging, or repeated coughing with feeds is on the red-flag list for compression at the base of the skull, and Westmead's guideline is explicit that poor oromotor function (trouble with sucking, swallowing, or coordinating the mouth) can be a sign of high spinal-cord compression. Report it to the care team the same day — it is usually something simpler, but this is one symptom that always deserves a look. If choking comes with a pause in breathing, blue or gray color, or floppiness, that is an emergency: call emergency services.
</Callout>

**Reflux (gastroesophageal reflux disease, GERD)** is more common than in average-height infants, and more common still in babies with breathing problems — and it works both ways, since reflux can aggravate airway obstruction. Persistent cases warrant referral to a pediatric gastroenterologist or pulmonologist rather than repeated rounds of guesswork.

### Feeding milestones, achondroplasia-specific (Westmead norms)

| Feeding skill           | Typical window in achondroplasia |
| ----------------------- | -------------------------------- |
| Takes smooth solids     | 4–7 months                       |
| Takes mashed solids     | 6–12 months                      |
| Finger feeding          | 8–18 months                      |
| Drinks from a cup       | 8–22 months                      |
| Self-feeds with a spoon | 15–27 months                     |

**Growth:** length, weight, and head circumference belong on **achondroplasia-specific charts at every visit** — the AAP report includes updated charts, and the consensus recommends parents keep their own copy or register of measurements. Birth size is near normal; growth then follows achondroplasia's own curves. Two directions matter: **poor weight gain despite adequate intake is a red flag now** (possible brainstem compression or a breathing problem), while *excess* weight gain becomes the concern later in childhood — healthy feeding habits genuinely start in these years. One thing that does not work: growth hormone is not an effective treatment for achondroplasia and is not recommended (see [Treatments](/treatments) for what is actually available).

## Milestones on their own timetable \{#milestones}

Almost all infants with achondroplasia have mild-to-moderate **hypotonia** (low muscle tone) that improves over the first two years. Combine that with a large head to balance, short limbs, lax joints, and deliberately delayed sitting, and motor milestones arrive **later and by different routes — and that is normal for achondroplasia.** The destination is the same: these children sit, stand, walk, and run.

<Callout type="info" title="Good to know: do not use standard milestone charts">
  The AAP instructs clinicians to assess motor development only against achondroplasia-specific milestone norms, and Westmead's guideline says standardized developmental assessments should not be used for this group at all. Standard charts (Denver, CDC) guarantee false alarms, needless anxiety, and inappropriate referrals. The flip side: **social and cognitive milestones should be on time** — a delay there is NOT expected in achondroplasia and should always be investigated.
</Callout>

The headline number parents want: **independent walking on average around 18–24 months**, with nearly all children walking by 36 months. Not walking by about 2 to 2.5 years deserves specialist review — not panic, review.

### Unconventional movement is normal movement

Children with achondroplasia solve the physics problem of a big head and short limbs in clever ways, and the solutions look unusual: **"snow-ploughing"** (pushing head-down along the floor with the feet), reverse snow-ploughing, **commando (army) crawling** — the most common floor mobility, since classic hands-and-knees crawling is uncommon — log rolling, bottom scooting, and "bear walking." Getting into sitting usually happens through lying on the tummy with the legs spread wide, then pushing the trunk upright; pulling to stand often goes through half-kneeling with one leg out. And they almost never play with their feet — they can't reach them. All of these are safe, normal variants, not problems to fix.

### Therapy that helps (and how not to "help")

- Physical and occupational therapy from someone familiar with skeletal dysplasia is valuable — but the guiding rule is **support, don't force**: never place a baby into positions (propped sitting, forced standing) they cannot get into themselves.
- Early-intervention services can be considered but are not automatically required just because of the diagnosis.
- Offer small, lighter toys and crayons that small hands with hypermobile fingers can actually grasp; a firm reclined seat with a play table lets a pre-sitting baby do "sitting" fine-motor play safely.
- **Don't "baby" the child.** They look smaller and younger than they are; give age-appropriate play, books, and language. Westmead adds a kind, honest note for parents: playgroups can be hard when other babies sit and walk first — your child *will* do all of it, a little later. It is fine for that to sting anyway; the [Wellbeing](/wellbeing) page is for exactly that.
- **Language:** about 13% of achondroplasia toddlers are "late talkers" (under 50 words and no word combinations at 24 months), and roughly 20% have delayed speech overall — usually linked to middle-ear fluid and hearing, not cognition. A formal **speech evaluation no later than age 2** is recommended.

## Ears, hearing & early speech \{#ears-hearing}

Middle-ear trouble starts early and is nearly the rule. The tube that drains the middle ear (the eustachian tube) is short and unusually horizontal in the achondroplasia skull base, so fluid lingers and infections recur: over 25% of children have chronic recurrent ear infections, and **more than half will eventually need pressure-equalizing (tympanostomy, or "grommet") tubes** — often placed during these first two years. The consensus recommends tubes when middle-ear fluid persists 3 months or more with documented hearing loss, and hearing aids are an option for persistent conductive loss.

Why the urgency? **Untreated fluid means muffled hearing during the exact window when speech is learned.** Treat the ears to protect the language.

### The hearing surveillance schedule

- Newborn hearing screen, with formal audiology follow-up of any failed screen
- **Formal behavioral hearing test by 9–12 months** (AAP; Westmead: a repeat hearing test at age 1 for every child; UK consensus: full audiology by 12 months)
- **Then regular audiology through early childhood** — at least annually per the AAP and the international consensus; **every 6 months in the preschool years** per the 2026 UK consensus, because this is the prime window for speech. Early ENT referral for recurrent or persistent ear disease either way.

<Callout type="caution" title="Caution: tell the ear surgeon about the diagnosis">
  People with achondroplasia have a higher rate of a vein anomaly in the middle ear (a high or dehiscent jugular bulb — the large vein lacking its usual bony roof), which can cause serious bleeding during ear-tube placement. The consensus specifically advises checking for it before myringotomy. Make sure any ENT surgeon knows the diagnosis in advance.
</Callout>

## The 0–2 visit schedule \{#visit-schedule}

The international consensus asks for multidisciplinary follow-up **at least every 2–4 months during the first year, then every 3–6 months**; the European Achondroplasia Forum says every 3–4 months to age 1, then every 3–6 months to age 3; the 2026 UK consensus asks for specialist-center visits **at least every 6 months** in year one, with local pediatric checks in between (the Westmead clinic, for reference, reviews every 4 months in year one, then 6-monthly to age 3). The [checklist](/checklist#guidelines-differ) compares them side by side. <Callout type="caution" title="Before any procedure or anesthesia in these years">
  The anesthesia team must know the diagnosis: the neck must be handled with great care (no forced extension for intubation — the foramen magnum is small; minimize all head and neck movement), drugs are dosed by weight and size rather than age, IV access can be harder, difficult-airway equipment should be ready, and spinal or epidural anesthesia is generally avoided unless imaging shows adequate canal space. The consensus recommends anesthesia by staff experienced with achondroplasia; the 2026 UK consensus adds spinal-cord (neurophysiology) monitoring for procedures longer than an hour. If your baby is on a growth medicine, ask the prescribing team ahead of time whether to pause it around the operation. More in the [Medical Library](/medical-library).
</Callout>

### A word about growth treatment

**Vosoritide (Voxzogo)**, a daily injectable medicine that counteracts the overactive *FGFR3* signal, is approved to increase growth in children with achondroplasia whose growth plates are open — and approval now extends to the youngest children in some regions (the US FDA expanded it in October 2023 to include infants and toddlers under 5; EU approval extends down to 4 months of age). It is a growth treatment, not a cure, and its effect on final adult height has not yet been established; crucially, it does **not replace one item of the surveillance on this page** — foramen magnum screening, sleep studies, and positioning rules apply exactly the same. Whether starting early reduces complications such as foramen magnum stenosis is still being studied, and early reports point both ways: in a small Japanese series of 9 children started before age 3, the opening at the skull base grew faster than in untreated children (one still needed decompression), while two babies started in their first week of life still developed worsening narrowing and needed decompression at 5 and 9 months. These are small, early reports — the practical message is that **MRI and sleep-study surveillance stay exactly the same on treatment**, as the 2025 international vosoritide guidelines say. A second medicine, **navepegritide** (Yuviwel, a weekly injection), was approved in the US in February 2026 for children aged 2 and over, so it is not an option in these first two years outside trials. Vosoritide is not licensed in Great Britain. Whether treatment is right for your child is a genuinely individual decision to make with a skeletal dysplasia team who knows your baby — this page is education, not medical advice. The full picture, including how it works, what the trials showed, and other drugs in development, is on the [Treatments](/treatments) page.

**The bottom line for parents:** these two years ask a lot — a specific test schedule, unusual carrying and seating rules, and a bigger care team than most babies need. The payoff is enormous. With modern surveillance, the once-feared risks of infancy become largely preventable; the kyphosis usually straightens itself as walking begins; the milestones arrive on achondroplasia's own reliable timetable; and the overwhelming majority of these children head into childhood healthy, walking, talking, and thriving — with completely normal intelligence and every prospect of a full, independent life.
