<Callout type="info" title="Why this page matters">
  Weight is the single modifiable factor that touches the most complications of achondroplasia: spinal stenosis symptoms, sleep apnea, blood pressure, joint pain, and long-term mobility. A small 2024 study (16 adults) found that moderate physical activity went hand in hand with better general health, vitality, and physical functioning in adults with achondroplasia. This is genuinely actionable — and it starts in infancy with feeding habits, not in adulthood with diet culture.
</Callout>

## Why standard BMI does not apply \{#bmi-problem}

Body Mass Index (BMI = weight ÷ height²) is routinely used to screen for over- and under-weight, but it has a structural problem in achondroplasia: the formula penalizes short height. Because height is in the denominator squared, a small reduction in height inflates BMI dramatically — even at a perfectly healthy weight. Using a standard BMI chart to assess a person with achondroplasia will often classify them as "overweight" or "obese" regardless of their actual body fat, because the chart was built on average-height data. A 2026 study of adults with restricted growth found that body weight scales with height to the power of about 1.4, not 2 as the BMI formula assumes — and that blood sugar and cholesterol were mostly normal despite high BMI readings.

**What to use instead:**

- **For children and teens: achondroplasia-specific charts** — US references (the CLARITY charts from Hoover-Fong et al., including weight-for-height, birth to 18 years; also in the AAP 2020 clinical report) and European references (Merker et al., 2018, including BMI, 0–20 years). These compare a child *within the achondroplasia population*, not against average-height norms.
- **For adults: there are no achondroplasia-specific weight or BMI cut-offs yet.** Track your own weight trend and waist measurement over time instead of a single BMI number.
- **Waist circumference** — measurable at any height, and in a 2025 US study of adults with achondroplasia it tracked closely with body fat measured by scan. Abdominal fat is the portion most closely linked to heart and metabolic risk in the general population.
- **Clinical judgment** — a clinician who knows the patient, can observe posture, mobility, and fatigue, and tracks weight trends over time provides more useful information than a population-comparison formula that was not designed for this population.

## Caloric needs — why less is often enough \{#caloric-needs}

Caloric needs scale with body size. A person with achondroplasia has a smaller body, with less lean mass to maintain, and so needs less total energy than an average-height person of the same age. A 2025 US study found that resting energy use in adults with achondroplasia was in line with their body size — nothing unusual about metabolism, just a smaller body. The practical problem is that portion sizes, activity-based calorie recommendations, and school lunch servings are all calibrated for average-height children and adults and systematically over-feed people who are shorter.

There is no published achondroplasia-specific calorie table (the evidence base is not large enough), but the principle is straightforward: smaller person, smaller portions — with the quality of food held to the same high standard as for any growing child.

### Infant feeding — the foundation

Healthy feeding habits begin in infancy, and the first two years set long-term energy-intake patterns. Key points specific to achondroplasia:

- **Breastfeeding works well for most babies with achondroplasia** and is encouraged — positioning rules (firm back support, head and neck in line) described on the [First Years page](/first-years#safe-handling) apply during feeding.
- **Smaller, more frequent feeds** are typical in the first year because gastric capacity is smaller in proportion to body size. Do not compare feed volumes to average-height peers.
- **Reflux is common** and can affect feeding efficiency; persistent regurgitation that causes poor weight gain or respiratory symptoms warrants pediatric gastroenterology review (see [First Years](/first-years#feeding)).
- **Choking with feeds is a red flag** — never attribute it to "messy eating" without ruling out brainstem compression at the foramen magnum. See [Warning Signs](/warning-signs#babies).
- **Weaning (solid introduction)** follows achondroplasia-specific timelines (smooth solids at 4–7 months, mashed by 6–12 months, finger foods by 8–18 months) — slower than average-height norms because of lower muscle tone in the mouth and trunk. Offer age-appropriate flavours and textures; don't restrict variety.
- **Baby-led weaning** (allowing a baby to self-feed soft chunks) is possible with appropriate positioning and monitoring, but given low tone and shorter fingers it is often done in combination with spoon feeding rather than exclusively. Consult your occupational therapist.

### Starting healthy habits young

Weight management is substantially easier when healthy eating patterns are established in childhood than when they need to be changed in adulthood. The guidance is not restrictive: it is about proportion and variety.

- Offer smaller portions calibrated to appetite, not to what same-age peers are eating (who are likely taller and more active).
- Prioritize vegetables, fruit, protein, and whole grains — the same balanced plate that is recommended for every child, just in a smaller quantity.
- Avoid using food as comfort or reward. Children with achondroplasia face more medical appointments, more procedures, and more social stress than most — it is tempting to buffer difficult moments with food, and it establishes a coping pattern that is harder to change later.
- Avoid restriction, dieting culture, or weight-focused language around children. In adolescence, when body image pressures are intense (see [Teen Years](/teens)), a history of food restriction increases disordered-eating risk. Focus on energy, strength, and enjoyment — not weight.

## Weight management across the lifespan \{#weight-management}

Many adults with achondroplasia carry extra weight, much of it around the middle. But the metabolic picture is not the same as in average-height obesity. A 2021 Norwegian MRI study (Fredwall et al.) compared adults with achondroplasia to average-height adults with the same BMI: those with achondroplasia had *less* internal (visceral) and liver fat, and lower blood pressure and cholesterol. In other words, BMI overstates body fat in achondroplasia. High blood pressure is still common in short-statured adults (see [Adults & Aging](/adults#heart-weight)), and weight is still not just a cosmetic issue in achondroplasia: it is a direct mechanical load on the narrowed spinal canal, on the bowed knees, and on an already-impaired respiratory system during sleep.

The honest cascade: excess abdominal weight deepens the lumbar curve, which compresses an already-tight spinal canal, which accelerates the walking-distance limitation that defines neurogenic claudication ([spinal stenosis](/adults#spinal-stenosis)). The same weight narrows the upper airway further, worsening sleep apnea, which raises blood pressure, which adds to cardiovascular risk. The cascade runs in both directions — weight loss (even modest amounts) genuinely improves stenosis symptoms and sleep apnea severity.

### Adults: practical strategies

- **Track trends, not numbers.** Given the BMI problem above, tracking waist circumference and energy-level trends is more informative than tracking BMI. Any upward trend in waist measurement or decreasing walking tolerance over months is the signal — not a single weight reading.
- **Eating pattern over diet plans.** The most effective eating pattern for long-term weight management is the one a person can sustain — usually a moderate reduction in ultra-processed foods, sugary drinks, and portion sizes, with meals centered on vegetables, protein, and legumes. Crash diets are counter-productive for anyone; for people with achondroplasia who have fatigue and pain from spinal disease, they are particularly undermining.
- **Timing and frequency.** Eating at regular mealtimes with planned snacks (avoiding continuous grazing) is associated with better satiety regulation. A registered dietitian experienced with complex medical cases can give individualized guidance.
- **Pain is not an excuse to be sedentary — but it is a reason to choose the right activity.** Chronic pain from back and joint problems makes exercise harder and less appealing. The solution is choosing activities that do not load the painful structures — the exercise section below covers this directly.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists (e.g., semaglutide) and obesity pharmacotherapy:** these newer medications are now widely used for obesity management in the general population and may be appropriate for adults with achondroplasia in whom conventional measures have been insufficient. There are no published achondroplasia-specific data on their use, and the decision belongs with a physician who knows both the general pharmacology and the individual's achondroplasia complication profile. If you take one of these medicines, tell the anesthesia team before any procedure — they slow stomach emptying, which matters for someone whose airway is already potentially difficult (see [Adults & Aging](/adults#anesthesia)). Raise it with your care team if conventional approaches have not been effective.

## Exercise and physical activity \{#exercise}

The International Consensus Statement (2022) urges an active lifestyle with slight modifications rather than exclusion — and the small 2024 study above found moderate physical activity went with better general health and vitality. The goal is not athletic performance; it is the preservation of mobility, muscle strength around the spine, and the mental-health benefits of regular movement.

### The recommended list

<Grid>
  <Card title="Swimming">
    The universal recommendation. Non-weight-bearing, joint-friendly, trunk-strengthening, and open to every age from infancy (aquatic physiotherapy from \~6 months) through old age. Swimming also helps manage weight without loading the spine. The AAP specifically names it as an acceptable activity.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Cycling">
    Excellent low-impact cardiovascular exercise with no axial spine loading. Bikes can be adapted (shorter cranks, lower frames, wide handlebars for stability). Stationary bikes and recumbent bikes are widely used. The AAP names cycling alongside swimming as the foundation activities.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Walking">
    Walking for fitness is appropriate, with the caveat that walking distance may be limited by neurogenic claudication in adults. Track how far you can walk comfortably rather than competing with a step-count target built for average-height bodies. Level terrain is preferable to hills and stairs, which load the lumbar spine more.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Golf, tennis, baseball/softball">
    Endorsed across sources. Non-collision, variable-intensity sports that can be played with equipment adapted to shorter reach (shorter golf clubs, adapted grip). Most recreational leagues are welcoming and accommodating.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Yoga and Pilates">
    Core-strengthening and flexibility work that can significantly reduce chronic back pain and improve posture — both important given the deep lumbar curve of achondroplasia. Choose a teacher who can modify poses for limited elbow extension and shorter limbs. Avoid inversions (headstands, shoulder stands) that load the cervical spine.
  </Card>

  <Card title="Strength training">
    Resistance training is beneficial for bone density, muscle mass, and spinal support — with modifications. Machine weights with adjustable seats are easier to adapt than free weights on the floor. Core work (planks, bird dogs, bridges) directly supports the lumbar spine. Avoid heavy axial loading (barbell squats, heavy overhead pressing) that compresses the spine vertically.
  </Card>
</Grid>

### The avoid list — and the reason

<Callout type="caution" title="Avoid these activities at all ages — the reason is spinal, not general fragility">
  The narrow foramen magnum (skull-base opening) and the narrow spinal canal mean that a sudden, forceful impact to the head or neck carries a genuine risk of spinal cord injury. GeneReviews and the AAP are unambiguous: **avoid collision sports (American football, rugby, ice hockey), trampolines, diving from diving boards, vaulting in gymnastics, and hanging upside down from knees or feet.** Many clinics also advise against heading the ball in soccer for the same reason. This is not about fragility — it is about anatomy. These activities are fine for average-height people whose spinal canals have adequate reserve. The achondroplasia spinal canal does not. See [Everyday Life](/daily-living#sports) for more on sport and recreation.
</Callout>

### What happens after exercise: fatigue management

People with achondroplasia cover the same distances as taller people but with more steps, more muscular effort per step (shorter stride), and often more pain. Fatigue — even after exercise that seems moderate — is normal and not a failure. Practically:

- Plan activity in blocks with rest periods rather than trying to sustain effort continuously.
- Morning exercise often works better than evening for people whose back pain accumulates through the day.
- Post-exercise soreness is expected; pain that is neurological (radiating, electrical, accompanied by weakness or numbness) is different and should be reported to the care team — it may indicate positional spinal compression.
- Mobility aids (canes, crutches, scooters for longer distances) are tools, not symbols of defeat. Using them preserves energy for the things that matter.

## Physical therapy and physiotherapy \{#physical-therapy}

Physical therapy (PT) or physiotherapy is valuable at every life stage in achondroplasia — and it is different enough from standard PT that a therapist experienced with skeletal dysplasia will produce better outcomes than one adapting protocols designed for average-height patients. If your local PT has not treated achondroplasia before, the Westmead Hospital therapy guidelines (Sydney Children's Hospitals Network, 2010) and the International Consensus Statement are the key references to share with them.

### Infants and toddlers (0–3)

The goal in infancy is supporting motor development within achondroplasia's own timetable, protecting the spine from kyphosis, and building the trunk strength that walking will eventually require.

- **Tummy time (prone play)** is the single most important exercise for infants — it builds neck extensors, shoulder girdle muscles, and trunk extensors that eventually support upright posture. Start on a slightly elevated surface (foam wedge) if flat tummy time is hard, gradually progressing to the floor. Daily sessions, building from a few minutes to 20–30 minutes of cumulative prone time per day.
- **Aquatic physiotherapy** can begin around 6 months once head control is sufficient and breathing status allows. Water supports the large head while the body moves freely.
- PT guides safe **milestone progression** — the specific routes children with achondroplasia use (army crawl, log roll, bottom scoot) rather than pushing for standard crawling positions that overload the spine.
- PT provides **adaptive equipment assessment** — whether and when to use walking aids, which strollers support the spine correctly, and how to adapt the home environment. An occupational therapist (OT) often works alongside.
- Avoid forced positioning, stretching of the elbows (which have a bony extension limit), or placing the child in positions they cannot get into independently.

### Children and school age (3–12)

- **Core strengthening** — the deep stabilising muscles of the trunk (transversus abdominis, multifidus) protect the spine during movement. PT builds these through supported play, balance activities, and eventually simple gym exercises.
- **Hip flexor stretching** — the deep lumbar curve (lordosis) of achondroplasia creates tight hip flexors, which worsen the lumbar load. A daily 30-second hip flexor stretch (lying on the back, pulling one knee to the chest) is simple and useful from age 3 upward.
- **Gait analysis and orthotics** — bowed legs change the mechanics of walking, often causing a compensatory foot position and altered trunk movement. A gait analysis by a PT experienced with lower-limb bowing can identify whether orthotics or shoe inserts help, and whether a referral to orthopedics for more formal assessment is indicated.
- **School PE modification plan** — PT should work with the school and produce a written plan describing safe activities (swimming, cycling), activities to avoid (trampolines, contact sports), and how to modify common PE activities (adapted races, shorter distances).
- **Hydrotherapy** continues to be valuable through childhood — many children with achondroplasia find it the most comfortable way to build fitness because water takes weight off joints and the spine.

### Adolescents and adults

- **Back pain management** — when back pain develops (40–70% of adults, as described on the [Adults page](/adults)), a PT program of core stabilisation, gentle spinal decompression exercises (child's pose, knee-to-chest stretches), and hip mobility is the first-line conservative treatment, alongside weight management. Published evidence in the general spinal stenosis literature (which applies here) supports exercise over rest for symptom management.
- **Neurogenic claudication exercise strategy** — when walking distance is limited by spinal stenosis, forward-flexed cycling and swimming preserve cardiovascular fitness without triggering the symptoms that walking does. This is not giving up; it is choosing the right modality for the anatomy.
- **Post-surgical rehabilitation** — after spinal decompression surgery, a structured PT program is essential. The surgical result does not last without rehabilitation, particularly re-building the trunk muscles that surgery disrupted.
- **Fall prevention** — in older adults, bowed legs, hypermobile ankles, and fatigue increase fall risk. PT balance training, appropriate footwear (wide, stable, low-heeled), and environmental assessment reduce this risk. See [Adults & Aging](/adults#aging).

### Finding a PT who knows achondroplasia

Ask your skeletal dysplasia center for a PT referral — most major centers either have an in-house PT who specializes in skeletal dysplasias or have established referral relationships with community PTs who have been trained in the specific protocols. If no specialist is available locally, a telehealth consultation with a PT at a major skeletal dysplasia center can assess and produce a written program that a local PT delivers. Little People of America (LPA) and Beyond Achondroplasia maintain informal community referral networks; peer recommendations from other families in the community consistently identify good local practitioners more reliably than clinic directories.

## Bone health, vitamin D, and calcium \{#bone-health}

A counterintuitive question: do people with achondroplasia have weak bones? The answer is nuanced and important.

FGFR3 signaling affects how bones grow in length — but not necessarily how they mineralise. The bones in achondroplasia are shorter, but they are not inherently fragile. Fracture rates in published series are not clearly elevated above those in average-height populations. That said, several factors can impair bone mineralisation in achondroplasia specifically:

- **Reduced sun exposure** — people with achondroplasia may be less physically active outdoors, and mobility limitations may reduce the incidental sun exposure that generates vitamin D in the skin.
- **Reduced weight-bearing exercise** — weight-bearing activity (walking, standing, resistance training) stimulates bone formation. People who are significantly limited by spinal stenosis and therefore spend more time sitting are at greater risk of disuse-related bone loss.
- **Post-menopausal bone loss** — as for all women, the estrogen drop at menopause accelerates bone loss. The interaction with achondroplasia-specific bone properties has not been well studied.

**Practical guidance (in line with general public health recommendations):**

- **Vitamin D:** there is no achondroplasia-specific advice, so standard recommendations apply. In the US the daily amount is 400 IU for babies, 600 IU from age 1 to 70, and 800 IU over 70; the UK NHS advises 400 IU (10 micrograms) a day for everyone from age 1, especially in autumn and winter, and all year for people who get little sun. The Endocrine Society (2024) supports a daily supplement for children and teens, and advises *against* routine vitamin D blood tests or higher-than-recommended doses for healthy adults under 75. Ask for a blood test only if there are symptoms or specific risk factors.
- **Calcium:** standard age-based targets apply. US recommended amounts are 700 mg/day at ages 1–3, 1,000 mg at 4–8, **1,300 mg at 9–18** (the teen years need the most), 1,000 mg for most adults, and 1,200 mg for women over 50 and everyone over 70. The UK figure for adults is 700 mg/day. Dairy, calcium-fortified plant milks, and leafy green vegetables are good sources. Supplementation is only necessary if dietary intake is genuinely inadequate.
- **DXA (bone density scan):** standard DXA scans are not reliable in achondroplasia — the reference values assume average-height skeletons and will systematically over-diagnose "low bone density" in shorter people. See the [Adults page](/adults#heart-weight) for the important caveat on interpreting DXA in this population. If bone density assessment is needed, it should be done with a metabolic bone specialist who can apply size corrections.
- **Bisphosphonates and bone drugs:** not indicated for achondroplasia absent a documented bone-density problem confirmed by a metabolic bone specialist. Do not accept these drugs based on a standard DXA reading without specialist input.

## Other nutritional considerations \{#vitamins}

### Iron

No excess iron deficiency is described specifically in achondroplasia. Standard age-appropriate dietary iron intake applies. Infants transitioning to solid foods benefit from iron-rich first foods (puréed meat, legumes, fortified infant cereals) as in any infant — the timing differs slightly (achondroplasia-specific feeding milestones), but the iron rationale is the same.

### Omega-3 fatty acids and anti-inflammatory diet

Chronic musculoskeletal pain — common in adults with achondroplasia — has been linked to systemic low-grade inflammation, and a diet rich in omega-3 fatty acids (oily fish, walnuts, flaxseed) and colourful vegetables has documented anti-inflammatory effects. While no achondroplasia-specific trials exist, these are broadly healthy dietary choices with no downside for this population.

### Supplement caution

A thriving supplement industry targets families of children with skeletal conditions, marketing products claimed to support "bone growth," "height increase," or "joint health" in dwarfism. There is no supplement that overcomes the FGFR3 signal — the mechanism is genetic, not nutritional. Standard micronutrient intake is all that is needed. Save the money and the hope for the actual treatments described on the [Treatments page](/treatments).

## Adolescent nutrition and body image \{#teens-body-image}

Adolescence deserves its own heading on this page, because the intersection of body image pressures and the genuine need to manage weight in achondroplasia is one of the most delicate family navigation tasks of the teen years.

- Achondroplasia-related weight gain often begins in early puberty, when hormonal changes shift fat distribution toward the abdomen — the same pattern seen in the general population, but amplified by shorter stature. This is not a failure of willpower; it is biology.
- At the same time, adolescents with achondroplasia are already managing body-image challenges related to height, proportion, and the perception of their body in the world. Adding a layer of weight-focused messaging can tip some teens toward disordered eating — restrictive behaviours (undereating, skipping meals), excessive exercise, or compensatory overeating.
- The *language* used matters enormously. Clinical teams and parents should frame food and activity discussions around energy, strength, mobility, and health — not weight, not size, not appearance. "Which breakfast keeps you energised until lunch?" is a different conversation from "you've gained weight and need to cut back."
- If you observe signs of disordered eating (restriction, secretive eating, distorted body perception, or excessive preoccupation with food or exercise) — refer the adolescent to an eating disorder–aware therapist, ideally one also familiar with chronic illness and disability. The [Wellbeing & Community page](/wellbeing#mental-health) has guidance on finding appropriate therapeutic support.
- Body-positive community connection — through LPA youth programs, camps, and peer networks — is one of the most protective factors against body dissatisfaction and disordered eating in this population. Young people who know other people who look like them are more likely to accept and care for their body. See [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing).

| Infancy (0–2)       | Weight and length plotted on **achondroplasia-specific charts at every visit** (monthly in year 1). Poor weight gain despite adequate feeding is a red flag (possible brainstem compression). Too-rapid weight gain is also noted.                                                                                                                          | Pediatrician / skeletal dysplasia clinic, with dietitian if concerns arise                 |
| Childhood (2–12)    | Weight for height on achondroplasia-specific references. Waist circumference where available. Activity levels and eating patterns discussed at clinic visits.                                                                                                                                                                                               | Pediatrician / skeletal dysplasia clinic; school physical education teacher                |
| Adolescence (12–18) | Weight for height trends. Screen for disordered eating. Body image assessment as part of routine psychosocial review. Physical activity habits. Calcium and vitamin D intake.                                                                                                                                                                             | Pediatrician, adolescent medicine; psychological/psychosocial support; dietitian if needed |
| Adulthood           | Weight trend and waist circumference (no achondroplasia-specific adult weight or BMI references exist). Blood pressure every visit. Fasting lipids and glucose per standard adult preventive schedule. Vitamin D blood test only if symptoms or risk factors. Physical activity habits. Walking distance trends (as a proxy for stenosis progression). | Primary care physician, with skeletal dysplasia specialist review every 3–5 years          |

<Callout type="tip" title="One simple tool: keep a walking-distance diary">
  Adults with achondroplasia consistently describe a shrinking comfortable walking distance as their first symptom of advancing spinal stenosis — often years before they mention it to a doctor, because it feels gradual. Keeping a simple note (even once a month: "comfortable before needing to rest: \~300 m") makes a trend visible that would otherwise be invisible. A clear downward trend — whatever the starting number — is the signal to request a whole-spine MRI, regardless of current medication status or weight.
</Callout>
