Here is the honest shape of adulthood with achondroplasia. Intelligence, careers, relationships, and parenthood are all on the table — the International Consensus Statement (2022), written by 55 experts across 16 countries, states plainly that most adults lead ordinary lives. At the same time, adulthood has a distinct medical agenda, and it is different from childhood's. The infant years were about the skull base and breathing; adulthood is about the **lower spine**, plus blood pressure, weight, sleep apnea, hearing, and pain. Nearly all of it is manageable — and much of it is preventable — *when someone is actually watching*. This page is about making sure someone is.

<Callout type="info" title="On life expectancy — read this first">
  GeneReviews (2026) describes life span in achondroplasia as usually near normal. Older US cohorts, followed up to 2007, showed average life expectancy about 10 years shorter, with extra deaths from infant brainstem compression, accidents, and heart disease in young and middle-aged adults. A 2018 study found that survival in childhood has improved with modern care, though not as fast as in the general population, and that more deaths now come from heart and stroke-related causes and accidents. Whether adult survival has improved is not yet known, and the reasons for the extra heart deaths are still being studied. What you *can* act on — blood pressure, sleep apnea, weight, and spine symptoms — is exactly what the monitoring on this page is about.
</Callout>

## The adult healthcare gap \{#healthcare-gap}

Children with achondroplasia are usually followed closely by skeletal dysplasia teams. Adults very often are not — and this is the single biggest structural problem in achondroplasia care. In a European Achondroplasia Forum survey, 8 of 15 provider centers reported that their patients did not transition to *any* adult multidisciplinary team; not one patient advocacy group rated the transition experience as good, and half rated it poor or very poor. The result is predictable: adults drift out of follow-up in their twenties, and reappear in their forties with advanced spinal stenosis that could have been caught a decade earlier. The burden is real: a 2025 study of US insurance records found adults with achondroplasia were admitted to hospital almost twice as often as matched adults without it, and more often still if they had spinal stenosis.

What good adult care looks like:

- **A named adult team.** GeneReviews recommends adults be seen every 3–5 years (more often if symptomatic) by a clinician familiar with achondroplasia; the International Consensus Statement (2022) recommends individualized, lifelong multidisciplinary follow-up. Where no adult achondroplasia clinic exists — which is most places — assemble your own: a primary care physician who will coordinate, plus orthopedics/neurosurgery, pulmonology or sleep medicine, ENT/audiology, and rehabilitation, called in as needed.
- **Your records, in your hands.** Keep copies of your genetic result, every MRI report, sleep study, and operation note. Adults with achondroplasia frequently outlive their childhood records and their childhood specialists.
- **A patient-held checklist.** The European Achondroplasia Forum published a practical adult follow-up checklist (Advances in Therapy, 2024) designed for exactly this situation — you bring it to your general practitioner, and it tells them what to check and when. Our [age-by-age care checklist](/checklist) summarizes the same ground.
- **Being the expert in the room — politely.** Most doctors you meet as an adult will never have treated achondroplasia. That is not a criticism of them; it is a fact of rare conditions. Arrive with your checklist and your history, name the specific risks (spinal stenosis symptoms, blood pressure cuff sizing, anesthesia precautions), and ask for the specific tests. Doctors overwhelmingly respond well to a prepared patient.

If you are a teenager or the parent of one, the handover itself — learning your own history and red flags before leaving pediatrics — is covered on the [Teen Years page](/teens). The International Consensus Statement (2022) recommends dedicated transition appointments in later adolescence for exactly this purpose (Rec 131).

## Spinal stenosis: the one condition every adult must know cold \{#spinal-stenosis}

If you remember one medical section of this entire site, make it this one. GeneReviews calls symptomatic spinal stenosis the most common medical complaint of adulthood in achondroplasia — and its worst outcomes are largely a function of *delay*, which means they are largely avoidable.

### What it is

People with achondroplasia are born with a narrow spinal canal — the bony tunnel that carries the spinal cord and nerves — because the pedicles, the bony pillars of each vertebra, are short and set close together. With ordinary age-related wear (bulging discs, thickening ligaments, arthritis), an already-tight tunnel narrows further and squeezes the nerves. This is spinal stenosis (narrowing of the spinal canal), most often in the lower back (the lumbar spine, typically levels L1–L4).

<SpinalCanal caption="People with achondroplasia start with a narrower spinal canal. Ordinary wear and tear that most people never notice can then squeeze the nerves." />

### How common it is

Very. A Norwegian population-based study (Fredwall et al., 2020) found symptomatic spinal stenosis in 68% of adults (34 of 50), with a median age of 33 at first symptoms — but a range of 10 to 67, meaning some people develop it as teenagers. It starts young: by age 40 about two in three participants had symptoms, and by 45 more than four in five. Older estimates, from small or selected groups, ran 10–30%. Of the affected Norwegians, 82% had had surgery, and stenosis was almost always multi-level (32 of 34 had two or more spinal levels involved). In the large US natural-history cohort (CLARITY, 1,374 patients), about 13% had undergone spine surgery at data cutoff, with a mean age of 22 at first spine surgery; survival-curve projections from natural-history data suggest lifetime risk keeps climbing steeply into old age.

### What it feels like — learn this pattern

Aching, burning, numbness, tingling, heaviness, or weakness in the **buttocks and legs**, brought on by **walking or standing**, and — this is the classic clue — **relieved by sitting, squatting, or leaning forward**. Bending forward physically opens up the spinal canal. Doctors call this pattern *neurogenic claudication*. Many people discover it accidentally: walking hurts, but pushing a shopping cart — leaning forward onto the handle — feels fine. Clinicians literally call this the shopping-cart sign. Other signs: shrinking walking distance (in the Norwegian study, people with stenosis walked on average 110 meters less in a six-minute test), low back pain, and legs that feel heavy or give way.

<StenosisPosture caption="Leg symptoms that come on with walking and ease when you lean forward or sit are the classic sign of spinal stenosis. Get them checked." />

One distinction worth knowing: leg pain on walking that is relieved simply by *standing still* suggests a circulation problem (vascular claudication) rather than nerve compression, which needs relief from *bending forward or sitting*. Tell your doctor exactly which one describes you — it changes the workup.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Seek urgent care — same day / emergency department — if…">
  • New trouble controlling urine or bowels (leaking, or being unable to go)  • Numbness in the groin or saddle area  • Rapidly worsening leg weakness or a foot that slaps or drags (foot drop). These can signal **cauda equina syndrome** — nerve compression that becomes **irreversible** without emergency surgery. In the Norwegian cohort, 41% of those with stenosis had urinary incontinence and 21% bowel incontinence; bladder and bowel symptoms are never something to watch and wait on. Ask directly for an urgent MRI. More red flags on the [Warning Signs page](/warning-signs).
</Callout>

### Why acting early matters so much

Surgical outcomes are consistently better the sooner surgery follows symptom onset — the duration of pre-operative symptoms is the strongest predictor of how complete recovery is. Yet in the Norwegian cohort the average gap from first symptom to surgery was nine years. Nine years of nerve compression is nine years of risk of permanent damage. This is why every adult (and every teenager, who may not develop symptoms for decades) should be able to recite the pattern: *leg symptoms on walking, relieved by bending forward — get evaluated.*

### Diagnosis and treatment

If symptoms appear, the test is an **MRI of the entire spine** — not just the painful segment, because stenosis is usually multi-level (International Consensus Statement, 2022). Referral should go to a spine center experienced with achondroplasia; standard-anatomy assumptions do not apply, and the 2026 International Achondroplasia Forum principles say spinal surgery should be done only at specialized centers.

Treatment goes in order:

- **Conservative first, when symptoms are mild:** weight loss (extra abdominal weight deepens the lumbar curve and directly worsens symptoms), physical therapy and core strengthening, activity modification, and pain management.
- **Surgery when symptoms are significant or progressing:** the operation is a wide, multi-level decompressive laminectomy — removing bone and ligament to un-pinch the nerves. A 2021 review of the published series found that about 9 in 10 patients had full or partial relief, but only about half had *full* relief — and symptoms that had lasted longer before surgery were linked to less complete recovery. A 2025 Dutch study suggested MRI guides for which levels need decompression: severe narrowing on the Schizas grading scale (grade C or D), or a dural sac (the nerve-containing tube) smaller than about 62 mm² in cross-section. These are guides, not cut-offs — they matched the surgical decision about 70–75% of the time — and the most narrowed level was usually L2–L3, higher in the back than in typical age-related stenosis. Surgeons add **fusion** (joining vertebrae with hardware) in defined situations: always in skeletally immature patients, and when the decompression spans more than five levels, crosses a junctional zone of the spine, or the spinal alignment is unfavorable. Repeat surgery is common: in the same review, about 4 in 10 procedures needed a re-operation, and a tear in the dura (the nerve sac lining) occurred in about 1 in 5.

**The strongest lever you personally control is weight.** Obesity aggravates stenosis symptoms and accelerates the need for surgery; weight control is, in a real sense, spine treatment. (As everywhere on this site: this is education, not medical advice — surgical decisions belong with a spine team that knows achondroplasia and knows you.)

## Chronic pain

Pain deserves its own section because it is common, undertreated, and manageable. In a Johns Hopkins quality-of-life study, 75% of adults with achondroplasia reported pain, versus roughly 19% of average-statured US adults; another study found 64% versus 25–35%. Back pain affects roughly 40–70% of adults and rises with age. The sources are mechanical and identifiable: spinal stenosis, chronic strain from the deep lumbar curve, joint stress from bowed legs, and general musculoskeletal load. The [Pain Management page](/pain) covers sources, medicines (including important breathing cautions), injections, and psychological approaches in detail.

What good care looks like, per the International Consensus Statement (2022):

- **Pain is assessed at every check-up**, with a structured tool such as the Brief Pain Inventory — a short questionnaire you can also use yourself to track pain over months and bring numbers, not vague impressions, to appointments.
- **Management is multidisciplinary:** physical therapy, weight management, medication, and psychological support together — not painkillers alone.
- **Its knock-on effects are taken seriously:** mood, sleep, work, and social life. Pain that is "just part of the condition" is a phrase to push back on; new or changing pain always deserves evaluation (it can be the first sign of stenosis).

## Heart health, blood pressure, and weight \{#heart-weight}

Here is the sobering number, and what to do with it. The landmark 42-year mortality follow-up (Wynn et al., 2007, about 790 individuals) found heart-disease-related death between ages 25 and 35 was more than ten times the general-population rate. These are historical cohorts, and the reasons are not fully understood: a 2021 Norwegian study found that adults with achondroplasia actually had *lower* blood pressure, cholesterol, and internal fat than average-height adults with the same BMI, suggesting other factors are involved. What is clear is that high blood pressure and sleep apnea are common and treatable — so those are where your effort goes.

### Blood pressure — measured correctly, every visit

- Blood pressure should be checked at **every adult medical visit** (International Consensus Statement, 2022) and treated on the same thresholds as the general population. In a US screening study of 403 short-statured adults, 42% had high blood pressure or were already on treatment for it.
- **Cuff size matters more than most clinics realize.** Standard adult cuffs fit rhizomelic (short-upper-segment) arms poorly and distort readings. Ask for a narrower cuff on the upper or lower arm segment; the forearm is an accepted measurement site when the upper arm is too short. If the clinic grabs the usual cuff, say so — a wrong-cuff reading is worse than no reading.
- Weight and blood pressure travel together: in adults with achondroplasia, each additional BMI unit raised the odds of hypertension by roughly 8–10% (American Journal of Medical Genetics, 2020).

### Weight — the through-line of adult health

- Excess weight worsens spinal stenosis, joint pain, sleep apnea, and cardiovascular risk. It is the one factor that touches every other section of this page.
- **Standard BMI charts mislead in achondroplasia** — short limbs inflate BMI. The achondroplasia-specific charts (Hoover-Fong US charts; Merker European charts) cover children and teens only; **there are no adult-specific weight or BMI cut-offs**. Track your weight trend and waist measurement instead of a BMI category — details on the [Nutrition & Exercise page](/nutrition#bmi-problem).
- Body composition is genuinely different: a 2021 Norwegian MRI study found adults with achondroplasia had *less* internal (visceral) and liver fat, and lower cholesterol and blood pressure, than average-height adults with the same BMI. In other words, BMI overstates body fat. But mechanical load on the spine, knees, and airway still rises with weight, so weight still matters.
- **DXA caveats:** standard DXA (bone-density) results are hard to interpret in achondroplasia — the T-score references assume average-stature skeletons, and smaller bones can look "low density" when they are not. DXA is useful for measuring body fat, but any bone-density result needs size-adjusted interpretation by a metabolic bone specialist. Fracture calculators such as FRAX have not been validated in this population.
- What works: eating habits built early (see [Childhood](/childhood)), plus regular low-impact activity adapted to spine and joints — swimming and cycling are the usual recommendations. Cholesterol, diabetes, and smoking-cessation care follow the same schedule as everyone else's.

## Sleep apnea and hearing loss \{#sleep-hearing}

Two quiet, treatable problems that a majority of adults carry — often without knowing it.

### Obstructive sleep apnea (\~60% of adults)

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) — repeated blockage of breathing during sleep — was found in 59% of Norwegian adults with achondroplasia (Fredwall et al., 2021); of those, 59% had moderate-to-severe disease and **almost half were previously undiagnosed**. The anatomy explains it: a small midface (midface hypoplasia), narrow nasal passages, a relatively large tongue base — plus weight. Untreated OSA raises blood pressure and strains the heart, and is thought to be one contributor to the historically higher heart-disease death rate in adults.

**Suspect it if:** loud snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, gasping awakenings, unrefreshing sleep, daytime sleepiness, morning headaches, poor concentration, irritability, or blood pressure that will not come down. Age over 40 and higher weight raise the odds. The response is simple: **ask for an overnight sleep study** (polysomnography). Treatment is usually CPAP — a bedside machine delivering gentle air pressure through a mask — plus weight loss; ENT surgery helps selected cases. CPAP users should be reviewed regularly by a sleep specialist (International Consensus Statement, 2022). The European Achondroplasia Forum (2025) advises screening for sleep apnea throughout life, including adulthood.

### Hearing loss

About 40% of people with achondroplasia have functionally important hearing loss (GeneReviews); adult studies report 33–55%, increasing with age. Most of it is conductive — a mechanical middle-ear problem left by a lifetime of eustachian-tube dysfunction and ear fluid (see [First Years](/first-years) for the childhood story) — layered on ordinary age-related loss. Hearing aids are underused: in a Norwegian study, 53% of adults had hearing loss but only 20% used hearing aids, and in a US screening at a national meeting, 55% of adults with achondroplasia failed screening while only about 3% of everyone screened used hearing aids. Half of Norwegian adults under 45 already had some (mostly mild) loss. Untreated hearing loss quietly isolates people; it is one of the most fixable problems on this page. The recommendation: formal audiology evaluation in adulthood and periodically after, screening starting *earlier* than in the general population, and a low threshold for hearing aids (International Consensus Statement, 2022).

## Joints, arthritis, and joint replacement \{#joints}

The evidence on arthritis is honestly mixed: one study found no excess osteoarthritis in the major weight-bearing joints (some researchers suggest the FGFR3 pathway may even be relatively protective of cartilage), while another reported about 33% prevalence. What is common regardless: knee pain and instability related to leg bowing and loose ligaments. Realigning bowed legs earlier in life (see [Childhood](/childhood) and [Teens](/teens)) reduces later joint stress.

When a hip or knee does wear out, replacement is absolutely possible — but non-standard. Small and differently shaped bones often need **custom or small-size implants planned from a CT scan**, done at high-volume skeletal dysplasia centers; published series report good outcomes with custom femoral stems (International Orthopaedics, 2012). The practical rule: **do not accept "you're too small for a joint replacement" from a general orthopedist** without getting a dysplasia-center opinion. And any joint surgery invokes the anesthesia precautions below.

## Anesthesia and surgery: every procedure, every time \{#anesthesia}

This never expires. Whether it is a knee replacement at 45, a colonoscopy at 55, or an emergency appendectomy at any age, the same precautions apply — and after the transition out of pediatrics, *you* are usually the one who has to raise them. Many adults carry a wallet card or medical-ID summary (there is a printable version on the [Warning Signs page](/warning-signs), and Little People of America publishes anesthesia guidance). The essentials the anesthesia team must know, drawn from the International Consensus Statement (2022):

- **Potentially difficult airway:** small mouth opening, midface hypoplasia, narrow nasal passages, large tongue, short neck, limited neck extension. Smaller-than-predicted breathing tubes are often needed, and bag-mask ventilation can be difficult. Difficult-airway equipment should be in the room before starting.
- **Protect the neck:** the craniocervical junction — the opening at the base of the skull (the foramen magnum) and upper cervical canal — is narrow for life. The neck must never be hyperextended; video laryngoscopy is recommended so intubation does not require bending the neck.
- **Dose medications by weight and body size**, never by age- or height-based formulas. IV access can be genuinely difficult; say so up front.
- **Screen for sleep apnea before surgery** — OSA raises post-operative breathing risk and may warrant overnight observation after sedation.
- **List every medicine** — including weight-loss medicines such as semaglutide (which slow stomach emptying) and sedating pain medicines such as gabapentin or pregabalin.
- **Spinal and epidural (neuraxial) anesthesia need special caution:** the congenitally narrow canal makes placement technically difficult and local-anesthetic spread unpredictable. Anyone considering neuraxial anesthesia should be screened for stenosis symptoms first.
- **For anything elective, choose experience:** prefer hospitals and anesthesiologists who have managed achondroplasia or other skeletal dysplasias before.

## Pregnancy, fertility, and building a family \{#pregnancy}

Start with the good news: **fertility and sexual health are generally normal.** Puberty happens on the usual timetable, menstrual patterns in women with achondroplasia match the general population, and men with achondroplasia have no documented fertility defect. People with achondroplasia date, marry, and raise children; what differs is the planning around pregnancy itself.

### Before pregnancy: genetics and options

Preconception genetic counseling is strongly recommended, and from adolescence onward everyone should be offered a clear discussion of inheritance (International Consensus Statement, 2022). The math is simple because achondroplasia is autosomal dominant (see [Understanding It](/understanding)):

- **One parent with achondroplasia:** each pregnancy has a 50% chance of achondroplasia and 50% of average stature.
- **Both parents with achondroplasia:** 25% average stature, 50% achondroplasia, 25% homozygous achondroplasia — a double dose of the gene change that is lethal in the newborn period. This is the scenario in which testing matters most, and surveys show affected couples use prenatal testing chiefly to detect it.

Because \~98–99% of achondroplasia comes from the same identifiable FGFR3 variant, testing is straightforward: chorionic villus sampling (CVS) at about 11–13 weeks, amniocentesis from about 15 weeks, cell-free DNA screening from the mother's blood in many locations, and preimplantation genetic testing (PGT-M) with IVF — testing embryos before transfer. PGT-M realities worth knowing: IVF success is age-dependent and rarely above 50% per cycle, a family DNA workup is needed first, and US costs often run $20,000–30,000+ per cycle with inconsistent insurance coverage. The community holds a range of views — some couples use PGT-M to select against the lethal homozygous form only, some to select an unaffected child, and others decline testing entirely. Nondirective counseling (laying out options without steering) is the standard, and the choice is genuinely yours.

### Pregnancy in women with achondroplasia

Pregnancy is manageable — and it is high-risk care that needs a plan, ideally made before conception (pre-conception evaluation, per the International Consensus Statement, 2022). The key elements:

- **Obstetrician-led care** (not midwife-only), ideally at a center experienced with skeletal dysplasia.
- **Breathing is watched, especially in the third trimester.** A smaller rib cage plus a growing uterus can compromise breathing; GeneReviews notes a slightly increased risk of respiratory failure and recommends a pulmonology consultation early in pregnancy, with baseline lung-function testing and respiratory monitoring as the pregnancy advances. Heart and skeletal status are monitored too.
- **Serial ultrasounds**, including at least one in the third trimester to look for achondroplasia in the baby.
- **Earlier planned delivery is common.** A 2026 review of the published studies found most deliveries were planned for 35–37 weeks, and 12.5–40% of births were brought forward because of breathing problems or preeclampsia. Standard pregnancy weight-gain charts do not apply; the review suggests a total gain of about 5–9 kg is reasonable.
- **Delivery:** by **planned caesarean section at a hospital with critical-care facilities** (GeneReviews, 2026). The International Consensus Statement is specific: any delivery beyond 32 weeks should be by caesarean, because the pelvis in achondroplasia cannot safely pass a baby's head, whether or not the baby has achondroplasia; before 32 weeks the team weighs the baby's size against the pelvis (Recommendation 19). The planned caesarean does not routinely need to be before term — timing is set by the expert team (Recommendation 20).
- **Anesthesia is a decision for experts, made in advance.** Both regional (spinal/epidural) and general anesthesia carry increased risk in achondroplasia — the narrow canal makes neuraxial placement difficult and dosing unpredictable, while general anesthesia brings the difficult-airway and neck-protection issues above. Either can be used depending on the situation and expert judgment. Encouragingly, a 2025 review of 80 published caesarean anesthetics found that 58 of 64 spinal or epidural anesthetics worked without needing conversion to general anesthesia; low blood pressure and temporary tingling occurred but were infrequent. The authors advise a reduced, step-by-step (titratable) dose. Case reports describe stenosis symptoms worsening after a spinal anesthetic, so existing stenosis symptoms should be reviewed beforehand. The non-negotiable: a **planned antenatal anesthesiology consultation** with someone experienced in short-statured patients, well before the due date.
- **Blood volume is smaller**, so even moderate blood loss can destabilize circulation; the team plans careful fluid management.
- **After delivery:** a baby of a parent with achondroplasia is examined and, if indicated, tested — if the diagnosis is confirmed, the [First Years page](/first-years) covers what comes next. The consensus also recommends adapting the postnatal environment (height-adjusted beds, cots, and toilets) so the new parent can care for the baby independently; the [Everyday Life page](/daily-living) has more on adaptive parenting equipment.

### Men with achondroplasia, and other paths

For fathers with achondroplasia the reproductive conversation is the same 50% inheritance chance and the same testing options — but with no delivery-related precautions, since pregnancy physiology is unaffected. Partner testing matters only if the partner also has a dominant skeletal dysplasia (a different compound risk, worth a genetics consult). And adoption is a well-trodden path: Little People of America's Adoption Committee, active since about 1961, has facilitated hundreds of placements of children with dwarfism, with grants available.

## Work and your rights \{#work}

Adults with achondroplasia work in every field. The law, in most countries, is on your side:

- **United States:** dwarfism is a recognized condition under the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA/ADAAA). Employers with 15 or more employees may not discriminate against a qualified person who can perform the essential functions of the job, and must provide **reasonable accommodations** unless doing so causes undue hardship. Typical accommodations are cheap and simple: step stools, adjustable-height desks and chairs, lowered workstation elements, reachers, small keyboards, accessible restroom modifications. The Job Accommodation Network ([askjan.org](https://askjan.org)) offers free, expert accommodation consulting; the EEOC enforces complaints.
- **Elsewhere:** the UK Equality Act 2010, the EU Employment Equality Directive (2000/78/EC), Australia's Disability Discrimination Act 1992, and Canada's human-rights acts provide broadly equivalent protection.
- **Disclosure is your call.** Requesting an accommodation does mean telling your employer about your condition; when, how, and how much to share beyond that is a personal choice, and organizations like LPA offer peer guidance on navigating it.

A reality check from the research, because it points at prevention: in older studies summarized in a 2020 review, about half of adults with achondroplasia worked full-time (US survey, 2003) and about 40% received disability or age pensions (small Australian study, 1981) — with pain, fatigue, and spinal stenosis described as the main work-limiting factors. That is one more argument for early spine care and weight management, because adults consistently describe work as "a key resource for well-being" (European Achondroplasia Forum, 2022). For the practical side — workstation ergonomics, tools, and home adaptations — see [Everyday Life](/daily-living).

## Healthy aging \{#aging}

Nothing about achondroplasia prevents living independently into old age. Aging well is mostly ordinary geriatrics with a few condition-specific twists:

- **Mobility aids are tools, not defeat.** Many older adults use canes, crutches, scooters, or wheelchairs for distance. Adopted early and on your own terms, they preserve energy, joints, and independence — the community is emphatic on this point. Physical therapy for strength and balance, and fall prevention, matter as in all aging, amplified by shorter reach and joint laxity.
- **Future-proof the home.** The standard aging-in-place review — grab bars, rails, lighting, step elimination — with the twist that everything must be mounted at heights you can actually use. An occupational therapist home assessment is worth its weight; see [Everyday Life](/daily-living).
- **Menopause:** there is no achondroplasia-specific menopause literature; timing and symptoms are assumed typical. The practical intersection is weight — menopause-era weight gain worsens stenosis and sleep apnea — and hormone-therapy decisions follow general-population rules. Post-menopausal bone health should still be assessed, but at a center that understands the DXA measurement caveats above.
- **Cancer screening is standard.** There is no increased malignancy risk in achondroplasia, so mammograms, colonoscopy, and the rest follow the general-population schedule (International Consensus Statement, 2022). The accommodations are logistical: mammography and scan positioning may need adjustment, and colonoscopy sedation invokes the standard airway and sleep-apnea precautions — tell the endoscopy team you have achondroplasia when you book, not on the day.
- **Advance care planning, normalized.** The same planning everyone should do — healthcare proxy, advance directive — plus one addition: document your anesthesia and airway precautions in the advance-care record, because an emergency intubation decision may one day be made by strangers who have never met you.
- **Keep the paper trail.** A patient-held record (the EAF checklist plus your own history of surgeries, imaging, and sleep studies) becomes more valuable with every decade.

## Mental health and quality of life \{#mental-health}

The honest data: adults with achondroplasia report lower self-esteem and higher rates of anxiety and depression than the general population. In a small US study (25 adults), 56% had a diagnosed psychiatric illness — nearly three times the general-population rate. A larger 2025 study in the US, UK, and Norway found a past psychiatric diagnosis in 31% of adults with achondroplasia or hypochondroplasia, and current clinically significant depression in about 1 in 4 and anxiety in about 1 in 8 adults with skeletal dysplasia — well above general-population rates. Physical quality-of-life scores tend to decline from about age 30 before plateauing after 50. GeneReviews recommends discussing social adjustment at each visit, and the International Consensus Statement (2022) recommends psychosocial support be offered as part of routine care across the lifespan, with quality of life assessed routinely.

The equally honest counterweight: the same research identifies what protects mental health — managed pain, meaningful work, and above all **community connection**. Many adults name peer connection through Little People of America and its international equivalents as the single most helpful psychosocial resource they have. If your mood, sleep, or engagement with life is slipping, treat it exactly like a stenosis symptom: real, common, and treatable — raise it. The [Wellbeing & Community page](/wellbeing) covers organizations, meetups, and finding a therapist who gets it.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Mental health emergency — act now">
  If you are thinking about suicide or self-harm, or are worried about someone who is, get help now: in the US or Canada call or text **988**; in the UK or Ireland call Samaritans on **116 123**; in Australia call Lifeline on **13 11 14**; elsewhere, your local emergency number. Do not wait for a scheduled appointment.
</Callout>

## Your adult monitoring schedule \{#monitoring}

GeneReviews recommends adults be seen every 3–5 years (more often if symptomatic) by a clinician familiar with achondroplasia; the International Consensus Statement (2022) recommends individualized lifelong follow-up. Between specialist visits, this is what an annual check with any primary care doctor should cover — bring this table, or the [care checklist](/checklist):

| Check                                            | How often                                                  | Why / what to ask for                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Spinal stenosis symptom review + neurologic exam | Every visit (at least annually)                            | Leg pain/numbness on walking relieved by bending forward, walking distance, bladder/bowel changes. Symptoms → whole-spine MRI and an experienced spine center. |
| Blood pressure                                   | Every visit                                                | With a properly sized cuff — narrower cuff or forearm measurement. Historically elevated cardiovascular mortality; treat on standard thresholds.               |
| Weight and lifestyle counseling                  | Every visit                                                | Weight trend and waist size, not standard BMI category (no adult achondroplasia references exist). Weight is the strongest modifiable factor for spine, sleep, and joints.                                   |
| Sleep apnea screen                               | Annually; sleep study when suspected                       | Snoring, witnessed pauses, daytime sleepiness, resistant hypertension → overnight sleep study; CPAP if confirmed.                                              |
| Hearing evaluation                               | Periodically, starting earlier than the general population | \~40% have significant loss; hearing aids are underused and effective.                                                                                         |
| Pain and fatigue assessment                      | Every visit                                                | Brief Pain Inventory or similar; multidisciplinary management, not painkillers alone.                                                                          |
| Mental health check                              | Every encounter                                            | Depression and anxiety are more common than in the general population and treatable.                                                                           |
| Routine adult preventive care                    | Standard schedule                                          | Cancer screening, vaccines, lipids, diabetes — same as everyone, with positioning/sedation accommodations arranged in advance.                                 |
| Whole-spine MRI                                  | As needed (symptom-driven)                                 | For new or progressing neurologic symptoms — not a routine screening test in asymptomatic adults.                                                              |
| Pre-surgery / pre-pregnancy planning             | Before every procedure or pregnancy                        | Anesthesia consultation every time; genetic counseling and obstetric planning before pregnancy.                                                                |

<Callout type="tip" title="The one-paragraph version">
  Know the stenosis pattern (leg symptoms walking, relief bending forward — and bladder/bowel changes are an emergency). Get your blood pressure taken with the right cuff at every visit. Treat weight as spine-and-heart medicine. Ask for a sleep study and a hearing test rather than waiting to be offered them. Tell every surgical team about your airway and neck, every time. And do not disappear from follow-up — the conditions of adulthood reward whoever is paying attention.
</Callout>
