Start with the honest picture: almost every serious complication of achondroplasia announces itself with recognizable signs, and almost every one is treatable when it is caught early. Historical studies found that *unmonitored* infants faced an excess risk of death of roughly 2–7.5% in early life — and that with modern screening and prompt treatment, that risk falls to as little as 0.3% (GeneReviews). The difference between those two numbers is not luck. It is families and clinicians who knew what to watch for and acted without hesitating. That is the entire purpose of this page.

Bookmark it, print it, and share it with grandparents, babysitters, teachers, and — when the time comes — the young person with achondroplasia themselves. The routine screening schedule that prevents most of these emergencies from ever happening lives on the [age-by-age care checklist](/checklist).

## How to use this page: three levels of urgency \{#how-to-use}

Every warning sign below is tagged with one of three actions. Learn the tiers first, and the rest of the page becomes simple.

<UrgencyLadder caption="Every warning sign on this page belongs to one of three tiers, from minutes to the next appointment. If you are unsure which tier applies, choose the one above." />

<Callout type="emergency" title="Tier 1 — Call emergency services now">
  Signs where minutes matter: stopped or pausing breathing, color change, sudden unresponsiveness, loss of bladder and bowel control with numbness. Call your local emergency number or go straight to the emergency department. Do not wait to reach your specialist first — call, go, and notify the specialist from the hospital.
</Callout>

<Callout type="caution" title="Tier 2 — Same-day medical contact">
  Signs that are not usually a minutes-count emergency but must not wait for a routine appointment: new weakness, feeding deterioration, breathing changes during sleep, a bulging fontanelle. Call your pediatrician, skeletal dysplasia clinic, or primary care team *today*. If you cannot reach anyone, use urgent care or the emergency department — moving up a tier is always the right mistake.
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Tier 3 — Mention at the next visit">
  Changes worth writing down and raising at the next scheduled appointment: a slowly recurring ear-fluid pattern, mild snoring without pauses, gradual hearing changes, a weight trend. Not dangerous today, but they change what surveillance your care team should do next.
</Callout>

One rule sits above all three: **when in doubt, move up a tier.** Nobody at a skeletal dysplasia clinic has ever been annoyed by a family who called too early about a breathing pause or new weakness.

## Babies and toddlers (birth to 2): what to watch \{#babies}

Infancy is the highest-stakes period, because the opening at the base of the skull (the **foramen magnum**) is at its narrowest and can press on the brainstem — the region that controls breathing (doctors call this **cervicomedullary compression**). This is exactly why the newborn sleep study, craniocervical imaging, and the handling rules described on the [First Years page](/first-years) exist. Two reassurances before the lists: many babies with significant narrowing have *no* symptoms — which is why the screening schedule, not parental vigilance alone, carries the load — and most babies never need surgery. Craniocervical decompression is performed in roughly 7–13% of infants in the first 2 years at experienced centers; around 20% of people lifetime in one large US cohort, with wide variation between centers (from about 5% to over 40%, highest at specialist referral centers). Every baby needs the screening; most will only ever need the screening.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Tier 1 — call emergency services now if a baby with achondroplasia:">
  • Stops breathing, or has long pauses in breathing\
  • Turns blue or gray around the lips or face — during feeds, sleep, or crying\
  • Becomes suddenly, extremely floppy, unresponsive, or impossible to rouse\
  • Loses skills they previously had (rolling, reaching, babbling — any regression)

  These can be signs of brainstem compression, and sudden infant death is the risk being prevented. While waiting for help: support the head and neck, keep them in a neutral position — neither chin-to-chest nor tipped back — and tell the crew immediately: *"achondroplasia — narrow foramen magnum — do not extend the neck."*
</Callout>

<Callout type="info" title="Normal for achondroplasia — not warning signs by themselves">
  A big head (macrocephaly) is expected — judge it only on achondroplasia-specific charts. The fontanelle normally stays open late, sometimes to age 5–6. Sweating during sleep, on its own, is common and normal. Motor milestones arrive later and by unusual routes — sitting alone around 9–14 months, walking around 16–30 months (nearly all by 36 months), "snowplowing" on the forehead, log rolling, bottom scooting — all normal variants. Feet turning outward in infancy self-corrects with weight-bearing. Two-word phrases arrive anywhere from 20–31 months; language is typically on time or only mildly delayed. The full developmental picture is on the [First Years page](/first-years).
</Callout>

## Children (2–12): what to watch \{#children}

By the school years the most dangerous period is usually behind you, and the watch-list shifts from the brainstem to the spine, the airway, and the ears — the territory covered in depth on the [Childhood page](/childhood). The nervous-system signs below are rare at this age, which is exactly why they are easy to dismiss as growing pains or a phase. Don't.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Tier 1 — treat as an emergency">
  • **Any head or neck injury** — a fall on the head, a sports collision, a car accident. The craniocervical junction is the vulnerable point in achondroplasia, so the threshold for emergency assessment and craniocervical imaging is deliberately low. Tell the team: no neck hyperextension.\
  • **Struggling to breathe, long pauses in breathing with a color change, or a child who is hard to wake.** (Snoring with pauses or gasping, without these, is a same-day call — see the table below.)\
  • **A sudden inability to walk, or new loss of bladder or bowel control together with leg weakness or numbness** — possible spinal-cord compression.\
  • **Morning headaches with repeated vomiting** — needs prompt emergency assessment.
</Callout>

### Tier 3 — book an appointment or raise at the next visit

- **A back curve that looks worse** — a persisting or deepening round-back (thoracolumbar kyphosis) after walking is established — book a pediatric orthopedic review with standing spine X-rays. Some rounding remains in up to about 30% of children and roughly 10–15% have a curve that progresses; a fixed curve can worsen adult spinal stenosis.
- **Leg bowing with pain, a limp, or a "thrust"** (an uncontrolled sideways lurch of the knee with each step) — pediatric orthopedics; correction options are best while growth plates are open. See [Childhood](/childhood) and [Treatments](/treatments).
- **A recurring ear-fluid pattern without acute pain**, or any concern about hearing or speech — annual audiology is already on the [checklist](/checklist); move it up if you are noticing "what?" more often. Untreated fluid muffles hearing in the exact window speech is learned.
- **Mild snoring without pauses** — note it, describe it at the next visit, and film a short clip of the sleep breathing to show the clinician.
- **Mood changes, teasing, school avoidance** — not a medical emergency but never "just kids being kids"; see [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing).

## Teens and adults: what to watch \{#teens-adults}

The defining medical issue of adulthood is **spinal stenosis** — the born-narrow spinal canal narrowing further with ordinary age-related wear until it squeezes the nerves. Norwegian population data found symptomatic stenosis in 68% of adults, with a median age of *first* symptoms of 33 — and a range starting at age 10. Teach teenagers these symptoms before they leave pediatric care: the person most likely to catch stenosis early is the one living in the body. The same study found an average of *9 years* between first symptom and surgery, and outcomes are consistently better the sooner surgery follows symptom onset. Full context is on the [Adults & Aging page](/adults) and the [Teen Years page](/teens).

<Callout type="emergency" title="Tier 1 — cauda equina syndrome: go to the emergency department now">
  • New numbness in the saddle area (groin, inner thighs, the skin that would touch a bicycle seat)\
  • New trouble controlling — or emptying — bladder or bowels\
  • Rapidly worsening leg weakness, or a foot that slaps or drops\
  These signal compression of the nerve bundle at the bottom of the spine (**cauda equina syndrome**), a surgical emergency: damage can become irreversible. In the Norwegian stenosis cohort, 41% of affected adults had urinary incontinence and 21% bowel incontinence — bladder and bowel involvement means the compression is already advanced. At the hospital, say the exact words: *"I have achondroplasia with congenital spinal stenosis. These are new saddle numbness and bladder symptoms — possible cauda equina — I need an emergency MRI."*
</Callout>

### Tier 3 — raise at the next visit

- **Gradual hearing decline** — about 40% of adults have functionally significant loss, mostly the mechanical (conductive) kind, yet in one Norwegian study 53% of adults had hearing loss but only 20% used hearing aids. It is a treatable, isolating problem: ask for audiology.
- **Creeping chronic pain or fatigue** — pain affects most adults (64–75% across studies) and deserves a plan, not stoicism: see [Adults & Aging](/adults).
- **Weight trend** — weight control is the single most powerful lever an adult holds against stenosis, sleep apnea, and heart risk; ask that blood pressure be taken with a properly sized cuff (standard adult cuffs fit short upper arms poorly and can distort readings).
- **Low mood or anxiety** — more common than in the general population and worth naming out loud; screening at every encounter is the guideline standard. See [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing).

## Pregnancy: warning signs and the delivery plan \{#pregnancy}

Pregnancy in a woman with achondroplasia is joyful, common — and formally high-risk, which means obstetrician-led care at a center experienced with skeletal dysplasia, never midwife-only care. Three things must be planned in advance rather than decided in the moment:

- **Delivery is by caesarean section.** The pelvis cannot safely pass a baby's head, whatever the baby's stature. Any delivery from about 32 weeks onward should be by planned caesarean section at a hospital with critical-care facilities; before 32 weeks, decisions need individualized expert assessment.
- **Anesthesia is planned antenatally.** Every pregnant woman with achondroplasia should have a scheduled anesthesiology consultation well before delivery (International Consensus recommendation) with someone experienced in short-statured patients. Both general anesthesia (difficult airway, neck protection) and spinal/epidural anesthesia (narrow canal, difficult placement, unpredictable dosing) carry achondroplasia-specific challenges; modern reports show neuraxial anesthesia usually succeeds — 58 of 64 published cesarean cases — but placement was often difficult and ultrasound guidance with careful incremental dosing is advised. There is no single right answer; there is only a plan made early.
- **Breathing is monitored in late pregnancy.** The small rib cage plus the growing uterus can compromise breathing; guidelines recommend a pulmonology consultation early in pregnancy with respiratory monitoring, especially in the third trimester.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Tier 1 — during pregnancy, go to the hospital now if:">
  • **Labor starts, or you think it might have.** Do not wait at home to be sure. Go to the hospital where your delivery plan is held — a pregnant woman with achondroplasia presenting in labor needs cesarean capability and an anesthesiologist informed of the difficult-airway and spinal-anesthesia issues *immediately*. Bring your anesthesia plan and notes.\
  • **Breathing difficulty that is new or worsening**, especially in the third trimester.\
  • Any standard obstetric emergency — bleeding, severe headache with visual changes, reduced fetal movement — on the same rules as any pregnancy.
</Callout>

Genetic counseling before or during pregnancy (inheritance odds, prenatal testing options) is covered on the [Adults page](/adults) and [Understanding It](/understanding).

## The ER quick card: print this and hand it over \{#er-card}

Families consistently report that emergency departments unfamiliar with achondroplasia make predictable, avoidable mistakes — extending the neck, misreading normal imaging findings as disease, using the wrong dosing logic. The anesthesia literature explicitly recommends that people with achondroplasia carry an alert card or medical-ID bracelet warning of the difficult airway and the need to avoid neck extension. The block below is written for the clinician, not for you: **print this page (Ctrl+P or Cmd+P — the print stylesheet strips the menus and leaves the content) and hand it over**, or photograph the card and keep it on your phone's lock-screen notes.

<Callout type="emergency" title="Emergency information — patient with achondroplasia (FGFR3 skeletal dysplasia)">
  **About this patient:** Achondroplasia is a bone-growth condition. Intelligence is normal — speak to the patient (or child) normally, by age, not by size.

  **NECK — the single most important line on this card:** The foramen magnum and cervical canal are congenitally narrow. Do NOT hyperextend the neck — during intubation, positioning, or cervical-collar fitting. Use video laryngoscopy and in-line stabilization. After any head or neck trauma, keep a low threshold for craniocervical imaging: the craniocervical junction is the vulnerable point.

  **AIRWAY:** Expect a difficult airway — midface hypoplasia, small nasopharynx, relatively large tongue, limited neck extension, possible poor mask fit. Have the difficult-airway kit ready and use a smaller-than-age/height-predicted endotracheal tube. Involve the most experienced anesthetist available.

  **BREATHING, SEDATION & OPIOIDS:** Obstructive sleep apnea is highly prevalent in achondroplasia and the thorax is small, so respiratory reserve is lower — use sedatives and opioids cautiously, extubate fully awake, and observe overnight after sedation or airway procedures.

  **DRUGS:** Dose by actual body weight — never by age- or height-based formulas.

  **SPINE:** Congenital spinal stenosis exists at all levels. Avoid spinal or epidural anesthesia (and approach lumbar puncture with caution) unless imaging confirms adequate canal space. New leg weakness, numbness, or any bladder–bowel change = possible cord or cauda equina compression → emergency MRI.

  **BLOOD PRESSURE & IV ACCESS:** Standard adult cuffs fit the short upper arm poorly — use a narrower cuff on the upper or lower arm segment. IV access may be difficult (limited elbow extension).

  **IMAGING — what is NORMAL in achondroplasia (do not over-read):** Macrocephaly, mild ventriculomegaly, and extra-axial fluid are baseline findings — do not shunt on ventricle size alone (AAP 2020). Narrowing lumbar interpedicular distance, a "champagne-glass" pelvis, and short vertebral pedicles are baseline skeletal findings, not acute pathology. Compare head circumference only to achondroplasia-specific charts.

  **INFANTS:** Apnea, cyanosis, profound floppiness, or feeding-related choking → treat as possible cervicomedullary (brainstem) compression: involve pediatric neurosurgery and handle the head and neck with extreme care.

  **PREGNANCY:** A pregnant woman with achondroplasia presenting in labor needs cesarean capability and an anesthesiologist informed of the difficult-airway and neuraxial issues immediately.

  **CONTACTS & MEDICATIONS (family: fill these in on your printed copy):** Skeletal dysplasia clinic \_\_\_\_\_\_ · Neurosurgeon (if followed) \_\_\_\_\_\_ · Medication list \_\_\_\_\_\_ · Growth medication and time of last dose \_\_\_\_\_\_

  **GROWTH MEDICATIONS:** Vosoritide (Voxzogo, daily injection) and navepegritide (Yuviwel, weekly injection) can both cause a transient drop in blood pressure — consider this if the patient is dizzy, tired, nauseated, or hypotensive, and note when the last dose was given. Before planned surgery, contact the prescribing team about whether to pause treatment.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Ready-made documents worth carrying">
  The European Achondroplasia Forum publishes a patient-held adult health checklist (Advances in Therapy, 2024), and Little People of America maintains printable anesthesia and medical guidance pages — both are designed to be handed to unfamiliar clinicians. Links live in the [Medical Library](/medical-library). A medical-ID bracelet noting "achondroplasia — difficult airway — do not extend neck" covers the scenario where you cannot speak for yourself.
</Callout>

## Before any planned surgery: the anesthesia checklist \{#surgery-checklist}

Every operation — ear tubes, dental work under sedation, appendectomy, spinal decompression — invokes the same precautions, at every age. Guidelines are blunt about the fix: elective procedures should happen where anesthesiologists and surgeons familiar with skeletal dysplasia work. This section is educational, not medical advice — decisions about any procedure belong with your own care team, ideally one experienced with achondroplasia. Ask these questions out loud at the pre-operative visit, and treat hesitant answers as a reason to ask for a referral:

- **Experience:** Has this anesthesia team cared for people with achondroplasia or another skeletal dysplasia? If not, will they consult with a center that has?
- **The neck:** Is the plan explicit that the neck will not be hyperextended, with video laryngoscopy (and fiberoptic backup) available so intubation does not require bending it?
- **Tube sizes:** Are smaller-than-predicted endotracheal tubes stocked and planned for?
- **Dosing:** Will all drugs be dosed by actual body weight, not age- or height-based formulas?
- **Spinal/epidural:** If neuraxial anesthesia is being considered, has imaging confirmed adequate canal space, and have stenosis symptoms been screened for? The default in achondroplasia is to avoid it unless both are true.
- **Sleep apnea:** Has the most recent sleep study been shared? Untreated sleep apnea raises post-operative breathing risk — the plan should include fully-awake extubation and overnight observation after sedation or airway procedures.
- **IV access and monitoring:** Is the team aware elbows may not fully extend (harder IV placement) and that blood-pressure cuffs need correct sizing? For operations longer than about an hour, will spinal-cord (neurophysiology) monitoring be used? The 2026 UK consensus recommends it.
- **Growth medication:** If the person takes vosoritide or navepegritide, has the prescribing team been asked whether to pause it around the operation (2025 vosoritide guidelines)?
- **Ear surgery specifically:** Does the ENT surgeon know about the raised risk of jugular bulb dehiscence — a large vein lacking its bony roof in the middle ear — which can cause serious bleeding during ear-tube placement?
- **Paperwork:** Bring the FGFR3 gene result, the latest sleep study, craniocervical or spine imaging reports, the medication list, and the ER card above.

The perioperative best-practice paper this checklist distills (White et al., 2017) is linked in the [Medical Library](/medical-library) — surgeons respond well to being handed the actual reference. For what the operations themselves involve, see [Treatments](/treatments).

## When you're not sure, call \{#not-sure}

Err toward calling. The entire safety record of modern achondroplasia care — infant mortality falling from as much as 7.5% to as little as 0.3% — was built by acting on ambiguous signs early, not by waiting for certainty. A phone call that turns out to be nothing costs ten minutes; the reverse mistake is the one this page exists to prevent.

- **Keep your specialist's on-call number on the ER card** — your skeletal dysplasia clinic (and neurosurgeon, if you have one) are the contact lines emergency staff will actually use.
- **Take symptoms seriously even when local doctors are unfamiliar.** Most clinicians will meet one or two patients with achondroplasia in a career. If a red-flag symptom is being waved off, say so plainly, ask for the specific test this page names (sleep study, craniocervical MRI, whole-spine MRI), and bring the published guidelines — the AAP 2020 health-supervision report and the 2022 International Consensus Statement are exactly what they are for, and both are linked with summaries in the [Medical Library](/medical-library).
- **For a clinician who wants a fast orientation**, the two quickest credible documents are the 2025 JCEM review "Approach to the Patient with Achondroplasia" and the 2026 plain-language overview of the International Consensus Statement — both free, both in the [library](/medical-library).
- **Keep the records binder current** — genetic report, sleep studies, imaging reports, surgery notes. The same documents get requested for decades, and in an emergency they answer questions faster than memory does.
- **You are not alone in this.** Little People of America and its international equivalents are full of parents and adults who have made these exact calls; see [Wellbeing & Community](/wellbeing).

And the closing truth, worth repeating: most families never dial the emergency number. The screening schedule catches problems before they become crises, the handling rules prevent the ones that can be prevented, and the rest of life happens far away from this page. Knowing it cold is what lets you close the tab and go live it.
