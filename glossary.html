Glossary

Every medical term a family is likely to hear — explained in plain English, with links to the pages where each topic is covered in depth.

Doctors, therapists, and research papers use a lot of specialized vocabulary, and much of it will be new on day one. Knowing what the words mean turns a frightening conversation into an understandable one — and helps you ask better questions. Terms are listed alphabetically; most entries point you to the page of this guide where the topic is explained fully, and many link to related terms so you can follow a thread.

A note on language

"Little person" (LP), "person with dwarfism," and "person of short stature" are all accepted, respectful terms — and most people simply prefer their name. The word "midget" is considered a slur and should not be used. Throughout this guide we say "average-height" rather than "normal," because people with achondroplasia are exactly as normal as anyone else.

A note on treatment terms

Entries about medicines and surgery (vosoritide, navepegritide, infigratinib, limb lengthening, and others) describe the published facts only. This glossary is educational, not medical advice — decisions about starting, changing, or declining any treatment belong with your own care team, ideally one experienced in skeletal dysplasia. See Treatments for the full picture.

A–B

Accelerated approval

A regulatory pathway in which the FDA approves a drug based on an early, measurable marker of benefit — for achondroplasia drugs, faster growth (annualized growth velocity) — while the long-term evidence (effects on final height, complications, and quality of life) is still being collected. Both vosoritide and navepegritide were approved this way, and continued approval depends on confirmatory data. See Treatments.

Acanthosis nigricans

Dark, velvety patches of skin, typically in body folds such as the neck and armpits. It occurs in roughly 10% of people with achondroplasia and is harmless in this context; it is also a defining feature of the much rarer, more severe FGFR3 condition SADDAN.

Achondroplasia

The most common form of disproportionate short stature (skeletal dysplasia), occurring in about 1 in 21,000 to 1 in 28,000 births (~4.7 per 100,000; older sources quote 1 in 15,000–40,000). It is caused by a single-letter change in the FGFR3 gene that slows the conversion of cartilage into bone, mainly in the limbs — intelligence is normal, and most people lead full, independent lives. The name literally means "without cartilage formation," which is a misnomer: cartilage forms normally; converting it to bone is what is slowed. See Understanding It.

Adaptive physical education (adaptive PE)

A modified — not skipped — school physical-education program: a written note from the medical team lists activities to avoid (collision sports, trampolines, gymnastics with axial loading, diving) and safe alternatives to encourage (swimming, cycling, and most everything else). It can be built into a 504 plan. See Childhood.

Adenotonsillectomy

Surgical removal of the adenoids and tonsils — the usual first operation for obstructive sleep apnea in children with achondroplasia, because relatively large tonsils and adenoids crowd an already-small airway. About 25–40% of children with achondroplasia undergo adenotonsillectomy; counting all pharyngeal (throat) surgeries over a lifetime, around 45% of people have at least one. See Childhood.

Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA)

The US civil-rights law under which dwarfism is a recognized condition: employers with 15 or more employees may not discriminate against a qualified person and must provide reasonable accommodations (step stools, adjustable desks, reachers) unless doing so causes undue hardship. The Job Accommodation Network (askjan.org) offers free accommodation advice. Equivalent laws exist in the UK, EU, Australia, and Canada. See Adults & Aging.

Amniocentesis

A prenatal test in which a small sample of the fluid around the baby is drawn, from about 15 weeks of pregnancy onward, allowing definitive testing for the familial FGFR3 variant. It is invasive and carries a small procedure-related miscarriage risk (well under 1% in experienced hands). Compare chorionic villus sampling and NIPT. See Understanding It.

Apnea

A pause in breathing. There are two kinds that matter in achondroplasia: central apnea, where the brain fails to send the "breathe" signal (in infants, a possible sign of brainstem compression at the foramen magnum), and obstructive apnea, where the airway itself is blocked. The causes and treatments are completely different, which is why a sleep study is used to tell them apart. See Warning Signs.

Apnea-hypopnea index (AHI)

The number of breathing pauses (apneas) and partial obstructions (hypopneas) per hour of sleep, measured during a sleep study — the standard yardstick for how severe sleep apnea is and whether a treatment worked. In one published series, adenotonsillectomy reduced children's AHI on average from about 14 to about 7 events per hour.

Audiometry (audiology assessment)

A formal hearing test performed by an audiologist. Because middle-ear fluid is nearly the rule in achondroplasia, guidelines call for formal behavioral audiometry by 9–12 months of age and then every year, ideally through the elementary-school years. See First Years and Childhood.

Autosomal dominant

An inheritance pattern in which a single altered copy of a gene — from either parent — causes the condition. For a parent with achondroplasia, each pregnancy has a 50% chance of achondroplasia, independent of previous children ("chance has no memory"). An unaffected sibling does not carry the gene change and cannot pass it on. See Understanding It.

BMI (body mass index) — and its big caveat

Weight divided by height squared, the usual screening number for body fat. Because BMI is height-dependent, it overestimates body fat in short-statured people, and standard cutoffs (like the "85th/95th percentile" rules) do not apply in achondroplasia — clinicians should use achondroplasia-specific BMI and weight-for-height curves instead. See growth charts and Childhood.

Brachydactyly

Short fingers — one of the characteristic hand findings in achondroplasia, along with the trident hand configuration. Small hands and shorter fingers are also why pencil grips, keyboards, and easy-fastening clothes can help at school. See Everyday Life.

C

Car bed

A crash-tested restraint that lets a baby travel lying flat instead of semi-reclined. Very small infants, or those with breathing concerns who fail a car-seat tolerance screen, may temporarily need one until they can safely tolerate a rear-facing car seat — ask the care team before hospital discharge. See First Years.

Cauda equina syndrome

A surgical emergency in which the bundle of nerves at the bottom of the spinal canal is compressed: warning signs are new trouble controlling urine or bowels, numbness in the groin or "saddle" area, rapidly worsening leg weakness, or foot drop. In an adult with achondroplasia these symptoms mean same-day emergency care — delay risks permanent damage. See Warning Signs and Adults & Aging.

Central sleep apnea

Breathing pauses caused by the brain failing to send the "breathe" signal — in infants with achondroplasia, a possible sign that the brainstem is being squeezed at the cervicomedullary junction. It is the dangerous form of apnea in early infancy, and when significant it is treated with decompression surgery, not with airway treatments. See First Years.

Cephalopelvic disproportion

A mismatch between the size of the baby's head and the size of the mother's pelvis. Because women with achondroplasia have a characteristically small pelvis, even an average-sized head cannot safely pass — the reason delivery is by planned cesarean section. See Adults & Aging.

Cervicomedullary junction (and compression)

The region where the lower brainstem meets the upper spinal cord, passing through the foramen magnum at the base of the skull — the area that controls breathing. In achondroplasia this opening is small, and in a minority of infants it squeezes ("compresses") the brainstem, which can cause central apnea, weakness, and — historically, before screening — sudden infant death. This is THE critical issue of infancy and the reason every baby gets a neurologic exam, sleep study, and imaging. See First Years.

Cesarean section (C-section)

Surgical delivery of a baby through the abdomen. For a pregnant woman who herself has achondroplasia, any delivery from about 32 weeks onward should be by planned cesarean section at a hospital with critical-care facilities (before 32 weeks, individualized expert assessment applies), because of cephalopelvic disproportion. An average-height mother carrying a baby with achondroplasia can often deliver vaginally, though cesarean may be needed for the baby's large head. See Adults & Aging.

Chondrocyte

A cartilage cell. Chondrocytes in the growth plates multiply, mature, and are converted into bone — the process (endochondral ossification) that overactive FGFR3 slows down in achondroplasia. See Understanding It.

Chorionic villus sampling (CVS)

A prenatal test taking a tiny sample of the placenta at 11–13 weeks of pregnancy, allowing definitive genetic testing earlier than amniocentesis. Like amniocentesis it is invasive, done at specialized centers, and carries a small procedure-related miscarriage risk. See Understanding It.

CLARITY

The Achondroplasia Natural History Study — a four-center US database of more than 1,300 people with achondroplasia spanning 60 years of records. It anchors the modern numbers for complication and surgery rates you will see quoted throughout this guide. See Medical Library.

Claudication (neurogenic)

Aching, burning, numbness, or weakness in the buttocks and legs brought on by walking or standing and — the classic clue — relieved by sitting, squatting, or leaning forward, which opens up the spinal canal. It is the hallmark symptom of spinal stenosis, and it must be distinguished from vascular claudication (poor blood flow), which eases just by standing still. See Adults & Aging.

Clinical trial phases

The stages of drug testing: Phase 1 checks safety and dosing; Phase 2 looks for early evidence the drug works; Phase 3 is the decisive randomized test against placebo; an open-label extension then offers the drug to everyone who took part. Every legitimate trial is registered on clinicaltrials.gov and never charges you for the experimental drug. See Treatments.

Clonus

A rhythmic, involuntary beating of the foot when it is flexed upward. A few beats can be normal in young infants, but sustained ankle clonus in a baby with achondroplasia is a red flag for spinal-cord irritation from cervicomedullary compression and warrants urgent review. See Warning Signs.

CNP (C-type natriuretic peptide)

The body's natural counter-signal to FGFR3: when CNP binds its receptor (NPR-B) on growth-plate cartilage cells, it switches off the overactive growth brake. Natural CNP is destroyed in minutes in the bloodstream, so it cannot be a drug itself — vosoritide (a longer-lasting analog) and navepegritide (a slow-release prodrug) are the two engineered solutions. See Treatments.

Conductive hearing loss

Hearing loss caused by a mechanical blockage — usually fluid behind the eardrum — rather than nerve damage. It is the common type in achondroplasia (about 40% of people develop functionally significant hearing loss), and it is largely treatable with ear tubes and hearing aids. Untreated fluid muffles hearing during the exact window when speech is learned. See Childhood.

CPAP (continuous positive airway pressure)

A bedside machine that gently blows air through a mask to hold the airway open during sleep — a mainstay treatment for obstructive sleep apnea at any age, used when adenotonsillectomy is not enough (BiPAP is a two-pressure variant). In adults with achondroplasia, treating sleep apnea also protects blood pressure and heart health. See Adults & Aging.

Craniocervical (cervicomedullary) decompression

Neurosurgery that removes a small rim of bone at the foramen magnum (sometimes with a patch to expand the covering membrane, called duraplasty) to give the compressed brainstem room. Roughly 7–13% of infants need it in the first 2 years at experienced centers; around 20% of people need it over a lifetime in one large US cohort, with wide variation between centers (5–43%). Outcomes at experienced pediatric neurosurgical centers are good, with marked improvement in neurologic function reported. See First Years.

CT (computed tomography)

An X-ray-based scan producing cross-sectional images — good at measuring bone, including the size of the foramen magnum against published achondroplasia norms, and it may avoid sedation. Its limitation: it does not show the brainstem, spinal cord, or spinal fluid well, which is why MRI is the preferred test for craniocervical screening. See First Years.

D–F

De novo mutation

A brand-new genetic change arising spontaneously in a single egg or sperm cell — not inherited, not caused by anything either parent did, and not preventable. About 80% of children with achondroplasia are born to two average-height parents this way. See Understanding It and paternal age effect.

DEXA / DXA (bone-density scan)

A low-dose X-ray scan (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) that measures bone density, used to screen for thinning bones. In achondroplasia, small studies suggest osteopenia can occur, but interpreting the scan requires size-adjustment expertise — standard reference values assume average-height bodies. See Adults & Aging.

Difficult airway

Anesthesia shorthand for a patient who may be hard to ventilate or intubate: in achondroplasia, midface hypoplasia (poor mask fit), a relatively large tongue, small nasal passages, a short neck, and limited neck extension all contribute, and smaller-than-age-predicted breathing tubes are often needed. It is one reason every surgery, anywhere, for anything, should be flagged to the anesthesia team in advance. See video laryngoscopy and Adults & Aging.

Disproportionate short stature

Short stature in which the limbs and trunk are affected unequally — in achondroplasia the trunk is near-average length while the limbs (especially the upper arms and thighs) are shortened. This is why sitting height looks typical while standing height does not. Compare proportionate short stature, where everything is smaller to the same degree. See Understanding It.

Dwarfism

An umbrella term for the roughly 400 recognized conditions causing short stature (the current medical catalog lists 436 genetic skeletal disorders); achondroplasia accounts for about 70% of cases. Accepted, respectful language: "little person," "person with dwarfism," "person of short stature" — the word "midget" is considered a slur. See Wellbeing & Community.

Endochondral ossification

The process by which a cartilage scaffold is converted into bone — how the long bones of the arms and legs, the spine, and the base of the skull grow. This is the process slowed by the overactive FGFR3 receptor, which explains nearly every feature of achondroplasia. Compare intramembranous ossification. See Understanding It.

ENT (otolaryngologist)

An ear-nose-throat surgeon. Families of children with achondroplasia get to know this specialist well: recurrent ear infections, ear tubes, and adenotonsillectomy for sleep apnea are all their territory. See Childhood.

Enuresis (secondary bed-wetting)

New bed-wetting in a child who was previously dry at night. In achondroplasia it is a red flag worth taking seriously — it can signal severe obstructive sleep apnea or, rarely, spinal-cord compression — and warrants prompt evaluation. See Warning Signs.

Eustachian tube

The narrow passage that drains and ventilates the middle ear. In achondroplasia these tubes are short and horizontally oriented, so they drain poorly — the root cause of the recurrent ear infections and fluid (otitis media with effusion) that affect 80–90% of young children. See First Years.

Feed-and-wrap MRI

A sedation-free technique for scanning young infants: the baby is fed, swaddled, and scanned while naturally asleep. It is feasible up to about 6 months of age, especially on fast scanners, and often allows the recommended craniocervical MRI without anesthesia. See First Years.

FGFR3 (fibroblast growth factor receptor 3)

The gene — and the receptor protein it encodes — at the heart of achondroplasia, located on chromosome 4. FGFR3 sits on growth-plate cartilage cells and normally acts as a brake on bone growth; in achondroplasia, a single-letter change (in ~99% of cases the same p.Gly380Arg or "G380R" variant) leaves that brake permanently stuck on. Because virtually everyone has the identical change, genetic testing is unusually simple and reliable. See Understanding It.

Fontanelle

The "soft spot" between an infant's skull bones. In achondroplasia it normally stays open later than usual — sometimes to age 5–6 years — which is fine; a bulging, tense, or hard fontanelle, however, is an urgent warning sign of raised pressure (hydrocephalus). See Warning Signs.

Foramen magnum

The opening at the base of the skull through which the brainstem and upper spinal cord pass. The skull base grows by cartilage-based growth, so in achondroplasia this opening is small at birth and its growth plates fuse early — in a minority of infants it becomes tight enough to squeeze the brainstem (cervicomedullary compression). Screening every infant with an exam, sleep study, and imaging is what makes this once-feared complication largely preventable. See First Years.

Frontal bossing

A prominent forehead — one of the characteristic facial features of achondroplasia, alongside macrocephaly and midface hypoplasia. It reflects the different growth of skull vault versus skull base, not anything about the brain inside.

G–H

Gain-of-function

A mutation that makes a protein overactive rather than broken. The achondroplasia change leaves the FGFR3 "growth brake" switched on continuously, even without its normal signal — plain English: the brake is stuck on. This is why treatments aim to release or bypass the brake rather than replace a missing part. See Understanding It.

Gene panel

A single test that reads many genes at once, used when a skeletal dysplasia diagnosis is unclear — there are over 400 to tell apart. Achondroplasia itself usually needs only targeted FGFR3 testing, because virtually all cases involve the same nucleotide; panels or full sequencing come in when anything looks atypical. See Understanding It.

Genetic counseling

A structured conversation with a genetics professional covering how the condition arose, the exact recurrence chances for your family, reproductive options, and what the future genuinely looks like — by guideline it should be accurate, unhurried, and nondirective (presenting options, not steering). Guidelines also say families should hear early that most people with achondroplasia lead productive, independent lives. See Understanding It.

Genu recurvatum

A knee that bends backward past straight when standing — a result of the loose ligaments common in achondroplasia. Together with bowing and tibial torsion it contributes to knee instability; an orthopedist assesses whether it needs anything beyond monitoring.

Genu varum (bowed legs)

Outward bowing of the legs — near-universal in achondroplasia (more than 90% of untreated adults have some degree) and usually painless. Referral to a pediatric orthopedist is warranted for a "thrust" (a lurch at the knee with each step), progressive or asymmetric deformity, or pain; correction options include guided growth and osteotomy. See Childhood.

GERD (gastroesophageal reflux disease)

Stomach contents washing back up the food pipe — more common in infants with achondroplasia than in average-stature infants, and it can aggravate airway obstruction. Persistent reflux warrants referral to a pediatric gastroenterologist or pulmonologist. Note: choking or gagging with feeds is never "just reflux" until brainstem compression has been considered. See First Years.

Gibbus

A sharp, angular hump in the spine — the fixed, wedge-shaped form that thoracolumbar kyphosis can take if it does not resolve. A fixed angular kyphosis matters long-term because it can worsen adult spinal stenosis, which is why persistent curves are braced and, rarely, operated on. See Childhood.

Gonadal (germline) mosaicism

The situation where a parent carries a mutation in only a fraction of their egg or sperm cells, but not in the rest of their body. It is the reason average-height parents of a child with de novo achondroplasia have a recurrence chance of about 1% — not zero — in future pregnancies. See Understanding It.

Growth charts (achondroplasia-specific)

Height, weight, head-circumference, and BMI curves built from children with achondroplasia (Horton 1978, Hoover-Fong 2007/2008/2017, Merker 2018, and others; all reproduced in the AAP report). Every measurement should be plotted on these — never on standard CDC/WHO charts, which would always look "abnormal" and hide real problems. The same rule applies to milestones: development is tracked against achondroplasia-specific milestone charts. See First Years and Medical Library.

Growth hormone

A hormone treatment that does not meaningfully help achondroplasia: children with achondroplasia are not growth-hormone deficient, and it is approved for this condition only in Japan (since 1997), where long-term data show final height gains of only about 3.5 cm in men and 2.8 cm in women. The AAP and international consensus do not recommend it for achondroplasia outside Japan. See Treatments.

Growth plate (physis)

The zone of cartilage near the ends of growing bones where new bone is made. Growth plates are where FGFR3 acts, where all the growth-promoting drugs work — and why those drugs only work while the plates are still open, i.e., before the end of puberty. "Open" versus "closed" growth plates is confirmed on X-ray. See Treatments.

Growth velocity

How fast a child grows, in centimeters per year. In untreated achondroplasia it runs a slow, steady ~4–5 cm/year through childhood with no pubertal growth spurt; annualized growth velocity is also the main endpoint the drug trials measured. See Childhood.

Guided growth (hemiepiphysiodesis)

A minimally invasive operation for bowed legs: a small "tension-band" plate is screwed across one side of the growth plate so the leg literally grows itself straight over 1–2 years, after which the plate is removed. Published correction rates are about 0.5° per month at the upper shin — but it only works while growth plates are open. Compare osteotomy. See Childhood.

Heterozygous

Carrying one changed copy and one typical copy of a gene. Ordinary achondroplasia is the heterozygous state — one altered FGFR3 copy is enough to cause it (see autosomal dominant). Compare homozygous achondroplasia.

Homozygous achondroplasia

Inheriting the FGFR3 change from both parents — a 25% chance in each pregnancy when both parents have achondroplasia. It is a much more severe, almost always lethal condition (babies are usually stillborn or die shortly after birth from respiratory failure and severe brainstem compression), which is why pre-conception counseling and prenatal testing are recommended for two-LP couples. See Understanding It.

Hydrocephalus

A true, progressive, pressure-raising buildup of cerebrospinal fluid that needs treatment — occurring in fewer than 5% of people with achondroplasia (shunt placement in about 4%). It must be distinguished from the harmless big head and extra fluid that are normal in achondroplasia (see benign ventriculomegaly); the alarm signs are head growth crossing upward through achondroplasia-specific percentiles, a bulging fontanelle, lethargy, vomiting, or developmental regression. See First Years.

Hyperreflexia

Overactive deep-tendon reflexes on examination. Brisk or asymmetric reflexes are an early physical clue to spinal-cord or nerve compression — the reason reflexes are checked at every visit in infancy and yearly through childhood. See Warning Signs.

Hypochondroplasia

A related but distinct FGFR3 condition (usually a different mutation, most often N540K) with the same pattern of features in a milder form — often not evident at birth, with fewer complications; about 10% have mild learning difficulties. Getting the exact diagnosis right matters: growth hormone may be considered in hypochondroplasia but does not work in achondroplasia. See Understanding It.

Hypotonia

Low muscle tone — "floppiness." Almost all infants with achondroplasia have mild-to-moderate hypotonia that improves over the first two years; it is the main reason motor milestones (sitting, standing, walking) come later, on achondroplasia's own normal timetable. Marked floppiness beyond what is usual, or new weakness, is a red flag. See First Years.

I–L

IEP and 504 plan

The two US school-support frameworks. A 504 plan provides accommodations — step stools, footrests, reachable storage, adaptive PE, restroom access — for a child who learns typically, and is what most children with achondroplasia need. An IEP (Individualized Education Program) adds specialized instruction and services such as school-based speech therapy, most often needed here for hearing-related speech delay. Both are legally binding and free, and parents are equal members of the team. See Childhood.

Infigratinib

A once-daily oral pill (BridgeBio) that directly quiets the overactive FGFR3 receptor — a tyrosine kinase inhibitor of FGFR1–3, at a small fraction of the dose once used in cancer care. Its Phase 3 trial (PROPEL 3) reported positive topline results in February 2026 (~+2.1 cm/yr height velocity difference), with regulatory submissions planned for the second half of 2026 — it is not yet approved anywhere. See Treatments.

Intramembranous ossification

Bone forming directly, without a cartilage scaffold — how much of the skull vault and the collarbones grow. Because this process does not depend on FGFR3's cartilage brake, those bones grow normally in achondroplasia — which is why the head is relatively large while the cartilage-grown skull base (and its foramen magnum) is small. See Understanding It.

Jugular bulb dehiscence

A large neck vein lacking its usual bony "roof" inside the middle ear — more common in achondroplasia, and it creates a small but real risk of serious bleeding during ear-tube surgery. The practical point: the ENT surgeon should always know the achondroplasia diagnosis in advance. See Childhood.

Kyphosis

An outward (rounding) curve of the spine — a "hump." In achondroplasia the important one is the infant thoracolumbar kyphosis, which usually resolves once walking begins; a curve that persists or stiffens is braced, and surgery is rarely needed. Compare lordosis, the inward curve. See First Years.

Laminectomy

Surgery removing the lamina — the bony roof of the spinal canal — along with thickened ligament, to un-pinch compressed nerves; in achondroplasia it is typically a wide, multi-level decompression for spinal stenosis, sometimes with fusion. Outcomes are consistently better the sooner surgery follows symptom onset, which is why recognizing neurogenic claudication early matters so much. See Adults & Aging.

Ligamentous laxity

Loose, stretchy ligaments, making joints hypermobile — common in achondroplasia at the knees, hips, and most joints, and a contributor to later motor milestones and knee instability. One notable exception: the elbows often cannot fully straighten, which is also why blood draws and IV placement can be tricky. See Childhood.

Limb lengthening (extended limb lengthening, ELL)

Elective surgery that cuts a bone and pulls the ends apart about 1 mm per day so new bone fills the gap (distraction osteogenesis, using external fixator frames or motorized internal nails). Full programs over several years can add 20–30+ cm of height — at the cost of months per segment, significant pain, and common complications; LPA's position is that it has no medical indication and should wait until the young person can genuinely take part in the decision. See Treatments and Teen Years.

Little person (LP)

A widely accepted, community-preferred term for a person with dwarfism — the "LP" in Little People of America. Alongside "person of short stature" and "person with dwarfism," it is respectful usage; most people, of course, simply prefer their name. See Wellbeing & Community.

Lordosis

An exaggerated inward curve of the lower back ("sway-back") that typically develops when a child with achondroplasia starts walking, often alongside tight hip-front muscles. It rarely needs specific treatment; marked cases benefit from gentle physical-therapy hip stretches. See Childhood.

LPA (Little People of America)

The main US support and advocacy organization for people with dwarfism and their families (lpaonline.org) — local chapters, national conferences, teen programs, a medical advisory board, and the chance to know other kids and thriving adults with dwarfism. The AAP explicitly names LPA as a resource clinicians should offer at diagnosis; equivalents exist in many countries. See Wellbeing & Community.

M–N

Macrocephaly

A genuinely large head — universal and expected in achondroplasia, and on its own not a disease. Head circumference is measured at every visit (monthly in year one) and plotted only on achondroplasia-specific charts; the alarm sign is not a big number but a head crossing upward through those percentiles. See First Years.

MAPK pathway

The signaling chain inside cartilage cells through which the stuck-on FGFR3 receptor delivers its excessive "slow down" message. CNP-based drugs (vosoritide, navepegritide) work by switching off this branch of the signal. See Treatments.

Midface hypoplasia

A smaller, flatter-appearing middle face with a depressed nasal bridge — a characteristic feature of achondroplasia that also explains several practical issues: narrow nasal passages and a crowded airway (sleep apnea), poorly draining ears, dental crowding, and a tricky anesthesia mask fit. See Childhood.

MRI (magnetic resonance imaging)

A scan using magnetic fields — no radiation — that directly shows the brainstem, spinal cord, and the fluid space around them, which is why it is the preferred test for screening the craniocervical junction in infancy (often possible without sedation via feed-and-wrap) and for evaluating adult spinal stenosis (whole-spine MRI, since narrowing is often multi-level). Compare CT, which shows bone better but not the cord. See First Years.

Multidisciplinary team (MDT)

The coordinated group of specialists guidelines recommend for lifelong achondroplasia care: genetics, pediatrics, neurosurgery, orthopedics, ENT and audiology, pulmonology/sleep medicine, PT/OT/speech, and psychosocial support — ideally coordinated through a skeletal dysplasia center. See Medical Library.

Mutation / pathogenic variant

Two names for the same thing: a change in a gene's DNA sequence that causes disease. "Pathogenic variant" is the newer, preferred laboratory term you will see on genetic test reports; in achondroplasia the pathogenic variant is FGFR3 c.1138G>A (or rarely G>C), producing the p.Gly380Arg protein change. See Understanding It.

Myelopathy

Injury or dysfunction of the spinal cord itself — in achondroplasia, the feared consequence of unrelieved compression at the craniocervical junction (high cervical myelopathy) or in a severely narrowed spinal canal. Signs include weakness, clumsiness, brisk reflexes, and sustained clonus; it is what timely screening and decompression exist to prevent. See Warning Signs.

Navepegritide (Yuviwel)

A once-weekly injectable form of CNP (Ascendis Pharma's "TransCon CNP"): the peptide rides on an inert carrier that releases it slowly, so one injection replaces seven. It received FDA accelerated approval on February 27, 2026 for children aged 2 and older with open growth plates, after its trial showed about +1.49 cm/yr growth over placebo with fewer injection-site reactions than daily vosoritide and no low-blood-pressure precautions; an EU decision is pending. See Treatments.

Neuraxial (spinal/epidural) anesthesia

Numbing medicine delivered around the spinal cord instead of general anesthesia. In achondroplasia the narrow canal makes placement technically difficult and dosing unpredictable, so guidelines advise avoiding it unless imaging shows adequate space and there are no neurologic signs; for cesarean delivery, modern reports show it can succeed in experienced hands, but every pregnant woman should have a planned anesthesiology consultation well before delivery. See Adults & Aging.

NIPT / cell-free DNA (cffDNA) testing

A prenatal test on a simple maternal blood draw: fragments of the placenta's DNA circulate in the mother's blood and can be tested for the fetal FGFR3 change, reported feasible from roughly 9–10 weeks. A prospective study found 100% sensitivity and specificity. Note this is a targeted single-gene test — different from the routine chromosome-screening NIPT most pregnant women are offered. See Understanding It.

NPR-B (natriuretic peptide receptor B)

The receptor on growth-plate cartilage cells that CNP binds to. Activating NPR-B switches off the MAPK branch of FGFR3 signaling — easing the growth brake — which is exactly what vosoritide and navepegritide are engineered to do. See Treatments.

O–P

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA)

Repeated blockage of breathing during sleep because the airway itself is narrow — from midface hypoplasia, relatively large tonsils and adenoids, low muscle tone, and (later) excess weight. It is common at every age in achondroplasia (studies in children range widely, commonly 30–70%; 59% of Norwegian adults, nearly half previously undiagnosed) and treatable: adenotonsillectomy, CPAP, and weight management. About one in five children with an abnormal sleep study has no symptoms — which is why sleep studies, not just observation, are used. See Childhood and Adults & Aging.

Orthosis (bracing)

An external support device. In achondroplasia the main one is a TLSO-type back brace (thoraco-lumbo-sacral orthosis), considered when infant thoracolumbar kyphosis persists or progresses after walking begins; foot orthotics (arch supports) help when flat feet cause pain. See Childhood.

Osteotomy

Surgically cutting and realigning a bone — used to straighten larger or stiffer leg bowing (often tibia and fibula), especially once growth has finished and guided growth is no longer an option. Realigning bowed legs earlier in life also reduces later joint stress. See Childhood.

Otitis media (with effusion)

Middle-ear infection ("acute otitis media") or persistent fluid behind the eardrum without infection ("otitis media with effusion") — both driven by the short, poorly draining eustachian tubes of achondroplasia and affecting 80–90% of young children. The fluid causes conductive hearing loss during the key years of speech learning, which is why the treat-the-ears-to-protect-the-language rule exists. See Childhood.

Paternal age effect

De novo achondroplasia mutations arise essentially exclusively on the father's copy of the gene, and the chance rises with the father's age (sperm stem cells carrying the change slowly expand over decades — "selfish spermatogonial selection"). It is a statistical observation across populations, the absolute risk remains very small at any age, and it is not something any father caused or could have prevented. See Understanding It.

Pedal extenders

Bolt-on extensions that bring a car's gas and brake pedals up to about 45 cm (18 in) closer to the driver — the main adaptation that makes driving fully achievable. A certified driver rehabilitation specialist (CDRS) can assess and prescribe adaptations, and the US NHTSA allows short-statured drivers to apply for an airbag on-off switch. See Teen Years and Everyday Life.

Pedicles

The paired bony pillars connecting each vertebra's body to its arch — the walls of the spinal canal. In achondroplasia the pedicles are short and close together, so everyone is born with a narrower-than-usual canal; add normal age-related wear and you get spinal stenosis. See Adults & Aging.

Penetrance

The likelihood that a person carrying a mutation actually shows the condition. Achondroplasia is 100% penetrant: everyone who carries the FGFR3 change has achondroplasia, with physical features that are relatively consistent from person to person (though complications vary). See Understanding It.

Pes planus (flat feet)

Feet with low or absent arches — typical in achondroplasia, along with short, broad feet and lax ankles. Practical care: wide, supportive, well-fitting shoes (width matters more than length) and custom orthotics if flat feet cause pain. See Everyday Life.

Polysomnography (sleep study)

An overnight recording of breathing, oxygen, carbon dioxide (end-tidal CO2), heart rate, and sleep stages — the gold-standard test for sleep apnea, ideally attended in a sleep lab and read by a pediatric pulmonologist or sleep specialist. Guidelines call for one in the newborn period or at latest within the first year for every infant with achondroplasia, repeated whenever symptoms appear and a few months after any airway surgery to confirm it worked. See First Years.

Preimplantation genetic testing (PGT)

Testing embryos created by IVF for a specific gene change before transfer to the womb — one of the reproductive options available to prospective parents with achondroplasia, alongside CVS, amniocentesis, and cell-free DNA testing. Surveys show affected couples use testing chiefly to detect the lethal homozygous form. See Understanding It.

Pseudoachondroplasia

Despite the similar name, a completely different condition caused by a different gene (COMP, on chromosome 19): the face and head are typical, size at birth is normal (growth slows around age 2), and the complications differ — cervical spine instability and early, severe joint disease. One more reason exact molecular diagnosis matters. See Understanding It.

R–S

Reachers (grabbers)

Long-handled pincer tools that extend reach for shelves, dropped objects, dressing, and more — one of the simplest, highest-value everyday adaptations, alongside step stools, light-switch extenders, and lever handles. An occupational therapist can advise on home and workplace setup. See Everyday Life.

Recurrence risk

The chance a condition will occur again in a future pregnancy. For achondroplasia: about 1% for two average-height parents (via gonadal mosaicism); 50% per pregnancy when one parent has achondroplasia; and when both parents have it, 25% average stature, 50% achondroplasia, 25% lethal homozygous achondroplasia. See Understanding It.

Restrictive lung disease

Reduced lung capacity because the rib cage is small — affecting under 5% of children with achondroplasia younger than 3, and worsened by living at high altitude. Watch for persistent fast breathing, poor weight gain, or respiratory fatigue; a pediatric pulmonologist should be involved early. See First Years.

Rhizomelia (rhizomelic shortening)

"Root-of-the-limb" shortening: the upper arms and thighs are shortened more than the forearms, lower legs, hands, or feet — the characteristic limb pattern of achondroplasia, visible on prenatal ultrasound and at birth. It is why sleeves and trouser legs need hemming more than bodies need resizing. See Understanding It.

SADDAN

Severe Achondroplasia with Developmental Delay and Acanthosis Nigricans — a rare FGFR3 condition (a different mutation, K650M) with severity approaching thanatophoric dysplasia but survivable. Unlike achondroplasia, it includes developmental delay and the skin finding acanthosis nigricans. See Understanding It.

Skeletal dysplasia

The medical umbrella term for genetic conditions of bone and cartilage growth — the current classification lists 436 of them. Achondroplasia is the most common skeletal dysplasia compatible with life; the precise gene and mutation determine prognosis, complications, and treatment, which is why molecular confirmation matters. See Understanding It.

Skeletal dysplasia center

A specialty clinic whose team manages bone-growth conditions every day — the recommended home base for achondroplasia care from diagnosis onward (US examples: Johns Hopkins' Greenberg Center, Nemours, CHOP; many countries have equivalents). Experienced centers coordinate the surveillance schedule, treatment decisions, insurance battles, and credible trials. See Medical Library.

Skeletal survey

A standardized full set of X-rays — skull, spine, chest, pelvis, each limb, hands, and feet — used to confirm the diagnosis when needed. Characteristic achondroplasia findings include square pelvic bones with a "champagne-glass" inlet, short vertebral pedicles that narrow going down the lumbar spine, and chevron-shaped growth plates at the knees. See Understanding It.

Snowplowing

A normal, inventive way some babies with achondroplasia get around: pushing along the floor on the head/forehead and feet. Along with log rolling, army crawling, and bottom scooting, it is a smart adaptation to a big head and short limbs — a normal variant, not a problem. See First Years.

Spinal stenosis

Narrowing of the spinal canal that squeezes the nerves — the defining medical issue of adulthood in achondroplasia, because the canal starts narrow (short pedicles) and age-related wear tightens it further, most often in the lower back. Symptoms typically begin around the 20s–30s (about 20% affected at ages 20–30, ~70% by 50+); the hallmark is neurogenic claudication, and new bladder or bowel trouble is an emergency. Weight control is the most powerful preventive lever, and surgery (laminectomy) works best done early after symptoms start. See Adults & Aging.

SSI (Supplemental Security Income)

A US benefit program for people with qualifying disabilities and limited income — the AAP schedule prompts pediatricians to discuss SSI eligibility with families of young children with achondroplasia, alongside early-intervention options. See First Years.

T–Z

Thanatophoric dysplasia

A much more severe skeletal dysplasia caused by different mutations in the same FGFR3 gene — almost always lethal before or shortly after birth due to severe chest and lung underdevelopment. Crucially for prenatal counseling, it is detectable on ultrasound by about 14 weeks, far earlier than achondroplasia (whose features are rarely visible before ~26 weeks) — early severe shortening points away from achondroplasia. See Understanding It.

Thoracolumbar kyphosis

The rounded "hump" where the mid-back meets the lower back, present in about 90–95% of infants with achondroplasia because trunk muscles are low-toned, the head is heavy, and the vertebrae are slightly wedge-shaped. The good news: it is flexible and resolves in roughly 90% of children once standing and walking begin; about 10% persist, which is when X-rays, bracing, and (rarely) surgery come in. Prevention is why the no-unsupported-sitting and no-C-position rules of infancy exist. See First Years.

Tibial torsion (internal)

A twist along the shin bone, so the leg points the foot inward — one component (with bowing and lax ligaments) of the leg-alignment picture in achondroplasia that orthopedists assess together. See genu varum and Childhood.

Tracheostomy

A surgical opening in the windpipe to bypass upper-airway obstruction — needed only rarely, for severe multilevel obstruction that other treatments (adenotonsillectomy, CPAP) cannot control. See First Years.

Transition to adult care

The planned handover from pediatric to adult health care — a known danger zone in achondroplasia, since children are usually followed closely by specialist teams and adults often are not. Guidelines recommend dedicated transition appointments in later adolescence so the young adult learns their own history, red-flag symptoms, and anesthesia precautions, and has adult providers lined up before leaving pediatrics. See Teen Years.

Trident hand

The characteristic hand posture of achondroplasia: short fingers held with a gap between the middle and ring fingers, resembling a three-pronged trident. It is a diagnostic clue at birth (occasionally even visible on ultrasound) and has little functional consequence. See Understanding It.

Tympanostomy tubes (grommets, "ear tubes")

Tiny pressure-equalizing tubes placed through the eardrum to drain and ventilate the middle ear — the standard fix for persistent fluid and recurrent infections. More than half of children with achondroplasia need them at least once (often in the first two years; about a quarter need repeat sets); the surgeon should know about the jugular bulb dehiscence risk in advance. See Childhood.

TYRA-300 (dabogratinib)

A next-generation oral FGFR3-selective inhibitor (Tyra Biosciences) aiming for infigratinib-like benefit with even less off-target activity; its Phase 2 trial (BEACH301) dosed its first child in 2025. It is years from any possible approval. See Treatments.

Ventriculomegaly (benign)

Mildly enlarged fluid chambers (ventricles) in the brain, usually alongside extra fluid over the brain's surface — a normal, expected MRI finding in achondroplasia that the AAP says should not be misinterpreted as needing a shunt. It is distinct from true hydrocephalus, which is progressive and raises pressure. See First Years.

Video laryngoscopy

Placing a breathing tube using a camera-tipped instrument, so the anesthesiologist can see the airway without bending the neck backward — recommended in achondroplasia because forced neck extension could compress the spinal cord at the narrow foramen magnum. See difficult airway.

Vosoritide (Voxzogo)

The first approved growth treatment for achondroplasia: a once-daily injection of a stabilized CNP analog (BioMarin), FDA-approved in November 2021 and expanded in October 2023 to children of all ages with open growth plates (EU: from age 4 months). Its pivotal trial showed about +1.57 cm/yr growth over placebo, sustained in multi-year extensions; the label uses weight-band dosing (younger, lighter children receive a higher per-kg dose; the pivotal trial dose was 15 µg/kg daily — dosing is your clinician's call, never a family decision from a website). Regulators' position: the effect on final adult height has not yet been established — long-term extension data are encouraging, and widely quoted projections of final-height gain are unvalidated model projections, not observed results. It does not replace any surveillance: MRI monitoring, sleep studies, and positioning rules apply exactly the same. See Treatments.

Z-score

A statistical measure of how far a value sits from the average, in standard deviations — Z = 0 is exactly average, Z = −2 is well below. You will meet it on growth charts and in trial results (for example, infigratinib's Phase 3 improved height Z-score by +0.32 versus placebo). On achondroplasia-specific charts, your child's Z-score is measured against other children with achondroplasia — the comparison that actually means something.
