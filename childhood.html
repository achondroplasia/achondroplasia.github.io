Childhood (Ages 2–12)

The riskiest window of achondroplasia is behind you. These years are about steady check-ups, a few very fixable problems — ears, sleep, legs, weight — and helping your child thrive at school and with friends.

The big picture: what childhood care looks like

If you have just arrived at this page from the first years, take a breath: the most medically delicate period of achondroplasia — infancy, when the opening at the base of the skull (the foramen magnum) is at its narrowest — is usually over by now. Childhood care is mostly surveillance and prevention, not crisis management. And the foundation never changes: children with achondroplasia have normal intelligence, attend regular school, and grow into independent adults.

What routine care looks like from here, based on the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) 2020 clinical report and the International Consensus Statement (2021):

  • A thorough medical review at least once a year (GeneReviews suggests every 6–12 months): height, weight, and head circumference plotted only on achondroplasia-specific charts; a neurologic exam including reflexes; a hearing check; questions about snoring and sleep; and a look at the spine and legs.
  • Orthopedic (bone specialist) review every 1–2 years, or sooner if problems come up.
  • The recurring themes of ages 2–12: ear infections and hearing, obstructive sleep apnea, bowed legs, a back curve that has not fully resolved, dental crowding, early weight gain, and making school physically accessible.
  • Motor differences are expected, not worrying. Later milestones reflect body proportions, a relatively large head, loose joints, and low muscle tone — never intelligence. Development should be tracked against achondroplasia-specific milestone charts, and standardized developmental tests designed for average-height children should not be used (Children's Hospital at Westmead therapy guidelines).
Tip for families

An age-by-age summary of every recommended check lives on the Care Checklist page — many families print it and bring it to well-child visits. If your pediatrician has never cared for a child with achondroplasia, bringing the AAP report (linked in the Sources below) is genuinely welcome, not rude.

Growth and growth charts

A child with achondroplasia plotted on a standard CDC or WHO chart will always sit "far below the curve" — which tells you nothing useful and can hide real problems, like poor weight gain or creeping excess weight. What growth typically looks like

  • Slow and remarkably steady. Natural-history data show a median height velocity of roughly 4–5 cm (about 2 inches) per year from age 2 to about age 10. Studies differ modestly — a 7-year multinational study found velocity drifting from about 7 cm/year at age 1 down to about 3.6 cm/year by age 10 — but all agree on the pattern: slow, steady, predictable.
  • There is no pubertal growth spurt in untreated achondroplasia — a key difference from average-height children, and one reason the height gap widens in the teen years.
  • Untreated adult height averages about 131 cm (4 ft 4 in) for men and 124 cm (4 ft 1 in) for women (GeneReviews: 129.9 ± 6.25 cm and 122.4 ± 5.9 cm; the AAP quotes an overall range of roughly 120–135 cm).
  • Growth hormone and nutritional supplements do not meaningfully increase final height in achondroplasia and are not recommended (AAP; growth-hormone therapy is approved only in Japan, and its effects appear mostly limited to the first two years of treatment).

A word about vosoritide and newer medicines

Vosoritide (Voxzogo), a daily injection, is FDA-approved to increase growth velocity in children with open growth plates (approved for ages 5 and up in 2021, expanded to all ages with open growth plates in October 2023). In trials it added on average about 1.57 cm/year of growth. Importantly, regulators note that its effect on final adult height has not yet been established — long-term extension data are encouraging, but final-height results are still accumulating. Whether to use it is a personal, family-by-family decision made with a geneticist or endocrinologist — this page is educational, not medical advice, so please take treatment questions to your own care team. The full picture, including other drugs in trials, is on the Treatments page.

Why standard BMI is misleading here

BMI is calculated from height, and it systematically overestimates body fat in short-statured people. There are no validated obesity cut-offs for achondroplasia, so the usual "85th/95th percentile" rules should not be applied. Clinicians should use the Hoover-Fong achondroplasia-specific weight-for-height and BMI curves plus clinical judgment — more on why this matters in the healthy weight section below.

Ears, hearing, and speech

Because the bones of the face and skull base grow differently, the drainage tubes of the middle ear (the eustachian tubes) are short, relatively horizontal, and drain poorly. Fluid builds up behind the eardrum, causing repeated middle-ear infections (recurrent otitis media) and temporary or lasting conductive hearing loss — sound is blocked mechanically, which is different from nerve deafness and usually fixable.

How common is this?

  • Recurrent middle-ear infection affects 80–90% of children with achondroplasia (International Consensus Statement); a classic study found about 90% had otitis media before age 2.
  • Compared with other children, a large US claims-database study (2022) found roughly a 2.2× higher risk of otitis media, a 12× higher risk of conductive hearing loss, and a 9.7× higher risk of needing ear tubes.
  • About 40% of individuals develop functionally significant hearing loss (GeneReviews), and more than half of children need ventilation tubes ("grommets" or pressure-equalizing tubes) at least once — multicenter series report roughly 47–50%, with about 23% needing a repeat set.

What should happen

  • A formal hearing test (audiometry) every year, ideally through the elementary-school years (AAP), with ENT (ear-nose-throat) follow-up whenever problems are found. The consensus statement recommends tubes when middle-ear fluid persists 3 months or more with documented hearing loss; hearing aids are an option for conductive loss that surgery does not fix.
  • Have the ears checked with any lingering cold or suspected ear pain — chronic painless fluid is easy to miss and quietly muffles a child's hearing during prime language-learning years.
  • Before any ear surgery, the surgeon must know the diagnosis. People with achondroplasia often have a high or uncovered jugular bulb (jugular bulb dehiscence) — a large neck vein that lacks its usual bony "roof" inside the middle ear — which creates a small but real risk of serious bleeding during tube placement. The consensus statement specifically recommends checking for this before myringotomy.

Speech and language

Language in achondroplasia is typically on time or only mildly delayed. On achondroplasia-specific norms from the Westmead therapy guidelines, two-word phrases appear at 20–31 months, and about 13% of toddlers are "late talkers" (fewer than 50 words and no phrases at 24 months); consensus literature estimates speech delay in roughly 20% overall. The AAP recommends a formal speech evaluation no later than age 2. If speech is delayed, the first step is always to rule out middle-ear fluid and conductive hearing loss — the most common and most fixable cause. Low facial muscle tone can also contribute. Speech therapy works well and can usually be delivered free through early-intervention or school-based services (see the school section).

Sleep and breathing: obstructive sleep apnea

The midface is smaller (midface hypoplasia), the airway is narrow, the tonsils and adenoids take up proportionally more room, and muscle tone is lower — so obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), where the airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, is common throughout childhood. Untreated, it erodes daytime learning, behavior, and heart health, and it worsens with weight gain. Treated, children often transform — better sleep, better school days.

How common: studies vary a lot by method — OSA is commonly quoted at 30–70% of children; the consensus statement cites 48–75% for sleep-disordered breathing, and a 2025 age-structured cohort found OSA in about 18% of 3–5-year-olds and 38% of 6–12-year-olds. Whatever the exact number, the practical message is the same: assume your child is at high risk and keep watching for symptoms at every age.

Symptoms worth reporting (the AAP's list)

  • Loud snoring; pauses in breathing; choking or gasping; "glottal stops" (little catch-in-the-throat sounds); chest sucking in with breaths (retractions); deep compensatory sighs
  • Restless sleep with repeated awakenings; night-time vomiting; morning headaches
  • New bed-wetting in a child who was dry (secondary enuresis) — an underappreciated OSA sign that also appears on the Warning Signs page because it can occasionally signal a spine problem
  • Daytime sleepiness, poor concentration, or behavior problems at school

One reassurance: heavy sweating during sleep, on its own, is common and normal in achondroplasia. Sweating plus obstructed breathing is what warrants a sleep study.

Testing and treatment

  • If OSA is suspected: referral to a sleep specialist and an overnight sleep study (polysomnography). Most children will have had a baseline study in infancy; repeat studies are done whenever symptoms appear or recur, and after any airway treatment to confirm it worked (the consensus statement recommends a follow-up study 2–4 months after surgery).
  • Adenotonsillectomy (removing the adenoids and tonsils) is the usual first-line operation. In published series it cut the apnea-hypopnea index on average from about 14 to about 7 events per hour, and only around 18% of children needed further therapy afterward. Overall, 25–40% of children with achondroplasia undergo adenotonsillectomy; counting all pharyngeal (throat) surgeries over a lifetime, around 45% have at least one.
  • If OSA persists after surgery: night-time positive airway pressure (CPAP or BiPAP) with regular sleep-specialist review, weight management, and — in the consensus pathway — an orthodontic option: widening the upper jaw (maxillary expansion) can increase upper-airway volume (see teeth and orthodontics). Tracheostomy is reserved for rare, very severe cases.
Surgery + untreated sleep apnea = extra care

Children with untreated OSA have higher breathing risks around anesthesia. Always tell the anesthesiologist about snoring or sleep symptoms before any operation — see the anesthesia precautions below.

Teeth and orthodontics

Because the upper jaw is smaller, dental crowding, anterior crossbite, open bite, and misalignment (malocclusion) are frequent — reported in more than half of individuals. The plan:

  • Routine dental care on the normal schedule — first dental visit by age 1, cleanings every 6 months. Nothing about achondroplasia changes cavity care.
  • An orthodontic assessment around age 5–6, ideally coordinated with the ENT and sleep team (International Consensus Statement); the AAP frames the first orthodontic evaluation around ages 6–8. Either way: earlier than many average-height children, because planning matters here.
  • Palatal (maxillary) expansion — gently widening the upper jaw — does double duty: it makes room for teeth and can enlarge the upper airway, which the consensus statement recommends considering when sleep apnea persists after adenotonsillectomy.
  • Some children later need tooth extractions for crowding; jaw surgery (Le Fort procedures) is only ever considered after bone growth is complete — a topic for the teen years and beyond.

Bones, joints, and the spine

This is the longest section because it covers the most moving parts — but notice the pattern: most of these findings are normal for achondroplasia, painless, and either resolve on their own or respond well to well-timed treatment. The job of the every-1–2-year orthopedic visit is to catch the minority that need action.

Bowed legs (genu varum)

  • Outward bowing of the legs occurs in 40–70% of children (International Consensus Statement), and more than 90% of untreated adults have at least some bowing. In childhood it is usually painless and functional; mild bowing needs no treatment, and the Westmead guidelines note that much of the early appearance of bowing reflects ligament laxity and improves as strength builds.
  • It is really a combination: bowing of the shin bone, inward twisting of the shin (internal tibial torsion), and loose ligaments making the knee wobbly.
  • Bracing does not work for genu varum in achondroplasia and is not indicated (consensus Rec 93) — a useful thing to know if it is offered.
  • See a pediatric orthopedist if: there is a "thrust" at the knee (an uncontrolled sideways lurch with each step), instability that makes walking hard, progressive or asymmetric deformity, or pain (AAP). Standing full-length leg X-rays are the standard work-up for angular deformity.
  • If surgery is needed, there are two main roads: guided growth (temporary hemiepiphysiodesis — a small "tension-band" plate placed across one side of the growth plate through a small incision, letting the leg literally grow itself straight over 1–2 years; only possible while growth plates are open) and osteotomy (cutting and realigning the bone, used for larger or stiffer deformities or after growth ends). Both should be done at centers experienced with achondroplasia, with timing tailored to the child.
  • One more knee tip: lateral (outer-side) knee pain without angular deformity should prompt an MRI to look for a discoid meniscus — an abnormally shaped cartilage pad — per the consensus statement.
  • For perspective: natural-history data suggest about 1 in 5 people (~21%) with achondroplasia has at least one lower-limb surgery in their lifetime — meaning 4 in 5 never do.

Kyphosis (round-back at the junction of the mid and low back)

  • Nearly all babies with achondroplasia have a flexible thoracolumbar kyphosis; it resolves with walking in about 90% of children, and the consensus statement notes about 30% keep some residual rounding (gibbus).
  • The follow-up rule: a kyphotic curve measuring 20–40° a year after the child starts walking needs careful follow-up by an experienced spinal team, and a kyphosis that has not resolved within a year of walking, or is progressing, should be referred to a pediatric orthopedic spine surgeon (consensus Rec 60).
  • Why it matters: a fixed, angular kyphosis can worsen future spinal stenosis — the narrowed spinal canal that becomes the dominant issue of adulthood. Management is spine X-rays if the curve persists, bracing for progressive curves, and surgery only rarely for severe fixed deformity.
  • Everyday prevention continues at school age: the AAP specifically advises supported sitting at school desks and during homework — feet on a footrest, a cushion behind the back (see the school section).

Sway-back (lumbar lordosis), loose joints, and feet

  • Lordosis: an exaggerated inward curve of the low back usually appears with walking, alongside tight hip-front muscles (hip flexion contractures). It rarely needs specific treatment (AAP), but the Westmead guidelines make an important connection: a hyperlordotic posture further narrows an already-small spinal canal, so if lordosis or hip tightness is marked, ask for a physical-therapy program of gentle hip stretches — gentle, because forceful stretching risks hip subluxation. Deep tendon reflexes should be checked yearly; brisk or asymmetric reflexes are an early clue to spinal stenosis, rare in childhood.
  • Hypermobility: most joints are loose — knees, hips, fingers, wrists — yet the elbows often cannot fully straighten. That elbow limitation is bony: do not let anyone splint, cast, or stretch it (Westmead), and never lift a child by pulling on the arms. It is also one reason blood draws and IV lines can be tricky.
  • Feet: typically short, broad, and flat (pes planus) with lax ankles. Practical care: wide, supportive shoes (width matters more than length), watch for pressure sores and nail problems from ill-fitting shoes, and use custom arch supports (orthotics) if flat feet cause pain or gait problems. Persistent foot pain or odd shoe wear deserves review at the regular orthopedic visit.

Joint pain and fatigue

The Westmead therapy guidelines flag this honestly: pain and fatigue in hypermobile joints are common — knees with walking and sport, hands with writing and fine-motor work — and both become more noticeable as school demands increase. The International Consensus Statement goes further: pain should be asked about at every check-up, at every age, using structured tools where helpful, because chronic pain is multifactorial and manageable when it is actually looked for. Practical responses: rest breaks, good seating, fine-motor accommodations at school (below), activity that builds strength without impact, and reporting persistent pain rather than assuming it is "just part of it."

Staying active — safely

The rule of thumb from every guideline: protect the neck and spine; otherwise, be as active as possible. The spinal canal is narrow and the top of the neck is vulnerable, so collisions and forces that jam down through the head and spine (axial loading) are the real dangers. But inactivity is a danger too — it feeds weight gain and weakness. The goal is redirection, not restriction.

AvoidEncourage
  • Collision and contact sports: tackle football, rugby, ice hockey, competitive martial arts and boxing
  • Trampolines (the AAP recommends against them for all children; the risk is higher here)
  • Head-first diving into water
  • Gymnastics with axial loading — vaulting, tumbling, and similar competitive gymnastics
  • Hanging upside down from playground equipment
  • Heading the ball in soccer (soccer itself, without heading, is generally fine)
  • Swimming and cycling — the most-recommended activities in every source, because they put minimal pressure on the spine
  • Walking, tennis, baseball/softball, golf, adapted skiing
  • Free play, dance, music, and the arts
  • Organized sport with peers: the Little People of America community and the Dwarf Athletic Association of America (DAAA) run sports for short-statured athletes

PE participation is essential — modify, don't exclude. The Westmead guidelines are emphatic that physical education matters for social development as much as for fitness, weight control, and skill-building. A short written note from the medical team listing the banned activities and encouraged alternatives works well, and in the US it can be written into a 504 plan (see school). The consensus statement urges families to support participation in appropriate sports and games and to encourage an active lifestyle at every age.

Healthy weight from the start — this really matters

If you take one prevention message from this page, take this one. Obesity is described in GeneReviews as a major problem in achondroplasia, and it starts early: excess weight gain often begins in early childhood and preferentially collects around the abdomen. Prevention beats treatment, and childhood is when the habits are set.

Why it matters more than in other children

  • Extra weight accelerates lumbar spinal stenosis — the narrowed-spinal-canal problem that is the most disabling complication of adult life (see Adults & Aging).
  • It worsens obstructive sleep apnea.
  • It overloads already-stressed knees, hips, and bowed legs.
  • Long-term follow-up links obesity in achondroplasia to roughly two-fold higher cardiovascular disease incidence (Saint-Laurent et al., 2019).

The counterintuitive part: calorie needs are LOWER

Resting energy expenditure in achondroplasia is lower than that of average-height people of the same age and sex (though higher per kilogram of body weight). Standard pediatric calorie recommendations and standard portion sizes are simply too much. Many parents also report their child "seems hungry all the time" — a described phenomenon in achondroplasia — which is why structured meals and planned snacks work better than grazing.

What actually works at home

  • Water instead of sugary drinks and juice — the single easiest calorie cut.
  • Limit ultra-processed and fried foods; build meals around whole grains, fruit, vegetables, and healthy fats.
  • Daily physical activity the child genuinely enjoys (see the activity list).
  • Family-wide habits, not a child-specific "diet." Everyone drinks water; everyone eats the same dinner. Singling the child out backfires.
  • Weigh at every visit and plot on the achondroplasia-specific weight-for-height and BMI curves — standard BMI categories misclassify. Ask for a dietitian referral early, at the first sign the weight-for-height percentile is climbing; some European teams start nutritional counseling as early as when the child reaches 75 cm in height.
Keep the framing kind

The goal is a strong, comfortable body that serves a long life — not a number, and never shame. Food and body-image conversations get harder in adolescence, so the easiest time to build neutral, matter-of-fact habits is now. There is more on body image and self-esteem on the Wellbeing & Community page.

Checkups, vaccines, and anesthesia

  • Immunizations: the completely normal schedule, on time. There are no achondroplasia-specific changes or contraindications (AAP; consensus Rec 33). Respiratory infections are harder on a child with a smaller chest and narrower airway, so routine influenza and COVID-19 vaccination are especially worthwhile.
  • Medication dosing should generally follow the child's weight, not age (AAP) — worth mentioning to any new clinician or pharmacist.
  • Blood draws and IV lines can be tricky because of limited elbow extension and body proportions — ask for the most experienced person available, and warn the team in advance.
Anesthesia precautions — every time, for any surgery

Any child with achondroplasia having any procedure under sedation or anesthesia — ear tubes, dental work, anything — needs these precautions, drawn from the AAP report, the perioperative best-practice guidance for skeletal dysplasias (White et al., 2017), and the International Consensus Statement:

  • The neck is the number-one concern. Uncontrolled neck movement during intubation could compress the spinal cord at the narrowed foramen magnum. The team must handle the head and neck carefully and avoid hyperextension.
  • Expect a potentially difficult airway: midface hypoplasia (poor mask fit), a relatively large tongue, small nasal passages, limited neck extension; smaller-than-age-predicted breathing tubes; video laryngoscopy or fiberoptic backup should be available.
  • Spinal or epidural anesthesia should generally be avoided unless imaging shows adequate space in the spinal canal and there are no neurologic signs (AAP).
  • Untreated sleep apnea raises breathing risks around anesthesia — report snoring and sleep symptoms beforehand; the child should be fully awake before the breathing tube comes out.
  • Choose experienced hands: whenever possible, use centers where pediatric anesthesiologists and surgeons familiar with skeletal dysplasias work — the consensus statement says surgery "should be performed by a surgical team familiar with achondroplasia".

School: readiness, accommodations, and friendships

Start from this: children with achondroplasia have normal intelligence and, in the AAP's words, "usually are included in the regular education program." School planning is about physical access and social readiness, not academic ability.

Before school starts

  • Encourage preschool attendance so your child practices socializing with peers, and work with teachers so the child is treated in an age-appropriate way — not one dictated by height (AAP). A 7-year-old the size of a 4-year-old still gets 7-year-old responsibilities and expectations.
  • Do a school walk-through with an occupational or physical therapist to spot barriers before day one: high sinks, toilets, lockers, water fountains, heavy doors, high doorknobs, blackboard reach. The Westmead team recommends a visit plus written recommendations for every school and childcare transition.

Classroom accommodations that work

This list merges the AAP recommendations with the unusually practical Westmead therapy guidelines (their school section is the most detailed published anywhere):

  • Getting around: a classroom near the toilets and on the ground floor; door handles lowered or fitted with a chain extender — the Westmead guidelines flag this as an emergency-egress issue, not a convenience; help with heavy doors.
  • Reach: bag hooks at reachable height (or a small step), in-class equipment on lower shelves, step stools at sinks, drinking fountains, and towel dispensers; lever taps instead of twist taps; a step at the canteen counter.
  • Seating — keep the child at peer height: children with achondroplasia have near-normal sitting height, so a regular chair and desk usually work if the feet are supported on a footrest and a cushion behind the back reduces the seat depth. Specialized "small" furniture is generally not required — and sitting level with classmates matters socially. Adapt outdoor and playground seating too, and offer a small chair for floor-time if hip or knee pain makes floor sitting hard.
  • Toilets: a step and/or grab rail; a floor-level urinal or step; the goal, called out explicitly by the AAP, is that the child can use the restroom independently. Some children need adult toileting help during the first school year until independence clicks — plan for it without embarrassment.
  • Energy management: extra rest breaks and extra time on long walks (excursions, sports fields); extra passing time between classes; a second set of textbooks at home so a heavy backpack never has to travel.
  • Fine motor and writing fatigue: small hands with short fingers and hypermobile joints make handwriting, cutting, and craft slower and more tiring — this is common and worth naming (Westmead). Monitor it, modify tasks, and prescribe equipment where needed: pencil grips, chunkier writing tools, reduced-copying tasks, and keyboard access as writing loads grow.
  • Clothing and PE: dress-code flexibility for clothes with easy fasteners and hemmed lengths; adaptive PE with the avoid/encourage list — participation is essential, modification is the method.

IEP vs. 504 plan (United States) — and equivalents elsewhere

  • A 504 plan (Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act) provides accommodations — step stools, footrests, restroom access, adaptive PE, extra transition time — for a child who learns typically. This is what most children with achondroplasia need.
  • An IEP (under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act) provides specialized instruction and services — school-based speech therapy, occupational therapy, or physical therapy — and applies only if the child qualifies for such services. Speech therapy for hearing-related delay is the most common reason.
  • Both are legally binding, free, reviewed at least annually, and parents are equal members of the team. Bring the AAP report to the meeting — schools respond well to written medical guidance.
  • Outside the US, most school systems have an equivalent formal process for disability accommodations; the same approach — a school visit plus written recommendations from the medical or therapy team — is exactly how the Australian Westmead clinic handles it, and it travels well.

Classmates, questions, and teasing

  • Prepare your child for the questions and curiosity of others (AAP). Rehearse short, confident answers: "I have achondroplasia — my bones grow differently, so I'm shorter. I'm 8, same as you."
  • Many families use a back-to-school letter to classmates' families or a brief class presentation — by the parents, the child, or an advocacy group. But let the child decide whether and how their difference is discussed.
  • Take teasing seriously. Schools' anti-bullying policies apply, the consensus statement flags a heightened bullying risk and calls for psychological support whenever needed, and there is a full section on handling bullying, resilience, and self-esteem on the Wellbeing & Community page.
  • Connect with the community. Little People of America offers local chapters, national conferences, and — crucially — the chance for your child to know other kids and successful adults with dwarfism. On language: "little person," "person of short stature," and "having dwarfism" are all accepted terms (and most of all, use their name); the word "midget" is considered a slur. More in the Glossary.

Growing independence at home

Every year between 2 and 12 is a chance to hand over another skill. Simple tools do most of the work: step stools in every room, light-switch extenders, reachers, lever door handles and taps, clothing with easy fasteners and tuckable loops, a toileting aid (an extended wiping wand) where short arms make independent toileting hard, and adapted tricycles and bikes for short limbs. An occupational-therapy consult can guide home setup. The Everyday Life page covers all of this in depth, room by room.

Red flags: when to get help fast

Serious complications are uncommon in this age band — but they are exactly the ones worth knowing cold. The full symptom-by-symptom guide is on the Warning Signs page.

Seek urgent evaluation if…
  • Breathing pauses, choking, or gasping during sleep → urgent sleep/ENT evaluation (severe obstructive sleep apnea).
  • New weakness, numbness, tripping, clumsiness, or a change in walking → same-week neurologic evaluation (possible spinal-cord compression — rare in childhood, but an emergency).
  • New bed-wetting in a child who was dry, or any loss of bowel or bladder control → neurologic evaluation (a possible spinal-cord or sleep-apnea sign).
  • Brisk, increasing, or asymmetric reflexes, or sustained clonus found on exam → spine imaging (early stenosis).
  • A severe or progressing back hump (kyphosis), or leg bowing with pain or a limp → pediatric orthopedics.
  • Any head injury or accident involving the neck → a low threshold for emergency assessment; the top of the neck (the craniocervical junction) is vulnerable.
  • Before ANY surgery or sedation, anywhere, for anything: tell the team about achondroplasia — the neck-handling and airway precautions are mandatory.
