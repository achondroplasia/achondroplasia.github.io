Immunizations & Respiratory Health

Standard immunization schedules apply fully to children and adults with achondroplasia — no modifications are recommended. But a smaller chest, vulnerable airways, and common sleep apnea make respiratory infections matter more here than in the general population.

The headline rule

Children and adults with achondroplasia should receive every vaccine on the standard national schedule — no vaccines are contraindicated, and none should be skipped or delayed because of the diagnosis. The International Consensus Statement (2022) is explicit: immunizations should be kept up to date. Beyond the standard schedule, a small number of additional vaccines and preventive measures are particularly recommended given the respiratory vulnerability in achondroplasia.

Standard immunization schedule — no changes needed

Both the AAP (2020) and the International Consensus Statement (2022) state that the standard childhood immunization schedule should be followed in achondroplasia without modification. This includes all routine vaccines: diphtheria/tetanus/pertussis (DTaP), poliovirus (IPV), measles-mumps-rubella (MMR), varicella, hepatitis A and B, rotavirus, meningococcal (MenACWY and MenB where recommended), and human papillomavirus (HPV) at the appropriate ages.

There is no evidence that any vaccine is less effective or more reactive in achondroplasia. The live vaccines (MMR, varicella) carry the standard mild risk of vaccine-strain illness but no additional risk in achondroplasia specifically — the FGFR3 pathway does not affect immune function.

Vaccines that are especially important in achondroplasia

The following vaccines deserve particular emphasis because respiratory illness hits harder in a child with a smaller chest, narrower airways, and often coexisting sleep apnea:

Annual influenza (flu) vaccine

Influenza is the single most important annually administered vaccine for people with achondroplasia. Here is why: influenza causes significantly more lower-respiratory-tract disease (pneumonia, bronchiolitis) in children with small chest cavities and reduced respiratory reserve. Sleep apnea, which affects 30–70% of children with achondroplasia, adds further vulnerability — a child with untreated or partially treated OSA who then catches influenza is at materially greater risk of respiratory deterioration than a child with normal airways.

Both inactivated influenza vaccine (the shot) and live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV — the nasal spray, for eligible children over 2) are appropriate in achondroplasia. The nasal spray is particularly useful in children who do not tolerate injections. Influenza vaccine is recommended annually from 6 months of age for everyone with achondroplasia.

Don't forget household contacts. Vaccinating parents, grandparents, siblings, and regular caregivers ("cocooning") reduces the chance of bringing influenza into the home. This matters most for infants under 6 months who cannot themselves be vaccinated.

RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) protection

RSV is the leading cause of bronchiolitis and infant hospitalization. In the general population it is usually a manageable winter illness; in infants with small rib cages and potential airway vulnerability, it can cause severe lower respiratory tract disease requiring hospitalization and supplemental oxygen. The landscape for RSV prevention changed significantly from 2023 onward:

  • Nirsevimab (Beyfortus): a long-acting monoclonal antibody given as a single injection before or during the first RSV season of a baby's life. Not a vaccine — the baby's immune system does not respond to it; rather, it provides passive antibody protection directly. The AAP's 2023 recommendation: nirsevimab for all infants entering their first RSV season, and for high-risk infants in their second season. This is now available in the US, EU, and Australia. For infants with achondroplasia, nirsevimab is particularly warranted given the respiratory vulnerability — discuss timing with your pediatrician in the late summer or early fall before RSV season begins.
  • Maternal RSV vaccine (Abrysvo): administered to the pregnant woman at 32–36 weeks of pregnancy, it provides passive protection to the newborn via transplacental antibody transfer. It may be offered as an alternative to or alongside nirsevimab in some settings.
  • Palivizumab (Synagis): an older monthly injection (given 5 doses over the RSV season) historically used for high-risk infants; largely superseded by nirsevimab for standard risk but still used in some circumstances. If nirsevimab is unavailable in your country, ask about palivizumab.
Timing matters — ask early

RSV season in the Northern Hemisphere typically runs October–March, and in the Southern Hemisphere approximately April–September. Nirsevimab should ideally be given just before the season begins for maximum protection. If your baby was born in late summer or autumn in the Northern Hemisphere, bring this up at the 1-month or 2-month appointment — don't wait to be offered it. In many practices, proactive family request is still needed.

Pneumococcal vaccines (PCV and PPSV)

Streptococcus pneumoniae causes pneumonia, meningitis, and ear infections — all of which hit harder in people with the small airways and ear anatomy of achondroplasia. The current conjugate vaccines (PCV13/PCV15/PCV20 depending on country and year) are included in the standard infant schedule, and that standard schedule is sufficient. However:

  • Children who have had documented recurrent pneumococcal disease should have their immunization record reviewed with a pediatrician to ensure complete coverage.
  • Adults with achondroplasia who have not been vaccinated with the higher-valency adult pneumococcal vaccines (PCV20 or PPSV23) should discuss catch-up vaccination with their primary care provider, particularly those with sleep apnea (which increases pneumonia risk).

COVID-19 vaccines

COVID-19 disproportionately affects people with existing respiratory vulnerability — small chest cavity, sleep apnea, and limited respiratory reserve all increase the risk of severe COVID-19 disease. Both the mRNA vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna) and other approved vaccines are appropriate in achondroplasia. There are no known interactions between COVID-19 vaccines and vosoritide, navepegritide, or other achondroplasia treatments. Follow the current national schedule for booster doses.

Managing respiratory infections when they happen

Despite best vaccination efforts, respiratory infections will occur. In achondroplasia, these warrant more proactive management than in average-height children:

When to escalate sooner

Lower the threshold for medical contact when an infant or child with achondroplasia has:

  • Fast or labored breathing — rib and neck retractions (sucking in), nasal flaring, grunting, or breathing rate clearly faster than usual for the child
  • Oxygen saturation below 92% (if you have a pulse oximeter at home, which many families of children with sleep apnea do) — or any desaturation reported on a home monitor
  • Poor feeding during an illness — an infant who is not feeding at all for 4–6 hours needs medical assessment
  • Unusual floppiness or lethargy beyond typical illness drowsiness
  • Any worsening of known sleep apnea signs during an illness — longer pauses, more labored breathing during sleep

Standard "watch and wait at home for 3 days" advice does not apply the same way to an infant with achondroplasia who has a respiratory illness. Call sooner, and tell the team the diagnosis — because the smaller chest and the potential sleep apnea change the risk calculus.

CPAP during illness

Adults and older children who use CPAP for sleep apnea should continue using it through respiratory illnesses — this is not the time to skip it. If nasal congestion makes mask use difficult, a humidifier, nasal saline rinse, or a brief course of decongestant (by medical advice) may help maintain seal. If CPAP truly cannot be tolerated during a severe illness, contact the sleep team or primary care for guidance on management during that period.

Antibiotics: when they are and are not appropriate

Most respiratory infections in children are viral — antibiotics do not help and can cause harm. The one exception where antibiotics are appropriate more proactively in achondroplasia: bacterial middle-ear infections (acute otitis media with purulent discharge, fever, and clear ear-infection symptoms) — given the frequency of ear disease, managing acute infections promptly reduces the risk of hearing damage. But viral upper-respiratory infections ("colds") — runny nose, mild cough, no ear pain, no fever or very low-grade fever — are treated the same as in any child: fluids, comfort measures, and watchful waiting.

High altitude considerations

A small rib cage provides less respiratory reserve, which matters at altitude. A published case series and clinical reports note that children with achondroplasia with already-compromised respiratory function are at particular risk of altitude-related respiratory complications. Practical guidance:

  • For infants, particularly those with known restrictive lung disease or sleep apnea: discuss any planned travel above approximately 8,000 feet (2,400 m) with your pulmonologist before going. A brief review of baseline oxygen saturation at altitude may be warranted.
  • Older children and adults who know their respiratory status: the same altitude precautions that apply to people with any respiratory limitation apply here. If you know you have borderline respiratory function or untreated sleep apnea, take altitude travel seriously.
  • Common achondroplasia complications (restrictive lung disease, sleep apnea) can manifest more obviously at altitude and then improve on return to lower elevation. Any symptom that appears at altitude — unusual breathlessness, worsened sleep, new oxygen desaturation — warrants prompt medical review and descent if significant.

Tobacco and air quality

Tobacco smoke and air pollution disproportionately affect people with reduced respiratory reserve — and this applies to both direct smoking and secondhand exposure. The International Consensus Statement (2022) includes smoking cessation as a specific recommendation for adults with achondroplasia, and avoiding secondhand smoke exposure for children is particularly important given the smaller lungs and higher frequency of respiratory complications. Vaping/e-cigarettes carry the same respiratory harm concern.

On air quality: in periods of high wildfire smoke, high ozone, or particulate pollution, children with achondroplasia should be kept indoors with filtered air where possible — the same advice given to children with asthma, and for the same reasons. An inexpensive HEPA air purifier in the bedroom is a reasonable investment for families in wildfire-prone regions.

Respiratory monitoring in achondroplasia

Beyond immunizations, proactive respiratory monitoring catches problems before they become crises:

CheckTimingWho
Sleep study (polysomnography) In the first year of life; repeat when symptomatic or after airway surgery All infants; all ages when symptoms arise
Pulmonology consultation When restrictive lung disease signs appear; early in pregnancy for women with achondroplasia; for infants with persistent fast breathing As indicated
Oxygen saturation monitoring Home pulse oximetry may be recommended for infants with known sleep apnea or infants at risk who cannot have immediate sleep study access As directed by care team
Spirometry (lung function) Not routine in children; used when clinically indicated; unreliable without size-adjusted references When restrictive lung disease is suspected
Consider a home pulse oximeter

Many families of children with achondroplasia, especially those with diagnosed sleep apnea, keep a clip-on pulse oximeter at home. They are inexpensive (US$20–30) and provide real-time oxygen saturation readings that are clinically useful during respiratory illnesses — allowing families to objectively track whether a child's oxygen levels are staying above 92% and make more informed decisions about when to seek care. Discuss with your pediatrician whether a home oximeter is appropriate for your child, and get guidance on what readings should prompt a call or a visit.
