Vision & Eye Health

Eye and vision problems are more common in achondroplasia than in the general population — most are easily treatable when caught early, but a few are urgent warning signs that need same-day review. This page covers all of them.

The summary

About 1 in 10–15 children with achondroplasia will be found to have strabismus (misaligned eyes). Refractive errors (needing glasses) are also more common. Neither is dangerous when detected and managed early, but both can cause permanent visual impairment (amblyopia) if missed. The surveillance schedule is simple: an ophthalmology exam by 6 months of age, and then regularly through childhood. The critical warning sign — downward-deviated eyes ("setting-sun" sign) or papilledema — belongs in the same urgent-review tier as bulging fontanelle.

Why achondroplasia affects the eyes

The skeleton of the orbit — the bony socket that holds each eye — develops partly by the same cartilage-based (endochondral) process that FGFR3 disrupts, and partly alongside the midface structures that are underdeveloped in achondroplasia. The result is an orbit that is slightly shallower and differently shaped than average, which affects how the eye muscles are aligned and how the optic nerve reaches the brain.

Additionally, the venous drainage channels at the skull base (the jugular foramina, mentioned on the First Years page in the context of benign ventriculomegaly and hydrocephalus) can slow cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) reabsorption. When CSF pressure is mildly elevated — even without true hydrocephalus — it can affect the optic disc at the back of the eye. And true hydrocephalus (affecting fewer than 5% of people with achondroplasia) has well-known ophthalmic consequences.

These mechanisms explain the four main categories of eye findings in achondroplasia, each described below.

Strabismus (misaligned eyes)

Strabismus — commonly called crossed eyes or wandering eye — means the two eyes do not point in the same direction at the same time. In achondroplasia, strabismus is estimated to occur in approximately 5–15% of children, compared with about 2–4% in the general population. Published case series report both convergent strabismus (one or both eyes turn inward — esotropia, the most common form) and divergent strabismus (exotropia, eyes turning outward).

Why it matters: amblyopia risk

When one eye is persistently misaligned, the brain receives two conflicting images and suppresses the image from the deviated eye to avoid double vision. If this suppression continues during the critical developmental window for vision (roughly birth to age 7–8), the visual pathways for the suppressed eye fail to develop fully — a condition called amblyopia (commonly called "lazy eye"). Amblyopia is the leading cause of monocular visual loss in children and it is almost entirely preventable with early treatment. This is the reason strabismus is not a cosmetic problem: untreated, it can permanently impair one eye's vision.

The good news: when strabismus and amblyopia are detected early and treated consistently, outcomes are excellent. Most children achieve good binocular vision and normal acuity.

Treatment

  • Glasses (spectacle correction): many cases of strabismus in children are driven by uncorrected refractive error — particularly long-sightedness (hyperopia), which requires extra focusing effort that pulls the eyes inward. Correcting the refractive error with glasses often straightens the eyes without any further intervention.
  • Patching and penalization: when amblyopia has developed, the treatment is forcing use of the weaker eye — either by patching the stronger eye (a fabric or adhesive patch worn for hours each day) or by using atropine eye drops in the stronger eye to blur it. This is boring but effective and must be done consistently during the sensitive developmental window.
  • Surgery: when glasses alone do not fully correct the alignment, surgery on the extraocular muscles (the muscles that move the eye) tightens or loosens selected muscles to realign the eyes. This is elective surgery under general anesthesia — invoking the full anesthesia checklist for achondroplasia, including airway precautions. Outcomes are generally very good in experienced hands.
  • Prism glasses: used in some adults and older children with residual deviation, prism lenses bend light to compensate for the misalignment.

Refractive errors

Refractive errors — the need for glasses or contact lenses to see clearly — are more prevalent in achondroplasia than in the general population. Published series report rates broadly higher than population norms. The types seen include:

  • Myopia (near-sightedness): difficulty seeing distant objects clearly. More common in children with achondroplasia than in same-age peers. Myopia typically progresses through childhood and stabilises in the late teens.
  • Hyperopia (long-sightedness, or far-sightedness): difficulty with close focus — particularly relevant as the cause of convergent strabismus (above). Infants and young children are normally slightly hyperopic; in achondroplasia this may be more pronounced.
  • Astigmatism: blurring at all distances due to irregular curvature of the cornea or lens. Common and easily corrected with appropriate glasses or contact lenses.

These conditions are straightforwardly manageable with corrective lenses — the challenge is detecting them early enough (before amblyopia develops) and ensuring glasses are actually worn consistently, which is a practical challenge in young children.

Glasses in children with achondroplasia: practical notes

Standard children's spectacle frames may sit poorly on a flatter nasal bridge — the nose is the main anchor point for most frames. Several practical solutions:

  • Frames with cable temples (the arms curve around the ear) rather than straight temples grip better without relying on nasal support.
  • Silicone nose pad adjusters and fitted-pad frames (common in East Asian-fit styles) are engineered for flatter nose bridges and often work well.
  • Strap or headband systems (such as sports straps or purpose-made infant goggle styles) hold glasses in place reliably in babies and toddlers.
  • An optician with experience fitting children with craniofacial differences — or the optician at your skeletal dysplasia center if one is available — is the best resource. Frames that won't stay on won't correct anything.
  • Contact lenses can be considered from adolescence onward, following standard guidance — there are no achondroplasia-specific contraindications.

Nystagmus

Nystagmus is an involuntary, rhythmic movement of the eyes — typically a slow drift followed by a corrective flick. It is reported occasionally in achondroplasia, most often when it co-occurs with strabismus, refractive error, or raised intracranial pressure. Isolated nystagmus is uncommon but warrants a thorough ophthalmological and neurological workup to identify its cause. Most forms of nystagmus in childhood are managed by treating the underlying cause (correcting the refractive error, treating raised pressure); some forms are idiopathic and stable. Nystagmus itself does not cause pain.

Urgent eye signs: papilledema and the setting-sun sign

Two eye findings in achondroplasia require urgent — not routine — medical review. Know them and act on them.

Tier 1 — seek same-day urgent review for these eye signs

"Setting-sun" sign: the eyes deviate downward so that a rim of white (sclera) is visible above the irises, giving the appearance of setting suns. In an infant with achondroplasia, this is a classic sign of raised intracranial pressure from hydrocephalus. It can also occur from severe cervicomedullary compression (brainstem compression at the foramen magnum). Either way, it warrants same-day urgent imaging and neurosurgical or neurology review — not a scheduled outpatient appointment.

Papilledema: swelling and elevation of the optic disc (the point where the optic nerve enters the retina at the back of the eye), caused by raised intracranial pressure. It is not visible to the naked eye but is seen on dilated fundoscopic examination by an ophthalmologist. Symptoms that suggest it may be developing include headaches (especially on waking), visual obscurations (brief grayings of vision, lasting seconds), pulsatile tinnitus, and in advanced cases visual loss. If any of these symptoms are present, a same-day ophthalmology or neurology review is warranted.

Papilledema is important for one specific reason beyond the raised pressure it signals: chronically elevated pressure — even at levels that do not cause symptoms — can gradually damage the optic nerve and cause permanent visual field loss. This is why monitoring for papilledema is part of the routine examination in any child with achondroplasia who has known or suspected hydrocephalus or cervicomedullary compression.

The ophthalmology screening schedule

The International Consensus Statement (2021) includes ophthalmology in the recommended multidisciplinary monitoring team for achondroplasia, and the AAP's 2020 health-supervision report lists an ophthalmological examination among the baseline assessments. Here is the practical schedule:

AgeWhat should happen
Birth / first weeks Red reflex check by the paediatrician or neonatologist as part of the newborn exam — same as any infant. Failure to elicit a red reflex (a white or absent reflex) warrants urgent ophthalmology referral.
By 6 months First formal ophthalmology examination — cycloplegic refraction (using dilating drops to measure refractive error accurately in an infant) and assessment of eye alignment. This timing is critical because it is the earliest point at which significant strabismus and refractive error can be treated to prevent amblyopia.
Annually through childhood Ongoing monitoring of refractive error (prescriptions change rapidly in school-age children), strabismus, and the optic disc if there is any history of raised pressure or ventriculomegaly. An experienced paediatric ophthalmologist is best placed to perform these reviews.
Any time: symptom-driven Any new squint, change in eye alignment, double vision, headaches, visual obscurations, deterioration in one eye, or parents noticing the eyes are not working together — prompt ophthalmology review, not a wait for the annual check.
Adulthood Standard adult eye care — routine optometry every 2 years or as directed, or more frequently if glasses prescription or visual changes occur. No additional achondroplasia-specific surveillance beyond standard adult recommendations, unless papilledema risk factors are present.

Vision monitoring in hydrocephalus and raised pressure

For the minority of children with achondroplasia who have true hydrocephalus or who are monitored for raised intracranial pressure, ophthalmology becomes a key co-monitoring specialty alongside neurosurgery. Specifically:

  • Regular dilated fundoscopic examination to look for papilledema, optic atrophy, or optic disc pallor (which would suggest chronic compression).
  • Visual field testing (by confrontation in young children, formal computerized perimetry from about age 7 onward) to detect peripheral field loss from chronic papilledema.
  • Optical coherence tomography (OCT) of the optic nerve head — a non-invasive retinal scan that can detect subtle disc swelling and track it over time — is increasingly used as a sensitive papilledema monitoring tool.
  • Treating the underlying cause (CSF shunting or endoscopic third ventriculostomy for true hydrocephalus, cervicomedullary decompression for raised pressure from craniocervical stenosis) is the treatment for any optic nerve damage from raised pressure — not eye surgery.

Vision in adulthood

Adults with achondroplasia who received appropriate childhood eye care generally have good visual outcomes. There are no published data to suggest any excess rate of glaucoma, macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, or other adult-onset eye conditions — standard adult screening applies.

The two adult considerations:

  • Residual strabismus: adults who had childhood strabismus may have a small residual deviation that they have adapted to. If this causes double vision, binocular vision problems, or significant cosmetic concern in adulthood, adult strabismus surgery is effective and available.
  • Myopia management: adults with high myopia (short-sightedness) are at modestly elevated risk of retinal detachment and should have regular fundoscopic examination and be instructed to seek urgent ophthalmic review for new flashing lights, floaters, or visual field loss — the same advice given to all high myopes. Refractive surgery (LASIK, PRK) is available to adults with achondroplasia by the same criteria as anyone — anesthesia precautions only become relevant if the procedure requires general anesthesia, which most do not.

What to tell the ophthalmologist

A brief written summary for any ophthalmologist who has not treated achondroplasia before:

Briefing note for the ophthalmic team

Diagnosis: achondroplasia (FGFR3 skeletal dysplasia). Key points for your examination: (1) This child/patient is at modestly elevated risk for strabismus, refractive error, and amblyopia — a thorough cycloplegic refraction and alignment assessment is appropriate. (2) The nasal bridge is flat — standard spectacle frames often do not sit securely; frame selection should account for this. (3) If there is a history of ventriculomegaly, hydrocephalus, cervicomedullary compression, or known raised intracranial pressure, please perform and document a dilated fundoscopic assessment of the optic disc at each visit and alert the neurosurgical team if papilledema is suspected. (4) Any procedure requiring general anesthesia invokes difficult-airway precautions — the anaesthetic team must be pre-briefed. (5) "Setting-sun" eye deviation in an infant is a same-day emergency — please escalate immediately.
